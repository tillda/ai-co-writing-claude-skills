# The Universal Law Formula Has Real Bite, but Kant's Absolutism on Lying Is Untenable

Kant's first formulation of the Categorical Imperative — act only on a maxim you could will as a universal law — captures a genuine moral insight. The position credits the formulation with the work it actually does (catching a class of cases like promise-breaking-for-convenience) while rejecting the absolutism Kant builds out of it (never lie even to the murderer at the door, never break a promise even to save the cargo and the crew).

The argument:

- **Promise-breaking-for-convenience.** The illustrative case for what the formula gets right. If everyone broke promises whenever it served their interests, the institution of promising would collapse, and the would-be promise-breaker could not have made the promise he is now contemplating breaking. There is something close to a contradiction in the will of an agent trying to free-ride on a practice he is undermining. This is a stronger claim than the Golden Rule: not "you wouldn't like it if others did this to you" but "you cannot coherently will the universal practice of which your action is an instance, because the practice would not exist if everyone followed your maxim". The formula identifies the parasitic-on-cooperation structure of certain wrongs.

- **Murderer at the door.** A homicidal Jill arrives asking where Jack — hiding in your attic — has gone. On Kant's view it is impermissible to lie, even to save Jack's life; silence is allowed but deception is not. The verdict is widely (and rightly) judged unacceptable, and the intuitive force against it is at least as strong as the formula's force in its core applications.

- **Cargo ship in a storm.** You have promised to deliver another's cargo. The ship will sink in the storm unless some weight is thrown overboard. On Kant's view it is impermissible to throw cargo overboard, since this both breaks the promise and intentionally destroys another's property — better that the ship sink with all hands. Same shape as the murderer case: the absolutism produces a verdict almost no one would actually live by.

- **The diagnosis: a strong default, not an algorithm.** The universalisability test reveals whether a maxim has the parasitic structure that makes lying-for-convenience and promise-breaking-for-convenience wrong as general practices. That is genuinely useful. But the test does not establish that *no* circumstance could justify lying or promise-breaking — it establishes that the maxim, as standardly described, is wrong as a general practice. In extreme circumstances the right maxim is narrower ("lie to a person who has forfeited a claim to truth by attempting murder") and does not self-undermine when universalised. Kant's mistake is not the test; it is treating the test as exceptionless and refusing to let circumstances refine the maxim. (The standard objection from indeterminacy of maxim-description bites against the formula-as-algorithm but not against the formula-as-default — when natural descriptions catch a class of wrongs reliably, the natural description carries the moral weight.)

- **Why I reject the rivals.** The strict Kantian reading takes the formula as exceptionless and inherits the murderer-at-the-door verdict; the strict consequentialist reading dispenses with the formula entirely and loses what it correctly captures about parasitic maxims. The middle position — the formula as a strong default that identifies a class of wrongs by their structure, with the absolutism abandoned — keeps the explanatory power and avoids the unacceptable verdicts. And if forced to choose between strict Kantian absolutism and strict utilitarianism (with the moderate-deontology option off the table), utilitarianism wins: its monstrous verdicts attach to stipulated counter-examples (Organ Harvesting under no-detection assumptions), while absolutism's attach to ordinary cases (the cargo-ship in a real storm, the actual murderer at one's actual door). Don't be an absolutist.

**Usage:** Additive to a Kant essay on the universal law formulation or on the ethics of lying. Engage Kant's promise-breaking derivation on its own terms first; the cargo-ship case and the murderer-at-the-door case are the test of how far the formulation can be pushed. The position's verdict is mixed: the formula has real bite, the absolutism does not. Where a prompt asks specifically whether the formula succeeds *as a test of the acceptability of practical maxims*, the answer is "as a default test, yes; as an exceptionless algorithm, no".
