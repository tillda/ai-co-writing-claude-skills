# Ryle's category mistake

**Question:** What are Ryle's grounds for claiming that Cartesian dualism commits a 'category mistake'? Are they good grounds?
**Answer:** Ryle's grounds are that the Cartesian treats "mind" as if it belonged in the same logical category as "body" — both as things, substances, fields of causal processes — when in fact mental-conduct vocabulary characterises the activities of human beings rather than naming an extra inner item; the grounds are good ones, both because the analogies (the University, the division, team-spirit) are illuminating and because the para-mechanical Cartesian framework breaks down on its own assumptions.

A category mistake, in Ryle's sense, is a particular kind of conceptual error. It happens when a concept is allocated to the wrong logical category, so that the speaker treats it as if it belonged with concepts of one kind when in fact it belongs with concepts of a different kind. Ryle's claim is that Cartesian dualism commits such a mistake about "mind". The Cartesian treats "mind" as belonging in the same logical category as "body" — both supposed to be substances, fields of causes and effects, items that can interact — when "mind" actually belongs in a different category and characterises human activities rather than naming an extra entity inside them. The dogma of the Ghost in the Machine is, on Ryle's account, the projection of this mis-allocation into a metaphysics. In my view, Ryle's grounds for the charge are persuasive, and the way he presents them — through analogies and through an internal critique of the Cartesian framework — makes the argument unusually compelling.

It is worth getting clear on what Ryle is *not* claiming. He is not denying that there are mental processes. He is not denying that there are minds in some sense of "minds". He is not arguing that "mind" should be reduced to "body" or to neural events. What he is arguing is that the Cartesian way of pairing "mind" and "body" treats them as terms of the same logical type — as if minds existed alongside bodies in something like the way trees exist alongside houses. Ryle thinks this is the source of the entire dualist puzzle.

The first ground for the charge is the set of analogies he uses to make the notion of a category mistake intelligible. The visitor to Oxford who, after being shown the colleges, libraries, and laboratories, asks "but where is the University?" has not understood the kind of thing the University is. The University is not another item collateral to the colleges and laboratories. It is the way those things are organised. The child watching a parade who asks "but when does the division come past?", after being shown battalions and squadrons, has made a similar mistake. The division is not a further unit beyond the battalions and squadrons; it is what they constitute together. The foreigner watching cricket who asks "but who exhibits team-spirit?", after seeing the bowlers and the batsmen, has made the same kind of mistake again. Team-spirit is not a further job alongside bowling and batting; it is the manner in which the jobs are performed.

These analogies illustrate the specific shape of the mistake. The Cartesian's mistake about "mind", Ryle argues, is structurally identical. Once one has been shown human beings — their bodies, their movements, their speech, their behaviour, their interactions with others — one is tempted to ask "but where is the mind?" The Cartesian replies by positing a further item, an extra entity collateral with the body, on which mental properties are predicated. Ryle's claim is that this is the same misallocation. Mental properties characterise the activities, manners, and dispositions of human beings; they do not name an extra item sitting alongside or inside the body.

The second ground is more theoretical and is the heart of Ryle's argument. Ryle traces the intellectual origin of the Cartesian misallocation. After Galileo, the mechanical model of the physical world looked complete. Mental phenomena did not fit the model, so philosophers — Descartes pre-eminently — sought a category for mind that contrasted with mechanism. The contrast they drew was misconceived. Instead of recognising that mental concepts characterise activity rather than name a thing, they assumed mental concepts must name a thing of a different kind. Mind became a non-mechanical analogue of body: not in space, not extended, not made of matter — a spectral machine instead of an ordinary one. This is what Ryle calls the *para-mechanical hypothesis*: the picture on which the differences between the physical and the mental are differences within a shared framework of things, processes, causes, and effects, rather than differences between two unlike sorts of vocabulary that should not be conjoined.

The argument's payoff is that this para-mechanical hypothesis breaks down on its own assumptions. Cartesians take for granted that we can already distinguish rational from non-rational behaviour, intelligent from stupid behaviour, virtuous from vicious behaviour, in other people. The whole motivation for distinguishing the mental from the merely mechanical depended on this assumption — otherwise there would have been nothing to "save from mechanism". But the Cartesian explanation requires that the immaterial causes of behaviour be inaccessible to anyone other than the person whose behaviour it is. So if Cartesianism were true, we would have no access to other people's mental lives, and our characterisations of others as intelligent, prudent, cowardly, kind, would be at best speculative and at worst impossible. The hypothesis the Cartesian uses to explain the mental presupposes that we already pick out the mental successfully, while making it inexplicable how we ever could.

This is the strongest part of Ryle's case. It does not just say "the Cartesian framework feels wrong"; it argues that the framework is internally broken-backed. The presupposed access to other minds and the explanatory machinery of immaterial causes pull in opposite directions. Either we have the access, in which case we do not need the immaterial-causes machinery; or we have the machinery, in which case the access is unaccountable.

Are the grounds good? In my view, yes. The analogies do real work, not just decorative work. They show that the same shape of mistake — looking for an extra item in the wrong category — is recognisable in cases that have nothing to do with the philosophy of mind. The structural diagnosis is therefore plausible. The internal critique then closes the case by showing that the Cartesian framework cannot be made consistent with its own starting points.

There are objections worth taking seriously. One is that Ryle's behaviourist-leaning reconstruction goes too far in the other direction — that in rejecting the mind as an inner item, he risks reducing mind to dispositions to behave in certain ways, which raises its own puzzles about subjective experience. This is a fair concern about Ryle's positive view. But it does not undermine his negative argument against Cartesian dualism. One can accept the diagnosis of the category mistake without endorsing every detail of Ryle's reconstruction. The diagnosis can be paired with a different positive view — for instance, one on which consciousness is genuinely non-physical *as a process* but not as a *substance*, in the way that fire is what burning fuel is doing, not a substance over and above the fuel.

A second objection is that the category-mistake diagnosis works only if we accept Ryle's view about how mental-conduct concepts function. If the dualist insists that "is in pain" really does name a property of an inner immaterial item, the dualist is just rejecting Ryle's premise. This is true but does not show that Ryle's argument fails. It shows where the disagreement is located. And once the location is identified, the Cartesian has to answer the internal critique about access to other minds, which the dualist conception cannot answer.

So Ryle's grounds are good ones. The analogies are illuminating; the historical diagnosis fits Descartes's project; the internal critique is sharp; and the alternative the diagnosis suggests is more attractive than the dualist picture it replaces. The category-mistake reading does not by itself prove physicalism, but it removes the chief reason philosophers have had for thinking that physicalism cannot be true.
