# Introduction to Philosophy (PY1020)

Single-textbook course built around *Reading Philosophy* (Guttenplan, Hornsby, Janaway, Schwenkler; Blackwell, 2021). Each module corresponds to one seminar and one or two chapters of that book.

**Essay scope:** textbook-bound. *Reading Philosophy* is the canonical reading and is sufficient on its own to answer any exam question on this course. Essays should stay within the textbook's selections and the authors named in this index's `**Sources:**` lists. Reaching beyond — to other primary texts, secondary literature, or general philosophical knowledge — is allowed only when (a) the exam prompt explicitly invites it, or (b) the user explicitly asks for it. Going beyond may earn a small bonus when handled well, but it is never necessary, and an essay that surveys outside material at the cost of the textbook authors will lose more than it gains. When in doubt, stick to the textbook.

**Topic emphasis:** ★★★ exam-heavy / ★★ recurrent / ★ peripheral. Stars reflect frequency in past papers (2019-2024) and the supplementary question bank, not philosophical importance — they are a rough guide for time allocation in revision, not for the depth of any individual essay.

## 1. Doubt & The Self

Cartesian skepticism, the method of doubt, the cogito, and Moore-style common-sense replies. Use whenever the essay touches Descartes, the *Meditations*, the existence of the self, or the problem of distinguishing dreaming from waking. Discussions of the cogito's circularity, what 'I' refers to in 'I think therefore I am', and the relation between doubt and self-knowledge belong here. The seminar combines two distinct exam themes (Doubt: First Meditation + Moore; Self: Second Meditation + Ryle/Strawson on dualism); both are routinely asked separately on the same paper.

**Topics (Reading Philosophy chs. 1-2):**
- ★★★ The method of doubt — dreaming argument, the malicious demon, what survives doubt (recurrent across years and supplementary)
- ★★★ Cartesian dualism — Strawson's case against, Ryle's 'category mistake' (2019, 2020, 2023 + supplementary)
- ★★★ The cogito — 'I am, I exist'; what 'I' refers to; whether the Second Meditation lays the First's doubts to rest (2021, 2023 + supplementary)
- ★★ Moore's proof of an external world — its success against First-Meditation scepticism (2023, 2024)
- ★★ Knowing our own minds better than the external world (2024)
- ★★ The Cartesian commitment to mind-body distinctness — when, in the *Meditations*, it actually gets argued
- ★★ Strawson's principle of individuation for minds — and whether it makes a problem for Descartes (supplementary)
- ★ Mathematical and eternal truths under doubt — '2+3=5', 'a square has four sides' (supplementary)
- ★ Hobbes's response — whether something corporeal might think (supplementary)
- ★ Arnauld's objection — that something extended might also belong to the thinker's nature (supplementary)

**Sources:**
- Reading Philosophy ch. 1 (Descartes' First Meditation)
- Reading Philosophy ch. 2 (Descartes' Second Meditation; cogito; the self)
- Moore 'Proof of an External World'
- Ryle 'Descartes' Myth' (the category mistake)
- Strawson *Individuals* (on persons and Cartesian dualism)

## 2. Tragedy

Hume on the paradox of tragedy and the conversion of the painful into the pleasurable; Feagin's contemporary reply. Use for any prompt about why we enjoy tragic art, the moral or epistemic value of tragedy, or the 'subordination of passions'.

**Topics (Reading Philosophy ch. 3):**
- ★★★ Feagin's meta-response solution — pleasure in our distress at suffering, what makes us 'the kind of people who respond negatively to villainy' (2019, 2020, 2023 + supplementary)
- ★★★ Hume's paradox of tragedy — why painful art is enjoyable, and Hume's own diagnosis (2021, 2024 + supplementary)
- ★★ The conversion of painful into pleasurable feelings — Hume's mechanism, the 'subordination of passions'
- ★★ Ethical significance of tragedy — whether the meta-response illuminates our moral character (supplementary)
- ★ Whether more suffering = more enjoyment — the strong reading of Hume (supplementary)
- ★ Hume vs. Feagin compared — whether either gives a satisfactory account on its own (supplementary)

**Sources:**
- Reading Philosophy ch. 3 (Hume *Of Tragedy*; Feagin response)

## 3. Dilemma

Moral dilemmas as genuine conflicts of obligation. Lemmon's framework, Foot's revisitation, Nussbaum's tragic question. Use for any prompt about whether moral dilemmas can be resolved, whether one is bound to act wrongly, or whether dilemmas reveal a defect in moral theory.

**Topics (Reading Philosophy ch. 4):**
- ★★★ Mill's Greatest Happiness Principle and dilemmas — whether utilitarianism can accommodate genuine moral conflict, or whether their existence refutes it (2019, 2020, 2021 + many supplementary)
- ★★ Lemmon's framework — Platonic vs. Sartrean dilemmas; what makes a dilemma genuine
- ★★ Genuine vs. apparent dilemmas — the role of overriding requirements; when conflict is not yet a dilemma
- ★★ Giving an example of a moral dilemma and analysing it — agent condemned to moral failure? (2024)
- ★★ Implications of dilemmas for moral theory — the 'reality of moral dilemmas' as a constraint on principles (supplementary)
- ★ Foot's revisitation — moral residue, whether the agent does something wrong
- ★ Nussbaum's tragic choices — costs of tragedy, why tragic choice is unavoidable (2023)

**Sources:**
- Reading Philosophy ch. 4 (Lemmon 'Moral Dilemmas'; Foot and Nussbaum in commentary)
- Mill *Utilitarianism* (the Greatest Happiness Principle, in commentary)

## 4. Friendship

Aristotle's account of *philia* in *Nicomachean Ethics* book 8 alongside Sarah Stroud on epistemic partiality in friendship. Use for prompts about whether friendship requires partiality (epistemic or moral), the relation between justice and friendship, or whether friends are obliged to bias their beliefs.

**Topics (Reading Philosophy ch. 5):**
- ★★★ Stroud on epistemic partiality — whether friends ought to be biased toward each other in belief, choosing friendship over epistemic virtue (2024)
- ★★ Tension between friendship and morality — partiality vs. impartial moral demands; whether the tension can be solved (2023)
- ★★ Aristotle's account of *philia* — the three types of friendship in NE bk. 8
- ★ Friendship and justice — relations between the two in Aristotle

**Sources:**
- Reading Philosophy ch. 5 (Aristotle NE bk. 8; Stroud on epistemic partiality)

## 5. Equality

Bernard Williams' 'The Idea of Equality' against Robert Nozick's entitlement theory of justice. Use for prompts about distributive justice, whether wealth-based distribution of medical care or legal protection is irrational, or libertarianism and its critics.

**Topics (Reading Philosophy ch. 6):**
- ★★★ Williams's *Idea of Equality* — need vs. merit, why distribution of medical/legal goods on wealth is irrational (2019, 2020, 2021, 2023)
- ★★★ Nozick's entitlement theory of justice — 'liberty upsets patterns', the Wilt Chamberlain case (2019, 2020, 2023, 2024 + supplementary)
- ★★ Williams's distinction between equality of opportunity and equality of respect — and whether they can be combined in practice (2023, supplementary)
- ★★ Distribution of medical care vs. barbering services — the Williams/Nozick exchange (2021)
- ★ Hierarchy and equality of respect — whether they are compatible given that consciousness of one's role is itself socially produced (2023)
- ★ Whether the Wilt Chamberlain example creates problems for Williams's defence of equality (supplementary)

**Sources:**
- Reading Philosophy ch. 6 (Williams *The Idea of Equality*; Nozick *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* selections)

## 6. Identity

Locke on personal identity through consciousness/memory, and Bernard Williams on 'The Self and the Future'. Use for prompts about what makes a person at one time the same as a person at another, body-swap and brain-transplant cases, and whether psychological or bodily continuity is fundamental.

**Topics (Reading Philosophy ch. 7):**
- ★★★ Locke's account — sameness of consciousness, memory criterion, the grounds for the view (2019, 2020, 2021, 2023, 2024 + supplementary)
- ★★ Williams's body-swap thought experiment — *The Self and the Future*, whether the case is best described as two persons swapping bodies (2024 — psychological continuity question; supplementary)
- ★★ The forgetfulness objection to Locke — whether his account can accommodate forgotten past actions (2019)
- ★★ Person vs. human being — Locke's distinction and what each requires (supplementary)
- ★★ The 'little finger' implication — whether all of consciousness in a finger makes the finger the person (2023)
- ★ Psychological vs. bodily continuity — which is fundamental (2024)

**Sources:**
- Reading Philosophy ch. 7 (Locke *Essay* II.xxvii; Williams *The Self and the Future*)

## 7. Freedom

Schopenhauer on the grounds of the will and Susan Wolf on real moral freedom, with P. F. Strawson's reactive-attitudes treatment of responsibility as the live alternative. Use for prompts about free will, determinism, moral responsibility, or whether genuine freedom requires the ability to do otherwise.

**Topics (Reading Philosophy ch. 8):**
- ★★★ Schopenhauer on self-consciousness and free will — why introspection cannot tell us whether we are free, the 'weathervane' deception (2019, 2020, 2021, 2023 + supplementary)
- ★★★ Strawson on reactive attitudes — freedom and resentment, the optimist/pessimist contrast, why determinism's truth would not make resentment inappropriate (2019, 2021 + many supplementary)
- ★★★ Wolf's asymmetrical freedom — 'sanity', the asymmetry in praise/blame, whether moral responsibility requires being able to do otherwise (2023, 2024)
- ★★ Compatibility of determinism with free will — whether determinism's truth bears on ascriptions of freedom (2021)
- ★ Schopenhauer's grounds of the will — the formal structure of his argument (supplementary)

**Sources:**
- Reading Philosophy ch. 8 (Schopenhauer *On the Freedom of the Will*; Wolf *Sanity and the Metaphysics of Responsibility*)
- Strawson 'Freedom and Resentment' (reactive attitudes; routinely on the exam alongside Schopenhauer)

## 8. Consciousness

Nagel's 'What is it like to be a bat?' against Churchland's reductive materialism. Use for prompts about the subjective character of experience, qualia, the explanatory gap, or whether consciousness can be physically explained.

**Topics (Reading Philosophy ch. 9):**
- ★★★ Nagel's 'What is it like to be a bat?' — subjective character of experience, why this makes the mind-body problem intractable, the overall argument against physicalism (2023, 2024)
- ★★ Churchland's reductive materialism — the 'Hornswoggle Problem' reply
- ★★ The explanatory gap — qualia and physical explanation
- ★ Whether the mind-body problem is intractable in principle, or only on Nagel's framing

**Sources:**
- Reading Philosophy ch. 9 (Nagel *What is it like to be a bat?*; Churchland reply)

## 9. Causality

Hume on causation as constant conjunction and Anscombe's case for causation as observable. Use for prompts on necessary connection, the regularity theory of causation, or whether causation is a relation we perceive or merely infer.

**Topics (Reading Philosophy ch. 10):**
- ★★★ Hume's account of causation — constant conjunction, the regularity theory, how we arrive at the idea of *cause* (2019, 2020, 2021, 2023 + supplementary)
- ★★★ Anscombe on observable singular causation — *Causality and Determination*, why singular causal claims are not implicit generalisations (2019, 2020, 2021 + supplementary)
- ★★ Whether causal claims are always general — the explicit Hume/Anscombe disagreement (2019, supplementary)
- ★★ Humean counterfactual dependence theory of causation — whether to accept it (2024)
- ★★ Necessary connection — whether we perceive it or merely infer it
- ★ Hume's two definitions of cause — what the second adds to the first, and why he thought it necessary (supplementary)

**Sources:**
- Reading Philosophy ch. 10 (Hume *Treatise* on causation; Anscombe *Causality and Determination*)

## 10. Qualities

The primary/secondary quality distinction from Locke, with Boyle's corpuscularianism in the background and Berkeley's idealist objections. Use for prompts about whether colour, sound, or taste are 'in' the object, whether the distinction collapses under Berkeley's pressure, or how qualities relate to scientific description.

**Topics (Reading Philosophy ch. 11):**
- ★★★ The primary/secondary quality distinction — Locke's argument and Berkeley's rejection (2019, 2020, 2021, 2023, 2024 + supplementary)
- ★★★ Berkeley's idealist objections — 'an idea can be like nothing but an idea'; whether primary qualities exist only in the mind (2020, 2023 + supplementary)
- ★★ Cases of differing appearance — same object appearing different to different perceivers or in different conditions; the implications Locke and Berkeley each draw (2019)
- ★★ Boyle's corpuscularianism — the scientific background to Locke's distinction; the textbook commentary route (supplementary)
- ★★ Locke's specific arguments for the distinction — and whether they hold up (2024, supplementary)
- ★ Arguments from variation — whether they support or threaten the distinction

**Sources:**
- Reading Philosophy ch. 11 (Locke *Essay* II.viii; Berkeley *Three Dialogues* selections; Boyle in commentary)
