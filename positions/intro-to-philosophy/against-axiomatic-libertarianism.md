# Against Axiomatic Libertarianism (Pro-Entitlement)

I broadly accept the entitlement conception of justice — holdings are just if they arose through just acquisition and just transfer; outcome-based "patterns" of distribution beg the question against the people whose voluntary transactions produced the holdings. The Wilt Chamberlain reasoning gets it right in spirit.

What I reject is Nozick's *axiomatic, rights-first* defense of that conception. That presentation is what makes him a weak defender of libertarianism, even though the underlying view is broadly correct.

**Usage:** Conclusion is *defeasible libertarianism* — pro-entitlement-conception, anti-Nozick-as-defender. Do not flatten the position into "Nozick is right" or "Williams is right". The considered view rejects both Williams's patterned egalitarianism and Nozick's brittle axiomatics; what survives is the entitlement conception held as a strong defeasible default rather than as an inviolable side-constraint.

The argument:

- **Political life is messy, and rigid theories handle the mess badly.** Public goods (army, courts, basic security), coordination failures (cartels), runaway concentrations (winner-takes-all markets) are not exotic edge cases — they are routine pressure points any libertarian theory must address. A theory built on inviolable side-constraints either rules absurdly on these (all taxation as theft, including the bit that funds the very security a minimal state requires) or smuggles in ad hoc exceptions that undermine the rigidity which gave the axioms their rhetorical force.

- **Rigid theories are easy targets.** When the structure has no built-in exception-handling, opponents only need one well-chosen counterexample to claim the whole framework collapses. A defeasible, consequence-aware libertarianism — one that treats "no coercive redistribution" as a *strong default* with named exceptions for genuine public goods and coordination failures — is harder to attack and closer to defensible practice. Axiomatic framing concedes this rhetorical ground for nothing.

- **Same structural failure as absolute deontology.** Any moral framework that asserts "this rule holds with no exception" loses the moment one vivid counterexample lands. The rule can be right in 99 cases out of 100 and absurd in the 100th — and absurdity in the 100th is enough to discredit the framework, *not because the rule itself is wrong*, but because the absence of wiggle room makes the rule structurally brittle. The fix is not abandoning the rule but building defeasibility into it. Same point applied to libertarianism: the entitlement conception is right; the no-exceptions axiomatic style is what kills its credibility.

- **So Nozick is the wrong vehicle for the conception he defends.** What the entitlement conception needs is an institutional, fallibilist defense — attentive to the actual track record of property-respecting orders — not a deductive proof from inviolable individual rights. Paradoxically, the axiomatic presentation hurts the cause it tries to promote: it hands the left low-hanging fruit (public goods, monopolies, externalities) and lets opponents treat the refutation of Nozick as the refutation of libertarianism as such.

- **Reply to Williams.** Williams's egalitarian intuitions about distribution-on-need (medical care, legal protection) are not vindicated by Nozick's failures. The reply to Williams is still entitlement-based — voluntary holdings produced by free transactions are presumptively just, and "rationality of distribution-on-need" begs the question against the producers whose transactions generated the holdings. But the reply should come from a defeasible libertarianism that openly handles public goods, not from Nozick's brittle axiomatic version.
