# The Open Question Argument Sinks Mill's Proof of Utility

Mill's "proof" tries to move from "each desires his own happiness" to "general happiness is good". The move depends on identifying the good with the desired (or with what increases happiness). Moore's Open Question Argument blocks every such identification.

- **The argument.** "Is what increases the world's enjoyment good?" is an open question — a coherent speaker can give either answer (an ascetic value system is consistent). "Is doing good good?" is not — no coherent value system denies it. So the questions are not synonymous, so "increases enjoyment" is not synonymous with "good". The same template defeats every proposed analytic identification of "good" with a non-evaluative property.
- **The standard replies don't work.** The "you've begged the question" reply expands "begs the question" so wide it would defeat Gettier against the justified-true-belief account of knowledge. The "perhaps you have misanalysed your own concept" reply still owes us a principled reason why the linguistic intuitions of competent speakers, after reflection, carry no prima facie weight against the proposed definition.
- **What this leaves Mill with.** Mill can retreat to a synthetic identification — happiness is what good things have in common, even though "good" doesn't *mean* "promotes happiness". But that retreat abandons the proof; it is now an empirical claim that needs independent support, and the support cannot come from the desire-to-desirability slide. The principle of utility, as Mill himself half-conceded, is "not of a kind to which proof in the ordinary sense is applicable".

**Usage:** Use whenever the prompt names Mill's proof, the proof of the principle of utility, or asks of what kind of proof utility is susceptible. Also carries weight against any modern naturalistic reduction of "good" to "satisfies desire".

**Applicable to:** Mill's proof of the principle of utility, of what kind of proof utility is susceptible, naturalistic fallacy, Open Question Argument, fact-value gap in Mill, desire and desirability, hedonism as definition of good, *ethics-historical / 5. Mill*
