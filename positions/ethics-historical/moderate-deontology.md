# Moderate Deontology over Absolute Kantianism

Kant's view that all moral requirements are categorical imperatives, with no exceptions for consequences, fails three pressure tests. The defensible position is moderate deontology — agent-centred constraints that hold by default but yield to sufficiently large stakes — pluralist (not algorithmic), and aware of its own remaining problems. Not all moral requirements are categorical in Kant's strict sense; the strongest are weighty defaults, and that is enough to sustain most of what Kant cared about while avoiding the verdicts that make absolutism untenable.

The argument:

- **The extreme-consequences test.** Suppose powerful aliens will destroy Earth, killing all eight billion people, unless one innocent person is killed. The absolutist who holds that intentionally killing the innocent is always wrong, regardless of consequences, must say: let humanity perish. Pluralise: a deadly disease will kill everyone except one immune girl whose hair could synthesise the cure; she will not consent to give one hair. The absolutist who holds that all rights are absolute must say: let humanity perish for the sake of one hair. Most people read these verdicts as reductios. The absolutist's options are to bite the bullet (the verdicts are right), to add ad hoc thresholds (which abandons absolutism), or to deny the cases (but they are stipulated). None is satisfying.

- **The portions-of-a-life test.** Killing just is shortening a life. The cases differ only in how much a life is shortened. Suppose the one innocent in the alien-threat case is ninety-four years old and terminally ill, with one month to live; he wants the month and refuses consent. May we shorten his life by one month to save eight billion? By one day? By one hour? By one tenth of a second? The absolutist who refuses any shortening must give the same verdict in every case, including the 0.1-second case. There is no qualitative line to draw, only a quantitative continuum, and on that continuum the absolutist's position becomes vanishingly hard to defend at the small end.

- **The risks-to-life test.** Every action carries some non-zero probability of killing someone. If the absolutist forbids any non-zero risk of killing the innocent, all action is forbidden — one would have to remain motionless until dead (which is itself a course of action with consequences). If the absolutist allows small risks but forbids large ones with sufficiently good consequences, the absolutism has been abandoned in favour of a threshold view. If the absolutist allows large risks with sufficiently good consequences but forbids certainties even with arbitrarily good consequences, the position becomes incoherent in the limit (a 99.99% risk is allowed, certainty is not, and the cliff between them lacks moral significance).

- **What moderate deontology commits to.** Some kinds of action — killing the innocent, framing the innocent, breaking serious promises — should not be performed even when they would produce slightly better consequences, because they violate agent-centred constraints that protect persons against being treated as raw material for utility-maximisation. But these constraints are not exceptionless. In sufficiently extreme cases (saving humanity from destruction; preventing catastrophic and otherwise unstoppable harm), a constraint may permissibly be violated. The view is pluralist: there are several distinct moral principles (do not kill, do not lie, keep promises, produce more good rather than less, treat as ends), and when they conflict there is no algorithm — moral judgment is required. Moral life is irreducibly judgmental; the demand for an algorithm is the mistake that produces both absolutism and act-utilitarianism.

- **Honest qualification: real problems remain.** Two are serious. First, *arbitrary cutoffs*: if it is wrong to kill one to save five but permissible to kill one to save a million, there is some threshold between, and no principled story about what makes that threshold the correct one. Utilitarianism avoids this (the threshold is "enough good to outweigh the cost", a principled criterion); absolutism avoids this (no threshold). Moderate deontology cannot. Second, *the aggregation problem*: two transfers that are each impermissible (steal $1 from B to give $2 to A; steal $1 from A to give $2 to B) combine to benefit both A and B — the deontologist must say each act is wrong while the combination is permissible, which is paradoxical. These are real costs.

- **The verdict.** Each of the three views — utilitarianism, absolute deontology, moderate deontology — has problems. Utilitarianism endorses Organ Harvesting and Framing under stipulation. Absolute deontology lets humanity perish to save one hair or 0.1 seconds. Moderate deontology accepts arbitrary cutoffs and an aggregation paradox. The honest comparison: moderate deontology's problems are the least bad. Endorsing arbitrary cutoffs and paradoxical aggregation in rare cases is a smaller cost than endorsing organ-harvesting under stipulation or endorsing the destruction of humanity to avoid a tiny rights-violation. And between the two extremes, utilitarianism beats absolutism: utilitarianism's monstrous verdicts attach to stipulated counter-examples, but absolutism's attach to realistic stipulations (let humanity perish for one hair) and the risks-to-life argument may even push absolutism to forbid all action whatsoever. The full ranking is: **moderate deontology > utilitarianism > absolute deontology**. Don't be an absolutist.

- **Why I reject the rivals.** Absolute deontology generates the verdicts that no one — including, in practice, no Kantian — actually accepts in the limit cases. Strict utilitarianism endorses bullet-biting on the standard counter-examples. Moderate deontology preserves the agent-centred element that makes deontology attractive while avoiding both extremes; its remaining problems are tractable in a way that the rivals' problems are not.

**Usage:** Additive to a Kant essay on hypothetical vs. categorical imperatives, on whether all moral requirements are categorical, or as the closing position in any Kant essay that has set up the absolutism / consequentialism dichotomy. Engage Kant's distinction first; introduce moderate deontology as the post-Kantian position that preserves Kant's agent-centred constraints while abandoning the absolutism that produces the murderer-at-the-door and cargo-ship verdicts. Be explicit about moderate deontology's own costs (arbitrary cutoffs, aggregation) — the position wins on net, not by being problem-free.
