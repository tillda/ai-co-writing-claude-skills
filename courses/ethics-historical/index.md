# Ethics: Historical Perspectives (PY1095)

Five-philosopher survey: Plato, Aristotle, Hume, Kant, Mill. Each module corresponds to one philosopher and the relevant primary text. Guttenplan's subject guide is the connective tissue and supplies essential and further reading. Exam structure: two questions per philosopher, candidates pick two essays in total. Questions cluster cleanly within a single philosopher's text and rarely cross modules; a 2024 cross-module prompt ("appeal to ideas from one of the philosophers to examine X") is handled by picking the single philosopher whose framework best fits and treating it as a single-philosopher prompt.

Each module's topic list is two-level: **unit of learning** (a coherent area you'd revise as a chunk) → **specification** (a particular essay-question-sized aspect of that unit, drawn from the past papers and course guide).

## 1. Plato

The *Republic*, books I, II and IV. Justice as a virtue of soul and state, the Thrasymachean and Glauconian challenges, the tripartite soul, and Plato's claim that the just life benefits the just person.

**Topics:**
- Thrasymachus's challenge
  - Thrasymachus's claim that justice is 'the advantage of the stronger', and whether he is right
  - Whether Thrasymachus's view is a political observation about rulers, a moral skepticism about the very idea of virtue, or both
- Glaucon and Adimantus's challenge
  - The significance of Gyges' ring
  - Whether justice belongs to the third class of goods (good in itself and for its consequences)
- The just city
  - Whether the *Republic* offers a convincing account of the just city-state
  - Plato's claim that justice is a kind of social harmony
- Rule and the philosopher-king
  - Whether Socrates gives a convincing account of who should rule in the city-state
- Rule of reason and universal justice
  - Whether the requirement that reason rule in the soul is consistent with the claim that all citizens can be just
- The tripartite soul
  - Whether Plato's account of justice relies on an implausible theory of the soul
  - Whether the conflict-principle is a sufficient basis for dividing the soul into three parts
- The soul/state analogy
  - The relationship between justice in the state and justice in the individual
  - Whether the analogy is a sound basis for the argument or a damaging change of subject from interpersonal morality to inner state
- Justice as virtue of the soul
  - Plato's claim that justice is a virtue
  - Plato's claim that justice is to the soul what health is to the body
- The defence of the just life
  - Whether the just person is better off than the unjust
  - Whether acting morally benefits both the individual and society
  - Whether doing wrong is worse than suffering it
  - Whether Plato's defence of justice is soundly derived from a correct account of the soul

**Sources:**
- Guttenplan guide ch. on Plato
- *Republic* bks I, II, IV (Shorey or Jowett translation; Stephanus pagination)
- Brown 'Plato's Ethics and Politics in The Republic' (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy)
- Vlastos 'Justice and Happiness in Plato's Republic'
- Sachs 'A Fallacy in Plato's Republic'
- Annas *An Introduction to Plato's Republic*

## 2. Aristotle

*Nicomachean Ethics*. *Eudaimonia* and the function argument; moral and intellectual virtues; the doctrine of the mean; *akrasia*; the contemplative life.

**Topics:**
- Eudaimonia
  - Whether Aristotle has a coherent / compelling account of flourishing (*eudaimonia*)
  - The role of virtue in Aristotle's conception of *eudaimonia*
- The function argument
  - Aristotle's account of the function of man, and whether it provides a satisfactory basis for ethics
  - Whether Aristotelian ethics can be sustained if no unified purpose for humanity can be identified
- The doctrine of the mean
  - Aristotle's claim that virtue is the mean between a pair of vices
  - Whether the doctrine of the mean is an empty abstraction or a recommendation of mediocrity
- Justice and the doctrine of the mean
  - How well justice fits the doctrine of the mean
- Moral and intellectual virtue
  - Why excellence of character and practical wisdom are interdependent
- From function to moral virtue
  - Whether Aristotle can show that excellence (in the function-argument sense) includes moral virtue
- Akrasia
  - Whether akrasia is possible
  - Aristotle's account of acting contrary to one's best practical judgment, and how it departs from Socrates' denial
- The contemplative life
  - The place of contemplation in Aristotle's conception of a happy human life
  - Whether Book X's elevation of contemplation coheres with the moral-virtue books

**Sources:**
- Guttenplan guide ch. on Aristotle
- *Nicomachean Ethics* bks I-II (function, virtue, mean), VI (intellectual virtues), VII (akrasia), X (contemplation) — Ross or Crisp translation, Bekker numbers
- Kraut 'Aristotle's Ethics' (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy)
- Hursthouse *On Virtue Ethics* (Oxford, 1999)
- Broadie *Ethics with Aristotle* (Oxford, 1991)

## 3. Hume

*Treatise of Human Nature* book III and *Enquiry Concerning the Principles of Morals*. Reason as inert, the moral sentiments, the natural / artificial distinction, the construction of justice, the sensible knave.

**Topics:**
- Reason as inert
  - Hume's claim that reason is 'inert', and whether he is right
  - Hume's claim that reason is the 'slave of the passions', and its role in his moral philosophy
- Reason and moral judgment
  - Hume's argument that moral judgments do not arise from reason (assess one argument)
  - Hume's account of the relationship between reason and passion
- Moral sentiment
  - Hume's account of the relation between virtue and pleasure
- The circle of virtuous motivation
  - Hume's claim that 'no action can be virtuous unless there be in human nature some motive to produce it distinct from the sense of its morality'
- Natural and artificial virtues
  - Whether Hume offers a convincing account of the distinction between natural and artificial virtue
  - What explanatory work the natural / artificial distinction does
- Justice as an artificial virtue
  - Whether Hume offers a convincing account of the artificial virtues
  - Why Hume describes justice as an artificial virtue, and whether what he says is defensible
- Obligation
  - Hume's account of obligation
  - Whether Hume could account satisfactorily for the kind of obligation Kant describes as categorical
- The sensible knave
  - Hume's response to the sensible knave
- Convergence in moral judgments
  - Whether Hume can account for convergence in moral judgments

**Sources:**
- Guttenplan guide ch. on Hume
- *Treatise* bk III pts I-II (esp. T 3.1.1 'Moral Distinctions Not Deriv'd from Reason')
- *Enquiry Concerning the Principles of Morals* §§I-III, IX, App. I
- Cohon 'Hume's Moral Philosophy' (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy)
- Mackie *Hume's Moral Theory*

## 4. Kant

*Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals* (also titled *Foundations of the Metaphysics of Morals*). The good will, acting from duty, the categorical imperative in its formulations, the freedom-morality reciprocity claim.

**Topics:**
- The good will
  - Kant's claim that the only thing good without qualification is a good will
  - The role this claim plays in Kant's wider moral theory
- Acting from duty
  - Kant's account of acting from duty / the motive of duty in *Groundwork*
  - Kant's concept of duty and its role in his moral theory
- Hypothetical vs. categorical imperatives
  - Whether all moral requirements are categorical imperatives
- The universal law formula
  - The most plausible formulation of the categorical imperative, and whether it works
  - Whether there is a defensible formulation of Kant's categorical imperative
  - Whether the categorical imperative succeeds as a test of the acceptability of practical maxims
- The humanity formula
  - What Kant means by 'treat humanity always as an end and never as a means only', and how much of moral conduct this principle can capture
- The ethics of lying
  - Kant's account of the ethics of lying
  - Whether appeal to the categorical imperative is enough to show lying to be morally wrong
- Freedom and morality
  - What Kant means by freedom and self-legislation as reciprocal concepts, and whether he is right
  - Whether acting morally and acting freely are one and the same
  - Whether Kant offers a defensible account of the relationship between freedom and morality

**Sources:**
- Guttenplan guide ch. on Kant
- *Groundwork* sections I-III (Akademie 4:393-463; Gregor or Wood translation)
- Korsgaard *Creating the Kingdom of Ends* (Cambridge, 1996)
- O'Neill *Constructions of Reason: Explorations of Kant's Practical Philosophy* (Cambridge, 1989)
- Wood *Kantian Ethics* (Cambridge, 2008)

## 5. Mill

*Utilitarianism* chs. I-V. The greatest happiness principle, higher and lower pleasures, the proof of utility, conscience as internal sanction, the reconciliation of justice with utility.

**Topics:**
- The doctrine of utilitarianism
  - What Mill thinks a moral requirement is
  - The main claims of Mill's utilitarianism
- Mill's conception of happiness
  - Whether Mill has a coherent conception of happiness
  - Whether Mill retains 'pleasure and the absence of pain' as the conception of happiness, or replaces it with something else
- Higher and lower pleasures
  - How Mill distinguishes higher from lower pleasures, and whether the distinction holds
  - What problem the appeal to higher and lower pleasures was meant to solve, and whether the appeal works
- Impartiality
  - Whether Mill provides a convincing case for the requirement to be 'strictly impartial as a disinterested and benevolent spectator'
  - Whether Mill satisfactorily explains what moves us to act for the sake of happiness in others
- Demandingness
  - Whether the utilitarian has a satisfactory response to the objection that 'it is exacting too much to require that people shall always act from the inducement of promoting the general interests'
- Conscience as the internal sanction
  - Mill's account of conscience as the ultimate internal sanction of all morality
- The proof of the principle of utility
  - Mill's proof of the principle of utility (explain and assess)
  - Whether Mill's 'proof' is sound, and whether it was meant to be
  - Of what kind of proof the principle of utility is susceptible, and whether it is capable of any kind of proof
- Justice and utility
  - Whether Mill provides a successful reconciliation between general utility and justice
  - Whether utilitarianism provides the best account of justice as ordinarily conceived
  - Whether Mill's utilitarianism takes adequate account of our ordinary intuitions about justice
- Act vs. rule utilitarianism
  - Whether Mill was an act- or rule-utilitarian, and which he should have been

**Sources:**
- Guttenplan guide ch. on Mill
- *Utilitarianism* chs. I-V (Crisp ed. or Hackett ed.)
- Crisp *Mill on Utilitarianism*
- West *An Introduction to Mill's Utilitarian Ethics*
- Driver *The History of Utilitarianism* (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy)
