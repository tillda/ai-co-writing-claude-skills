# Can the sceptical doubts raised in Descartes's First Meditation be successfully laid to rest in the manner proposed in the Second Meditation?

In my view the Second Meditation lays only some of the First's doubts to rest. The cogito succeeds in establishing one indubitable truth — that I exist, as a thinking thing — but it does not succeed in re-establishing the beliefs that the First Meditation demolished, namely beliefs about the external world, about other minds, and about the formal disciplines. The Second Meditation gives Descartes a foundation, but a very small one, and the strategy he uses later to expand from it depends on premises that will not survive close examination. So the doubts of the First Meditation are not successfully laid to rest in the Second.

To assess this, we should be precise about what the First Meditation has put in doubt. The senses argument has undermined ordinary perceptual beliefs. The dreaming argument has extended that doubt to all beliefs grounded in present sensory experience. The deceiving-God argument and the malicious demon have extended doubt further still, reaching the formal disciplines like arithmetic and geometry. By the close of the First Meditation, every one of Descartes' former beliefs is in doubt. The task of the Second Meditation is to find something that survives this radical doubt and to use it as a starting point for rebuilding knowledge.

The move Descartes makes is the cogito. The thought is this. Suppose a deceiver of unlimited power is deceiving me about everything. Even so, for him to deceive me, I must exist. There must be something for him to deceive. So the proposition 'I am, I exist' must be true whenever I think it. The deceiver cannot make me not exist while making me believe I exist; the very supposition of deception presupposes a deceived subject. The cogito is therefore self-verifying — its content is guaranteed by the act of entertaining it.

The cogito does succeed at what it claims. As a response to the demon, it is unanswerable. The demon can corrupt my reasoning, falsify my perceptions, undermine my arithmetic, but he cannot make me not exist as a thinker. The bare existence of the meditator survives even the most radical sceptical scenario. Descartes also extends the cogito to a slightly broader claim: I exist as a thinking thing, where 'thinking' includes doubting, willing, imagining, and sensing. This too is secure. Even if my faculty of imagination is producing illusions, the imagining is happening, and the imagining is a kind of thinking. So the existence of my present mental activity is secure.

This is genuine progress. The First Meditation's most radical doubt — that even my reasoning about transparent truths might be corrupted — does not reach the cogito, because the cogito does not depend on the reliability of any inference. It is not a deduction from premises. It is a self-verifying performance. Whatever else is in doubt, I am not.

But the cogito only gets us so far. The First Meditation has demolished a vast range of beliefs, and the cogito re-establishes only one: that I exist as a thinking thing right now. Most of what the First Meditation undermined is still in doubt at the close of the Second. Beliefs about the external world remain doubtful, since the cogito does nothing to show that any world exists beyond my mind. Beliefs about other minds remain doubtful, since the cogito only reaches my mind. Beliefs about my own past remain doubtful, since the certainty of present existence does not extend backward through memory. And the truths of arithmetic and geometry, undermined by the demon, are not directly recovered either.

Descartes is aware of this and proposes a strategy to extend the foundation. The strategy depends on the principle that whatever I perceive clearly and distinctly is true. He thinks the cogito gives him a paradigm case of clear and distinct perception, and so any belief that has the same character of clarity should also be true. From this he hopes to recover at least the formal disciplines. He then plans to argue, by clear and distinct perception, that God exists and that God is no deceiver, and so that my faculties are reliable when used properly. This would lay the First Meditation's doubts to rest by removing the demon scenario altogether.

The trouble is that this strategy is not contained in the Second Meditation alone — the proofs of God come later — and even on its own terms it is open to a famous objection. The Cartesian Circle problem says that Descartes uses clear and distinct perception to prove God's existence, then uses God's non-deception to underwrite clear and distinct perception. This is circular. If I do not yet know that my faculties are reliable, I cannot trust my clear and distinct perception of God's existence; if I do not know that God exists, I cannot trust my clear and distinct perceptions of anything else. The bridge from cogito to a wider knowledge of the world depends on a step that the First Meditation has put in doubt.

So the Second Meditation, taken alone, does not lay the First's doubts to rest in any broad sense. It lays one doubt to rest — the doubt about the meditator's own existence. It does not lay to rest the doubts about the external world, other minds, or the past. To do that, Descartes needs the later meditations and the appeal to God. And the appeal to God is itself open to the circularity worry.

We might be tempted to defend Descartes by saying that the cogito is enough on its own, because once we have a single indubitable truth we know the project of building knowledge is possible. But this confuses the modal claim with the substantive one. Showing that knowledge is possible is not the same as showing that any particular belief about the world is known. The First Meditation's doubts are not a worry that knowledge is impossible in principle; they are a worry that, given my actual situation, I do not know what I think I know. The cogito refutes the impossibility worry, but it does not address the actual doubts about hands, fires, and arithmetic.

A different defence says that the cogito alone is sufficient because, once we have it, we can recover the rest by ordinary inference. But the demon scenario specifically targets ordinary inference. To use inference to extend the cogito to other beliefs would require ruling out the demon, and the cogito does not rule out the demon. It only shows that whatever the demon does, I exist.

So the verdict is that the Second Meditation succeeds in establishing a foundation — the cogito — but does not succeed in laying the First Meditation's doubts to rest. The foundation is real but narrow. The bridge from the foundation to the world requires further argument that Descartes has not given by the close of the Second Meditation, and that, when given in the later meditations, runs into circularity. The First Meditation's doubts about the external world, other minds, and the formal disciplines remain in force.
