# The Universal Law Formula Has Real Bite, but Kant's Absolutism on Lying Is Untenable

Kant's first formulation of the Categorical Imperative — act only on a maxim you could will as a universal law — captures a genuine moral insight. The position credits the formulation with the work it actually does (catching a class of cases like promise-breaking-for-convenience) while rejecting the absolutism Kant builds out of it (never lie even to the murderer at the door, never break a promise even to save the cargo and the crew).

The argument:

- **What the formula gets right.** The promise-breaking-for-convenience case has a genuinely self-undermining structure when universalised. If everyone broke promises whenever it served their interests, the institution of promising would collapse and the would-be promise-breaker could not have made the promise that he is now contemplating breaking. There is something close to a contradiction in the will of the agent who tries to free-ride on a practice he is undermining. This is a stronger claim than the Golden Rule. It is not "you wouldn't like it if others did this to you"; it is "you cannot coherently will the universal practice of which your action is an instance, because the practice would not exist if everyone followed your maxim". The formula identifies the parasitic-on-cooperation structure of certain wrongs.

- **The absolutism overshoots.** Kant pushes the formula to absolute prohibitions: never lie, never break a promise, regardless of consequences. Two cases make the cost vivid. The murderer at the door asks where the friend hiding in the attic has gone; on Kant's view it is impermissible to lie, even to save the friend's life — silence is allowed but deception is not. The cargo ship caught in a storm carries goods belonging to another, promised for delivery; on Kant's view it is impermissible to throw any cargo overboard to save the ship, the crew, and the cargo's owner from drowning, because that would intentionally destroy another's property and break the promise. These verdicts are widely (and rightly) judged unacceptable. The intuitive force of the cases against Kant's verdicts is at least as strong as the intuitive force of the formula in its core applications.

- **The diagnosis: the formula gives a strong default, not an algorithm.** What the universalisability test reveals about a maxim is whether the maxim has the parasitic structure that makes promise-breaking-for-convenience and lying-for-convenience wrong in the standard run of cases. That is genuinely useful. But the test does not establish that *no* circumstance could justify lying or promise-breaking — it establishes that the maxim, as standardly described, is wrong as a general practice. When the circumstances are extreme (a murderer, a sinking ship), the maxim that fits the case is not "lie whenever convenient" but something narrower like "lie to a person who has forfeited a claim to truth by attempting murder", which does not generate the same self-undermining when universalised. Kant's mistake is not the test; it is taking the test as exceptionless and refusing to let circumstances refine the maxim.

- **A note on maxim-formulation.** Some objections to the formula focus on the indeterminacy of maxim-description: the same act can be described under many maxims, some of which pass the test and some of which fail. This is a real difficulty for using the formula as an algorithm but not a fatal one for using it as a default — when the formula reliably catches a class of cases under a natural description, that natural description is what carries the moral weight. Kant himself would not accept "lie to murderers" as a permissible refinement; the position takes this as a point against Kant, not against the formula's usefulness in identifying the structure of wrong-making in the standard cases.

- **Why I reject the rivals.** The strict Kantian reading takes the formula as exceptionless and inherits the murderer-at-the-door verdict; the strict consequentialist reading dispenses with the formula entirely and loses what it correctly captures about parasitic maxims. The middle position — the formula as a strong default that identifies a class of wrongs by their structure, with the absolutism abandoned — keeps the explanatory power and avoids the unacceptable verdicts. And if forced to choose between strict Kantian absolutism and strict utilitarianism (with the moderate-deontology option off the table), utilitarianism wins: its monstrous verdicts attach to stipulated counter-examples (Organ Harvesting under no-detection assumptions), while absolutism's attach to ordinary cases (the cargo-ship in a real storm, the actual murderer at one's actual door). Don't be an absolutist.

**Usage:** Additive to a Kant essay on the universal law formulation or on the ethics of lying. Engage Kant's promise-breaking derivation on its own terms first; the cargo-ship case and the murderer-at-the-door case are the test of how far the formulation can be pushed. The position's verdict is mixed: the formula has real bite, the absolutism does not. Where a prompt asks specifically whether the formula succeeds *as a test of the acceptability of practical maxims*, the answer is "as a default test, yes; as an exceptionless algorithm, no".
