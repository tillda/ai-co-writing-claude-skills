# Is Moore's proof of an external world successful against the kind of philosophical scepticism entertained by Descartes in the First Meditation?

Moore's proof is not successful as a proof against the kind of scepticism Descartes entertains in the First Meditation. The proof satisfies Moore's own three conditions for a rigorous proof, but only if we already accept that he knows the premise — and that is precisely what the Cartesian sceptic denies. So the proof begs the question against its target. In my view, however, this does not mean the underlying Moorean response is wrong. The shift it embodies — taking the absurdity of the conclusion as a reason to reject one of the premises — is a legitimate and powerful answer to Cartesian doubt, even though the proof itself fails on its own terms.

Moore's proof goes like this. He holds up his right hand and says 'Here is one hand', then his left hand and says 'and here is another'. The premise is the perceptual claim that here is a hand and here is another. The conclusion is that two human hands exist, and so external things exist. Moore writes that this is a perfectly rigorous proof. He gives three conditions a successful proof must meet. First, the conclusion must be different from the premise. Second, the premise must be known. Third, the conclusion must follow from the premise. He thinks his hand-proof meets all three.

The first and third conditions are clearly met. The conclusion that two hands exist is different from the premise that here is a hand and here is another, since the conclusion could be true even if Moore's hands did not exist (someone else's hands might exist instead). And the inference from 'here is a hand' to 'a hand exists' is straightforward. So if there is trouble, it must be in the second condition: does Moore know the premise?

This is exactly where the Cartesian sceptic objects. Recall that Descartes has supposed a malicious demon who could make me believe I have hands when I do not. If that supposition cannot be ruled out, then I do not know that I have hands. And if I do not know it, then Moore's premise fails the second condition, and the proof is not rigorous after all. Compare two arguments side by side. The Cartesian argument runs: I do not know that a deceiver is not deceiving me; if I do not know that, I do not know that I have hands; so I do not know that I have hands. Moore's argument runs: here is a hand and here is another; so external things exist. These arguments cannot both be sound. They share a domain — perceptual knowledge — and they reach incompatible verdicts.

The Cartesian sceptic will say that Moore's argument is not sound because its premise is not known. Moore will reply that he does know the premise, and that the sceptical argument is unsound at premise (1) — because he does, in fact, know that no demon is deceiving him. Each side is rejecting the premise the other is using. The disagreement is total, and the proof on its own does nothing to break the deadlock. Moore even concedes this. He admits that he cannot prove that he is not dreaming, and so cannot satisfy a sceptic who insists on such a proof. So as a proof against Cartesian scepticism, Moore's argument fails — not because the inference is bad, but because the premise begs the question.

But this is not the end of the story, and here I think the Moorean response is right. The shift is to ask which of the two arguments has more plausible premises. Three propositions are jointly inconsistent. (A) I know that I have hands. (B) To know that I have hands, I must know that no demon is deceiving me. (C) I do not know that no demon is deceiving me. The sceptic rejects (A). Moore rejects (C). Each rejection is logically possible. The question is which is more reasonable.

Plausibility comes in degrees. Some propositions strike us as more obvious than others. (A) is, on its face, about as obvious as a proposition can be. Almost everyone, on reflection, will say they know they have hands when they look at them. (B) and (C) are abstract epistemological theses. (B) says that knowledge of ordinary facts requires antecedent knowledge of the absence of all sceptical scenarios — a strong condition that is much less obvious than (A). (C) likewise depends on substantial assumptions about what counts as evidence and justification. So when we are forced to give up one of the three, the most reasonable choice is to give up the one with the lowest initial plausibility. That is not (A).

So the Moorean response says: the conclusion that I do not know I have hands is so implausible that it gives us a reason to reject one of the premises that led to it. This is a legitimate form of reasoning. If a valid argument leads from plausible premises to an absurd conclusion, we should reject the least plausible premise, not the conclusion. The sceptic's argument is valid. But its conclusion is more incredible than any of its individual premises. So the reasonable response is to reject one of those premises — typically the demand that knowledge of ordinary perceptual facts requires antecedent knowledge that no sceptical scenario obtains.

So the Moorean response succeeds where the Moorean proof fails. The proof, taken on its own, begs the question. The response, taken as a strategy of plausibility comparison, gives a real reason to prefer common-sense knowledge over scepticism. Moore himself seems to glimpse this. He admits he cannot prove the premise to a sceptic, but insists that he can know things he cannot prove. That insistence is the Moorean response in nascent form.

There is an obvious worry. If we are allowed to reject any sceptical premise on the ground that it leads to an implausible conclusion, then we have not really engaged with the sceptical reasoning. We have just refused to take it seriously. But this misreads the strategy. The Moorean is not refusing to engage. The strategy says: every valid argument can be run as a modus ponens or as a modus tollens. If the premises are individually more plausible than the negation of the conclusion, accept the conclusion. If the negation of the conclusion is more plausible than at least one premise, reject that premise. This is just basic reasoning under uncertainty. The sceptic is the one who refuses to apply it consistently.

So the verdict is mixed. Moore's proof of an external world, taken as a proof, does not work against Cartesian scepticism. The premise begs the question. But the underlying Moorean response, properly stated, is the right answer to the sceptic. The conclusion that we do not know ordinary perceptual facts is less plausible than the premises the sceptic uses to derive it. So the rational move is to reject one of those premises, and the Moorean strategy gives us principled grounds for doing so.
