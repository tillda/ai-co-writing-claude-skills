# Wittgensteinian Conceptualism

Concepts are not definitions we have stored and could in principle write down. They are **patterns of use** — dispositions to apply a word in some situations and not others, acquired through exposure to others applying it. This is the Wittgensteinian view of concepts that Huemer defends in *Understanding Knowledge* §2.6.3, and I take it to be roughly correct. The view has consequences for almost every philosophical debate in which someone demands a definition or proposes a "theory" of a concept.

Concepts are acquired by use, not by theory:

- "Green" was acquired by grouping roughly green things and calling them "green" — no colour theory required.
- "Genocide" was acquired by witnessing gross historical evil. Ask ordinary people what genocide is and they argue from cases, not from international-law definitions.
- "Is Bitcoin money?" is settled by how the concept of "money" was acquired — using tokens in certain ways — not by reading economics textbooks. Technical use is a different matter; the ordinary concept is what ordinary use creates.

Concepts can also fail or be broken:

- "Witch" referred to a Frankenstein cluster — partly women cursed by intercourse with the devil (no referent) and partly ordinary women being hated on (real referent). The concept lumped these together; trying to "analyse" witchhood was always going to fail.
- "Ether", "phlogiston", "chlorosis (green sickness)" — concepts existed in people's heads, but the referents did not. Being a concept and being instantiated come apart, and a theory of meaning that demands every meaningful term refer to something real fails at the start.

The argumentative path:

- **The Lockean picture is wrong.** Lockeanism (concepts are introspectable mental items composed of simpler concepts; understanding a word is knowing its definition) predicts that careful philosophers should produce many correct definitions over time. After sixty years of effort on "knowledge" alone — ninety-eight catalogued counterexamples and no consensus definition — they have produced none (Huemer §2.6.1). Generalising: "good", "justice", "cause", "time", "if" — same story. The Lockean view is empirically wrong about how concepts work.
- **The Wittgensteinian view explains the failure.** Concepts are *dispositional* mental states. Their content is not transparent to introspection but is revealed by activating the dispositions in particular cases (Huemer §2.6.3). Most features of a concept therefore cannot be read off by armchair reflection; they show up only when we judge cases. That is exactly why precise definitions are so hard to construct, and why pushing a theory onto an ordinary concept invariably produces weird counterexamples — the Gettier cases for JTB are the canonical example, but they are not special.
- **Quality space.** Concepts mark out fuzzy, idiosyncratic regions in a multi-dimensional "quality space" (Huemer §2.6.3). The shape of a region is determined by patterns of use, which are themselves shaped by speakers' interests, the distribution of things in the world, and historical contingency. There is no reason another set of concepts should perfectly match the contours of any given region. So most ordinary concepts are simply not definable in terms of others.
- **Linguistic intuition is the test of conceptual analyses, not the other way around.** When evaluating a proposed definition, we run it past intuition on cases: "does this *feel* like knowledge?" If our intuitions matched a stored definition, this methodology would be backwards — we would derive the verdict from the definition, not the other way (Huemer §2.6.3). The fact that intuition adjudicates is itself evidence that intuition (i.e. activated dispositions) is our access to the concept's contours, while definitions are at best post-hoc summaries.
- **The Sense / Reference distinction is on the right track but overcommitted.** Frege usefully gave the use-acquired side of meaning ("sense") breathing space alongside the bearer ("reference"). That much is right. But the binary structure overcommits: not every meaningful expression must have *exactly* two parts, and not every meaningful expression must have a non-empty reference. "Vulcan", "Santa Claus", "phlogiston" have rich use-content with no referent. Better to drop the rigid two-part structure and say: concepts have fuzzy use-content; whether anything in the world satisfies that content is a further empirical question, separable from whether the concept exists.
- **"Theories" of concepts are parasitic on usage.** When a theory of reference (causal-historical, descriptivist, cluster, Russellian) seems to fit, it fits because it happens to track the usage pattern of competent speakers. The success runs in this direction — the use was already there, and the theory just managed to describe part of it. The theory does not *generate* the usage; it *describes* it, often imperfectly. This is why competing theories all have plausible cases: each picks out a slice of the actual practice.
- **Methodological consequence.** Refusing to "define your terms" on demand is not laziness — it is fidelity to how concepts actually work. Demanding precise definitions is demanding something concepts mostly don't have. We can clarify (mention salient cases, distinguish from neighbouring concepts, note logical connections), but we should not promise necessary-and-sufficient conditions that survive every counterexample (Huemer §2.6.4).

Crucial qualification: technical terms introduced by stipulation can have precise definitions — "prime number", "monotonicity", "right triangle". The Wittgensteinian view is about ordinary, philosophically interesting concepts (knowledge, justice, cause, water, Hesperus) — not about stipulated technical vocabulary. Where a concept has been deliberately built by definition, the definition is what it is by stipulation; that is a separate case.
