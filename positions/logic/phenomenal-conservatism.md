# Phenomenal Conservatism (Justification of Deduction, )

It has to be stated clearly that this justification is ment in **epistemological** sense, with all pros and cons it carries. This is ment to be in contrast with justification ment as "derivability" in logic - that is completely different line of argumentation. Therefore, when talking about Logic, this has to be debated as a supplementary argumentation next to the canonical positions of logicians.

In Epistemological sense, this justification is, for example, dirrefrent from truth (thing can be justified but false)., defeasable or even agent-specific. I justify deductive laws via Michael Huemer's Phenomenal Conservatism (PC): *if it seems to S that P, then in the absence of defeaters, S thereby has at least some prima facie justification for believing that P*. Applied to deduction, the seeming that modus ponens preserves truth, that LNC holds, that a given proof is valid, *is itself* the foundational warrant. We do not justify deductive laws by inference at all — the intellectual seeming is the terminus, not a stage in an argument.

Defesibility is obvious from one historical example: The naive comprehension axiom (or unrestricted comprehension) is a foundational principle of early, intuitive set theory stating that for any property \(\phi(x)\), there exists a set containing exactly the elements that satisfy that property. This is now considered false, but it SEEMed "obvious" when set theory was in development and therefore NCA had prima-facie justification (in epistemological sense).
The argumentative path:

Given the "additional" nature of this epistemological arugment in Logic per se, this move should be restricted to about 1/4 of answering question about justification of logical laws.

Argumentative outline is:

- **Intellectual seemings are foundational justifiers.** PC treats rational seemings on a par with perceptual ones. When you entertain modus ponens or the law of non-contradiction, the principle *seems* compelling on reflection. That seeming, by PC, is itself prima facie sufficient for justified belief — no further derivation required.
- **Carroll's regress dissolves.** Lewis Carroll's tortoise shows you cannot justify MP by adding it as a premise — you would need a further rule to license that move, ad infinitum. Susan Haack sharpens this into a dilemma: any putative justification of deduction is either circular (using deduction) or impotent (using induction). PC denies that the demand for such a justification is well-formed in the first place. The seeming itself is foundational, so the regress never starts.
