# List of symbols

## Chapter 1

$A, B, C$ etc. are used as variables ranging over English sentences or propositions. (p. 23)

$A_1, \ldots, A_n; C$ gives the general form of an argument (in English) with $n$ premises, $A_1, \ldots, A_n$, and conclusion $C$. (p. 23)

$\models$ called “double turnstile”: “$A_1, \ldots, A_n \models C$” abbreviates: “$A_1, \ldots, A_n; C$” is valid. As a special case, “$(A_1, \ldots, A_n) \models$” abbreviates: “$(A_1, \ldots, A_n)$” is inconsistent. As a further special case, “$\models A$” abbreviates: “it is logically impossible for $A$ to be false”. (p. 24)

$\not\models$ “$A_1 \ldots A_n \not\models C$” abbreviates: “$A_1, \ldots, A_n; C$” is not valid. (p. 24)

$F, G, H$ etc. are used in chapter 1 to mark places which can be occupied by predicates, and in chapter 4 as predicate-letters (more or less the same role). (p. 39)

$\alpha, \beta, \gamma$ etc. are used in chapter 1 to mark places which can be occupied by names, and in chapter 4 as name-letters (more or less the same role). (p. 39)

## Chapter 2

$\mathbf{P}$ the propositional language. (p. 54)

$p, q, r, \ldots$ sentence-letters. (p. 54)

$\neg, \&amp;, \lor, \rightarrow, \leftrightarrow$ symbols of $\mathbf{P}$. (pp. 54–5)

$X, Y, Z$ etc. are used as variables ranging over $\mathbf{P}$-sentences (later, over sentences in whatever artificial language is under discussion). (pp. 55, 58)

$\models_{\mathbf{P}}$ abbreviates “$\mathbf{P}$-valid”. (p. 58)

# Chapter 4

## Q

the quantificational language (sometimes called the language of first order (predicate) logic, or the language of quantification theory). (p. 153)

$\exists, \forall$

quantifiers of $\mathbf{Q}$. (p. 154)

$x, y, z, x'$ etc. variables of $\mathbf{Q}$. (p. 154)

&lt;. .&gt; denotes a sequence. The names of its members fill the dots, distinct names separated by commas. (pp. 157, 400)

$\vdash_{\mathbf{Q}}$

abbreviates “Q-valid” (pp. 160, 182).

$\alpha = \beta, \alpha \neq \beta$

abbreviate, respectively, “$= \alpha\beta$” and “$\neg = \alpha\beta$”. (p. 182)

iota-operator used by Russell to formalize “the” as a quantifier. (p. 194)

T, W

unary quantifiers supposed to correspond to “most” and “few”. (p. 225)

$\mathbf{Q}+$

$\mathbf{Q}$ augmented by “T” and “W”. (p. 225)

$\mu, \phi$

binary quantifiers corresponding to “most” and “few”. (p. 227)

QB

$\mathbf{Q}$ augmented by “$\mu$” and “$\phi$”. (p. 227)

$\lambda$

binary quantifier corresponding to “every”. (p. 229)

$|\sigma|$

the cardinality of the set $\sigma$. (p. 230)

A, E

substitutional universal and existential quantifiers. (p. 231)

QS

$\mathbf{Q}$ augmented by names and “A” and “E”. (p. 232)

$\triangle, \nabla$

unary universal and existential predicate quantifiers. (p. 236)

$f, g \ldots$

variables for “$\triangle$” and “$\nabla$”. (p. 236)

$\supseteq$

inclusion: $\Gamma \supseteq \Gamma'$ iff every member of $\Gamma$ is a member of $\Gamma'$. (p. 230)

QF

a language with the syntax of $\mathbf{Q}$ but with interpretation rules not requiring every interpretation to assign every name-letter an object. (p. 241)

**QN** Q augmented by “☐” and “◇”. (p. 257)

**PNS** PN augmented by “☐→”. (p. 272)

δσ expression denoting 1 or 0, depending as σ is true or false. (p. 296)

W Predicate constant of QW assigned, on every interpretation, the set of all possible worlds. (p. 304)

**QW** Q augmented by “W”, and, later, by “at”, and, later, by “w*”. (p. 304)

at ν such operators, for some variable ν, form open sentences from closed sentences. “At ν, p” says, roughly, that p is true at world ν. (p. 308)

w* name in QW of the actual world. (p. 310)

☐ corresponds to “actually”. (p. 311)

**QC** QW augmented by “C”. (p. 319)

C Cxy corresponds, in QC, to “x is a counterpart of y”. (p. 319)
