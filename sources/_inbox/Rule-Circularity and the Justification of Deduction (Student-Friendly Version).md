I. The Skeptical Challenge

Paul Boghossian recently tried to make us doubt whether basic logical rules can really be justified. He noted that we usually justify logical rules by using those exact same rules—a circularity that most philosophers think is an inevitable and perfectly fine feature of fundamental logic. Boghossian, however, argued that this circularity is actually dangerous. He did this by pointing to a completely broken, made-up logical rule called "tonk," trying to show that if we accept circular justifications for good logic, we must accept them for bad logic too. I am going to show you exactly why Boghossian's analogy falls apart upon closer proof-theoretic inspection.

II. The "Tonk" Argument

To make his case, Boghossian presents an argument (originally from Crispin Wright) that tries to prove tonk's rules are truth-preserving. Let's break down the logic using simple symbols: T() for "is true," \rightarrow for "if... then," and t for tonk.
Here is the core machinery of Boghossian's Tonk Argument:

• It relies heavily on an assumed "Meaning Postulate," which claims that the compound statement T(P~t~Q) is completely equivalent to T(P)~t~T(Q).

• It assumes that we can seamlessly jump back and forth between the sentence "P" and the sentence "'P' is true" using what logicians call the T-Scheme.

• It ultimately tries to reach a conclusion justifying tonk, specifically the formula P \rightarrow (P~t~Q), by using the very rule of tonk-introduction itself inside the proof.
III. The Secret of Meaningful Logic: Harmony
Before we can dismantle tonk, we need to understand how real logical connectives (like "and" or "if/then") actually work. Any logical word gets its specific meaning from two complementary rules:

• Introduction Rules: These dictate the conditions required to create a logical statement. Think of this as the speaker's obligation—the work you must put in before you are allowed to assert the conclusion.

• Elimination Rules: These dictate what you are allowed to extract from a logical statement once it's made. Think of this as the listener's entitlement—the information they can safely take to the bank.

These two rules must be in perfect balance, a concept we call "harmony". A listener shouldn't be allowed to extract more information out of a statement than the speaker was required to put into it. This balance isn't just a nice feature; it is the absolute requirement for a logical word to have any meaning at all. When rules are harmonious, proofs can be streamlined into a "normal form" where they directly dismantle premises and reassemble them without any pointless, roundabout detours.

IV. How Truth Connects to Harmony

When introduction and elimination rules are harmonious, they naturally play nicely with the concept of truth. In my own work on inferential truth-theory, I show that we don't just have to assume truth works; we can swap out basic logical rules with "truth-predicational" versions to prove it.

For a connective to be meaningful, it must obey two basic rules regarding truth:

• Distribution of Truth: If you know T(A \wedge B), you can safely extract T(A) \wedge T(B).

• Compounding of Truth: If you separately have T(A) and T(B), you can combine them into T(A \wedge B).

Because standard connectives have harmony, we can mathematically derive that they obey Compounding and Distribution. We don't just have to stipulate it as an unproven axiom.

V. Tonk's Spectacular Failure

Now let's look at Arthur Prior's mischievous "tonk." Prior played a trick by taking the introduction rule of "or" and permanently gluing it to the elimination rule of "and".

• Tonk-Introduction: If you have A, you can create A~t~B. (This means tonk acts like the phrase "regardless whether").

• Tonk-Elimination: If you have A~t~B, you can extract B. (This means tonk acts like the phrase "even if").

These rules are completely out of harmony. If I put A into the logical bank, tonk-elimination lets you withdraw B—a completely unearned, unrelated statement! Because these rules clash so horribly, the symbol t simply fails to have any semantic value or sense whatsoever. It is entirely meaningless. When you use it, you commit a fatal fallacy of equivocation, shifting meanings midway through your thought process.

VI. Destroying the Tonk Argument

Because tonk has no meaning, Boghossian's entire Tonk Argument collapses.

• The Fake Axiom: Boghossian's argument relies on a "Meaning Postulate" for tonk. But since tonk is meaningless, this postulate is either a false claim or totally devoid of content. You cannot base an argument on an axiom about the meaning of a word that fundamentally lacks meaning.

• The Truth Failure: I have demonstrated that if you try to build a direct, normal-form proof of how truth distributes or compounds over tonk, it mathematically fails. The connective is so broken that you literally cannot prove T(P~t~Q) works as Boghossian needs it to.

• The T-Scheme Failure: The Tonk Argument assumes you can safely use Semantic Ascent (moving from P~t~Q to T(P~t~Q)). But my proofs show that even these basic truth jumps fail when tonk is the dominant operator. Therefore, the crucial step from line 5 to line 6 in Boghossian's argument is simply invalid.

Furthermore, Boghossian tries to save tonk by claiming it has a "conceptual role" even if it lacks meaning. But in any rational system meant to transmit truth, you cannot have a conceptual role without meaning. Tonk allows a user to prove absolutely any proposition from zero assumptions, which completely trivializes logic. No rational thinker could actually hold this concept in their cognitive system.

VII. Conclusion

The supposed problem of rule-circularity isn't worsened by using multiple rules of a connective in our reasoning. In fact, it is exactly because an elimination rule perfectly balances an introduction rule that we know our logical symbols are functioning correctly and meaningfully.
Ironically, later in his own paper, Boghossian correctly admits that tonk has no consistent meaning because it cannot be assigned truth values consistently. He just didn't realize that this admission completely destroys the "Meaning Postulate" he accepted to build his skeptical Tonk Argument in the first place. Ultimately, the introduction and elimination rules for well-behaved connectives actually constitute their meaning; they are none the poorer for having circular justifications, and they don't leave room for Boghossian's skepticism.
