# Arnauld's extended-thinker objection

**Question:** 'Although I clearly and distinctly know my nature to be something that thinks, may I not perhaps be wrong in thinking that nothing else belongs to my nature apart from the fact that I am a thinking thing? Perhaps the fact that I am an extended thing may also belong to my nature' (Arnauld). Does Descartes have a satisfactory response to Arnauld's objection?

**Answer:** No: Descartes responds by appealing to the *completeness* of his clear and distinct idea of himself as a thinking thing, but the appeal begs exactly what Arnauld is questioning, and Arnauld's structural worry — that perceiving one essential property does not preclude unperceived others — is left unanswered.

Arnauld's objection is the most precisely targeted of the early replies to the *Meditations*, and in my view Descartes does not give a satisfactory answer to it. The objection homes in on a step in Descartes's reasoning that Hobbes also queried but from a different angle: the move from "I clearly and distinctly perceive myself to be a thinking thing" to "thinking exhausts my nature, and extension forms no part of it". Arnauld accepts that the meditator clearly and distinctly perceives himself as a thinking thing. He just denies that this licenses the further conclusion. Perhaps extension is also part of the meditator's nature, even if the doubt prevents the meditator from seeing it.

To make the structure of the worry visible, consider Arnauld's geometrical analogy, which his original objection draws on. A person can clearly and distinctly know that a right-angled triangle has the property that its angles sum to two right angles. From this it would be foolish to conclude that having angles that sum to two right angles is the *only* essential property of a right-angled triangle, and that, say, satisfying the Pythagorean relation between the squares of its sides is not. The first property is more obvious than the second, and a thinker could grasp the first without grasping the second; but both belong to the nature of a right-angled triangle. What this shows is a general principle: clear and distinct perception of one essential property is not the same as clear and distinct perception that the property is the *only* essential one. Arnauld is applying this principle to the meditator's self-conception.

So when the meditator finds that he is a thinking thing, and finds further that under the doubt no bodily content forms part of his self-conception, what he has is a clear and distinct grasp of the property of thinking. He does not have a clear and distinct perception that thinking is the only essential property. To get the latter he would need an additional argument that his self-conception is *complete* — that nothing essential to him is being left out by the doubt. The doubt by itself cannot supply this, since the doubt is precisely a state in which corporeal claims are bracketed.

Descartes's published reply to Arnauld is to insist that his idea of himself is complete in exactly this sense. He argues that he has a clear and distinct conception of himself as a thinking, non-extended thing, and a clear and distinct conception of body as an extended, non-thinking thing, and that two ideas that are clearly and distinctly perceived as separable can correspond to two really distinct substances, by the power of God. The completeness claim is doing the work: Descartes asserts that his idea of himself, as it stands at the end of the Second Meditation, is complete enough to license the metaphysical conclusion.

This reply is not satisfactory. There are two main reasons.

The first is that the claim of completeness is not earned by anything in the *Meditations*. The Second Meditation produces the meditator's self-conception under the doubt; it does not show that this self-conception is exhaustive. To show exhaustiveness, Descartes would need to demonstrate that any essential property the meditator has must show up in the self-conception. But the doubt, by design, suppresses bodily content. So whatever bodily essence the meditator might have is precisely what the method has hidden from view. Treating the absence of bodily content from the self-conception as evidence that the meditator has no bodily essence reverses the direction of the inference. Absence under the doubt is what we should expect of any property that depends on the body, regardless of whether the property is essential to the meditator. The doubt is not a lie-detector for essential properties; it is a filter that removes a particular class of properties whether or not they are essential.

The second reason is that the completeness claim relies on the criterion of clear and distinct perception in a way that, by the structure of the *Meditations*, it cannot. The reliability of clear and distinct perception is established (or attempted) only in the Third Meditation, and even granted reliability, the criterion needs to be applicable to the case at hand — to the meditator's self-conception — without circularity. Arnauld's worry is exactly that the criterion does not deliver completeness in this case. The meditator has a clear and distinct idea of himself *as having* the property of thinking; he does not, just by having that idea, have a clear and distinct idea of himself *as not having* the property of extension. The former is a positive grasp of one property; the latter is a much stronger claim that requires a survey of all possible properties and a verification that extension is not among them. The clear-and-distinct-perception criterion, even on its strongest reading, does not vouch for the second.

There is a related point that puts pressure on Descartes from a different angle. If the meditator's self-conception is the result of the doubt, and the doubt suppresses bodily content, then the conception of himself as non-extended is a feature of the conception under the doubt, not of his actual nature. To take the conception as showing the actual nature is to confuse a fact about the structure of self-presentation with a fact about the structure of self-being. This is a generalisation of Hobbes's worry: even if the meditator presents himself to himself as a non-extended thinker, the thing that is doing the presenting could itself be extended. The doubt is incapable of distinguishing these two scenarios.

A defender of Descartes might respond that Arnauld's objection, if generalised, makes any clear-and-distinct judgement about essential properties hostage to a completeness condition that no inquirer could ever meet, and so paralyses metaphysical inquiry. There is something to this rhetoric but it does not vindicate Descartes. Arnauld is not requiring perfect knowledge of all essential properties. He is requiring a justification for the claim that thinking is the *only* essential property when the method by which the meditator arrived at this conclusion was bound to omit bodily content. This is a local demand, not a global one. The Cartesian could meet it by giving an independent argument — say, by giving an argument that thinking *cannot* coexist with extension in a single substance, or that the connection between mental properties and bodily properties cannot be that of two essential properties of the same thing. But Descartes does not give such an argument; he simply asserts completeness.

The objection has consequences for the metaphysical conclusion the *Meditations* is aiming at. Without completeness, the move from clear-and-distinct conceivability of thinking-without-extension to real distinctness of mind and body fails. The Sixth Meditation argument inherits the gap. So Arnauld's worry, though framed as an objection to the Second Meditation's self-conception, in fact threatens the entire dualist conclusion of the *Meditations*.

What is the right thing to say, then, about the meditator's nature on the basis of the Second Meditation? Something modest. Thinking is the property the meditator cannot suppose himself to lack while doubting. The meditator is at least a thinker. Whether he is also an extended thing, the Second Meditation's procedure is unable to determine. The doubt has bracketed the question. Settling it requires looking outside the doubt, where considerations from natural science, from the dependence of mental events on neural events, and from the absence of any plausible mechanism for non-physical thinking, all weigh in favour of the view that the thinking thing is in fact corporeal. Arnauld's objection clears a space that physicalism then occupies.

So Descartes does not have a satisfactory response. The completeness claim he uses to bridge from the Second Meditation's self-conception to the Sixth Meditation's metaphysics is precisely what Arnauld questions, and Descartes's defence of it is question-begging. The right verdict is that the meditator's nature is undetermined by the *Meditations*' method, and that the simplest alternative — that the thinker is a corporeal thing whose corporeality the doubt has temporarily put out of view — survives Descartes's arguments untouched.
