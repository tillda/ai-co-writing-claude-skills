# The cogito and 'I am a thinking thing' How does the proposition 'I am, I exist' contribute to Descartes's Second Meditation argument that he is a thinking thing?

In my view the proposition "I am, I exist" does real work but more limited work than Descartes thinks. It establishes that something exists whenever the meditator thinks, and that this "something" cannot coherently be doubted. That much I accept. But the further move — from "I exist whenever I think" to "I am, in essence, a thinking thing" — overreaches. Descartes slides from the modest claim that thinking is sufficient for his existence at this moment to the much stronger claim that thinking is what he essentially is. The first is true; the second does not follow.

Set out in steps, the argument of the Second Meditation runs as follows. The meditator has, in the First Meditation, doubted everything he can — the senses, the body, even mathematical truths under the malicious-demon hypothesis. He now asks whether anything survives this doubt. He notices that even if he is being deceived, even if there is no body and no external world, the very fact that he is thinking, doubting, deceiving himself or being deceived, requires that there be someone thinking. From this comes the famous proposition: "I am, I exist, is necessarily true whenever it is put forward by me or conceived in my mind." This is what "I am, I exist" contributes at the first stage. It serves as Descartes's "Archimedean point" — one piece of certainty rescued from radical doubt, the foundation on which everything else can be rebuilt.

The contribution to the argument that he is a thinking thing comes in the next stage. Descartes asks: granted that I exist, what am I? He runs through the candidates. He had previously thought of himself as having a body, as moving, as nourished, as engaged in sense-perception. But all these properties depend on having a body, and the existence of a body is precisely what is in doubt under the malicious-demon hypothesis. So none of these can be what he essentially is, in the sense relevant here — he cannot affirm them under the doubt. What survives? Thinking. He cannot doubt that he thinks, because to doubt is to think. So thinking is something he can affirm of himself even under the most extreme doubt. He concludes: "I am, in the strict sense, only a thing that thinks."

The contribution of "I am, I exist", then, is twofold. First, it survives the doubt as the one item of indubitable knowledge, which gives Descartes something to anchor his enquiry to. Second, by considering *what* exists when "I am, I exist" is true, he tries to identify the essential nature of the "I". The cogito proposition is not just a starting point but the lever Descartes uses to extract a thesis about his nature.

Now for the evaluation. The first part of the argument is, in my view, sound. It is hard to see how one could coherently doubt one's own existence at the very moment one is thinking. To doubt is to think, and thinking requires a thinker. The cogito, in this minimal form, is plausible.

The second part is where the trouble starts. Descartes moves from "I cannot doubt that I think" to "I am essentially a thinking thing — and only a thinking thing". This is a slide. From the fact that I cannot affirm of myself, under the doubt, that I have a body, it does not follow that I do not have a body. It only follows that I do not, at this stage, *know* that I have a body. The Method of Doubt is an epistemic tool, not a metaphysical one. Descartes is conflating "I cannot affirm under doubt that I am extended" with "I am not extended". This is the famous error Arnauld pointed out: that what I clearly and distinctly perceive about myself does not exhaust what I am. I might be a thinking thing *and* an extended thing without being able to tell from the doubt alone.

Hobbes raised the related objection that there is nothing in "I am, I exist" that rules out being something corporeal that thinks. The cogito establishes that something thinks; it does not establish that this something is non-physical. That further inference requires an extra premise — that thinking and extension are incompatible — and Descartes does not establish it. He simply assumes that, because he can entertain the thought of himself thinking without entertaining the thought of himself as extended, he must therefore be capable of existing without extension. But conceptual separability does not entail real separability. I can think of the morning star without thinking of the evening star, but they are the same planet.

So the cogito does what it sets out to do: it grounds existence under doubt. The slide from there to "I am a thinking thing" — meaning a wholly non-physical, separable substance whose essence is thought — is the part that goes wrong. The most that follows is that I am, at minimum, a thinking thing. Whether I am also a corporeal thing remains entirely open.

The better view, I think, is that the thinker is something physical engaging in mental processes. The brain is what does the thinking, and "I" refers to the person — a single subject who is both bodily and mental. The cogito is consistent with this view: the thing that cannot doubt its own existence whenever it thinks can perfectly well be a particular brain, organised into the kind of pattern we call a mind. Nothing in the cogito rules this out, and contemporary evidence — that thinking depends on neural function in highly specific ways — strongly supports it.

In conclusion, "I am, I exist" contributes the foundational certainty of the Second Meditation: it gives Descartes one indubitable proposition to build on, and it lets him ask what kind of thing exists when this proposition is true. That much is achieved. But the further inference to "I am, in the strict sense, only a thing that thinks" is not earned. It conflates what survives doubt with what is the case, and it leaves room for the corporeal thinker that Hobbes pointed out. The cogito proves that I exist when I think; it does not prove that I am nothing but a thinker.
