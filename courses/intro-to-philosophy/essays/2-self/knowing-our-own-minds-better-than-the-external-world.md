# Knowing our own minds better than the external world Explain Descartes' argument that we know our own minds better than the external world. Is his argument plausible?

In my view Descartes's argument is partly plausible and partly not. It is plausible that, under the kind of radical doubt he sets in motion, certain facts about one's own current thinking survive in a way that facts about the external world do not. To that extent there is a real epistemic asymmetry. But Descartes wants much more: that the mind in general is better known than the body, and that we have privileged, reliable, and complete access to our own mental lives. These stronger claims do not follow, and they are not true. So the argument secures a narrow point and over-extends it into a metaphysical thesis it cannot support.

Descartes's argument runs as follows. In the First Meditation he doubts everything he can — the senses, the existence of external objects, even his own body. The malicious-demon hypothesis makes it conceivable that all his beliefs about the world outside him are false. In the Second Meditation he discovers that one thing survives the doubt: his own existence as a thinker. He cannot coherently doubt that he is thinking, since to doubt is itself to think. From this he draws a conclusion not just about existence but about knowledge. While the existence of any external thing — a piece of wax, a fire, his own body — is open to doubt, the existence of his own thinking is not. The mind is therefore, he says, "easier to know" than the body.

The case is reinforced by the wax passage. Descartes considers a piece of wax. Its sensible qualities — colour, shape, smell, hardness — change completely when it is held to the fire. What remains the same? Only the abstract idea of an extended, flexible, changeable thing. And that idea, he argues, is grasped not by sense but by the intellect. So even when we think we know a body well, the knowing is the work of the mind. From this Descartes concludes that the mind is better known than any body, since every act of knowing a body is at the same time a clearer act of knowing the mind that does the knowing.

Now to evaluation. The narrow asymmetry has some force. If we restrict ourselves to a particular moment of conscious thought, it is hard to deny that the proposition "I am thinking now" is more secure than any proposition about the external world. The malicious demon can deceive me about the wax, but cannot make it the case that, when I am thinking, I am not thinking. So under radical doubt, present-tense first-person reports about the occurrence of thinking enjoy a special status. This much is plausible.

But Descartes wants the conclusion to extend much further, and several objections show the extension fails.

The first objection concerns the scope of self-knowledge. We know now that people are routinely wrong about their own minds. Consider work in psychology on confabulation: people give confident reasons for choices that experiments show were made on quite different grounds. Consider Freud's point about the unconscious: people are driven by motives they sincerely deny. Consider ordinary cases of self-deception, where someone insists they are not jealous, or not in love, while their behaviour shows otherwise. In each case the subject's first-person report does not match what is going on in their mind. If first-person access were as direct and reliable as Descartes claims, none of this should be possible. The Cartesian might reply that the subject still has direct access to the *appearance* of their state, even when they are wrong about its real character. Perhaps. But that is a much weaker claim than "we know our own minds better than the external world". It only tells us that we know how things seem to us — and this we knew already. The interesting claim, that we know what is really going on in our minds, is precisely the one undermined by the empirical evidence.

The second objection is that the argument does not establish what Descartes wants it to. Even granting that "I am thinking" survives doubt, this only shows that one proposition about the mind is more secure than any proposition about the body. It does not show that the *mind* is better known than the *body* in general. By analogy: the proposition "either it is raining or it is not" is more secure than any empirical proposition about weather, but it does not follow that we know the disjunction better than we know whether to take an umbrella. Descartes is generalising from one privileged proposition to a general thesis about epistemic access, and the generalisation is unwarranted.

The third objection, mentioned for completeness, is that the wax passage proves less than Descartes claims. Even if knowing a body involves the intellect, that does not show the mind is better known than the body. It only shows that the mind is involved in knowing the body. By the same reasoning, the eye would be better known than what it sees, since seeing requires an eye. The conclusion does not follow.

The better view, I think, is the one suggested by the physicalist picture. The mind is what the brain is doing. Knowing one's own mind is not a matter of inner inspection of a private substance; it is a matter of being in certain physical and functional states and being able to report on some of them. There is a real and useful asymmetry between first-person and third-person access to a particular brain — the subject has direct functional access to certain of their own states that no one else has — but it is not the metaphysically loaded asymmetry Descartes wants. We have *some* privileged access to *some* of our mental states. We do not have complete or infallible access to our minds as a whole.

In conclusion, the narrow point is plausible: present-tense reports of one's own thinking have a security that reports about the external world lack. But Descartes wants to leverage this into a general thesis that the mind is better known than the body, and the leverage fails. We are routinely mistaken about our own motives, beliefs, and feelings; the asymmetry covers only a small part of the mental, and even there the conclusion does not generalise to anything as strong as Descartes draws. So the argument is plausible only in its weakest form, and the strong Cartesian thesis should be rejected.
