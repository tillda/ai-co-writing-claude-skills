# Tarski's Hierarchy (against)

I reject Tarski's hierarchical treatment of truth — and, by extension, of reference, ascription, and assertion. The objection is not narrowly that the hierarchy fails as a solution to the Liar; it fails as an analysis of these semantic relations full stop, because it does not reflect linguistic intuition about how we use them. We have a single, general truth predicate; we have a single, general relation of reference; we apply both across orders without equivocation. The hierarchy denies all this in order to secure formal consistency.

## The view to reject

The substantive Tarskian thesis — the philosophically interesting one, not the merely methodological one — holds that there is no such thing as truth in general. There is only first-order truth (truth₁), applicable to sentences about ordinary objects; second-order truth (truth₂), applicable to sentences about first-order sentences; and so on. Each truthₙ is defined in a metalanguage one level up; no predicate spans the whole hierarchy. By extension, the same structure must be imposed on every semantic relation that supports a Liar-shape paradox: refer₁, refer₂, …, ascribe₁, ascribe₂, …, assert₁, assert₂, ….

## Best argument for the view

The pull is real. Unrestricted self-application of the schema demonstrably yields contradiction; if consistency requires "true" to be index-bound, the hierarchy is the disciplined response. The construction is rigorous and shows that consistent formal semantics is achievable so long as the order distinction is respected.

## Why I reject it

1. **The truthₙ predicates do not match how "true" is used.** Ordinary English has a single, fully general truth predicate, deployed across orders without equivocation. If a colleague says, "The statement 'Obama is a Muslim' is false. Obama is not a Muslim", I can reply, "Everything you just said is true" — covering one second-order and one first-order claim. I am not punning, switching predicates, or making a category mistake. Whatever the truthₙ have in common, we evidently grasp it, and what we grasp is the general notion the substantive Tarskian denies.

2. **The same problem afflicts the parallel hierarchies for "refer", "ascribe", and "assert".** Liar-shape paradoxes can be built from each: *the smallest natural number not referred to by this expression*, *this sentence lacks the feature this sentence ascribes to it*, *what this sentence asserts is not the case*. To stay consistent, the Tarskian must hierarchise all of them. But I plainly refer to physical objects and to expressions using *the same* relation of reference — this very sentence does both. Denying a general relation of referring (or asserting, or ascribing) is even less defensible than denying a general truth predicate, and natural language plainly permits these self-referential predicates without breaking down. The truth-hierarchy is one symptom of an across-the-board denial of perfectly ordinary semantic relations.

3. **The theory cannot state itself.** A theory that talks about the whole hierarchy at once has no determinate order itself, so no truthₙ applies to its own sentences. The substantive Tarskian cannot, on his own terms, claim that his theory is *true*. By parity, none of the refer / assert / ascribe hierarchies applies to it either: its sentences cannot even be said to assert, refer to, or ascribe properties to anything. The view is self-undermining in the strongest sense.

4. **The truth_N patch improves the theory without rescuing it.** Repair: introduce truth_N, where a sentence is true_N iff it is true_n for some *n*. "Everything you just said is true" then spans first- and second-order claims without contradiction. But we can still understand a *fully general* notion of truth — true regardless of order, regardless of whether the sentence has an order at all — under which a sentence that says *a* is *F* is true iff *a* is *F*. That fully general notion is what English speakers operate with; truth_N approximates it without giving it back.

5. **The methodological reading is just changing the subject.** If the proposal is merely that we agree to refrain from using a general truth predicate, the puzzle is not solved; it is sidestepped. The general predicate is there in English; switching attention to a stipulated language in which it isn't is no diagnosis. Methodological reading: evasive. Substantive reading: implausible and self-undermining. Nothing else is on offer.

## Considered view

The hierarchy is an ingenious technical achievement and the wrong analysis of these semantic relations. It does not reflect how "true" is used (unequivocally across orders), it forces the same disfiguring move on "refer", "ascribe", and "assert" where it is plainly worse, and it leaves the theory unable to state itself. The truth_N patch improves it without rescuing it. The right response to the Liar is not hierarchical amputation of our semantic vocabulary but recognition that the Liar fails to express a proposition for independent reasons — see *Liar's Paradox*; for why the more sophisticated formal alternative fares no better, see *Kripke's Fixed-Point Theory of Truth*.
