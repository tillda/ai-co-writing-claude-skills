# Strawson's individuation principle for minds Strawson states the principle: 'If we are to talk coherently about individual consciousnesses or minds...we must know the difference between one such item and two such items.' Is this principle acceptable? Does it make a problem for Descartes?

In my view the principle is acceptable, and it makes a serious problem for Descartes. The reason is simple. To talk coherently about individuals of any kind — chairs, numbers, planets, minds — we must be able in principle to distinguish one from two. If we have no way of telling whether we are referring to one item or two, we are not really referring to a particular at all. Descartes treats minds as basic non-physical substances, but he gives us no way to count them. So either his talk of minds is incoherent in the way Strawson alleges, or his minds are not the substances he claims.

The principle Strawson sets out is a general one about reference to particulars. To use a singular term and intend it to pick out an individual, we must be able to draw the distinction between picking out one such individual and picking out two. In Strawson's example, talk of individual consciousnesses requires us to know what the difference is between one consciousness and two consciousnesses. This is sometimes called a *criterion of identity*, sometimes a *principle of individuation*. The thought is that nothing counts as a kind of particular if its instances cannot in principle be distinguished and counted.

This principle is highly plausible. Consider a parallel. If someone tells us that there are "auras" all around us but cannot say in any case whether we are dealing with one aura or with two, we will conclude that "aura" does not pick out a kind of thing at all, or at any rate not a kind of *particular thing*. The same goes for any candidate kind of object. To exist as a particular is to be one rather than two, and we must be able to tell, at least in principle, which it is. The principle is not metaphysically extravagant; it is a basic constraint on what counts as singular reference.

One might worry that the principle is too strong. After all, we sometimes talk about individuals whose criteria of identity are vague or contested — clouds, nations, organisms in odd cases. Does this mean we should weaken the principle? I think not. In each of these cases there is *some* working criterion in ordinary use, even if marginal cases are hard. Clouds are individuated by spatial continuity and density; nations by territorial and political boundaries. The challenge to the Cartesian is more radical: he gives us no criterion at all. Descartes's mental substance has no spatial location, no temporal boundary apart from its thinking, and no relation to any body that could fix its identity. There is nothing in his account that would let us answer, in any case, whether we are dealing with one Cartesian mind or two.

This is where the problem for Descartes bites. He claims that each person is identical with a single mental substance. But what makes my mind one rather than several? What makes it different from yours, considered just as mental substances and apart from any bodies? Descartes might appeal to the unity of consciousness — the fact that my thoughts are all *my* thoughts, available to a single point of view. But unity of consciousness across time and across thoughts is exactly what Strawson's principle demands an *account* of. To say "there is one mind here because there is one consciousness" simply restates the claim of unity without explaining how the individual is picked out. We need to know what makes the consciousness one consciousness, and Descartes does not tell us.

The Cartesian might reply that the individuation of minds is primitive — minds are the kinds of thing that just *are* one or many, and no further account is needed. But this reply is unsatisfactory. We do not accept this move for any other kind of particular. We do not say that chairs or numbers are individuated primitively, with no further story to be told. The Cartesian is helping himself to a free pass that he would not extend to anyone else. And the move is suspicious because, in the case of bodies, individuation is straightforward: bodies occupy distinct regions of space, and that is what makes them one rather than two. The Cartesian has thrown away the only obvious resource for individuating mental items — namely, the bodies they are tied to — and offers nothing in its place.

A second Cartesian reply is to bite the bullet and grant that we cannot in principle know whether two minds inhabit one body, or one mind inhabits two. But this is a high cost. It means abandoning the everyday use of mental terms, on which we routinely take ourselves to know whether one person or two is in pain, thinking, deciding. If Cartesianism cannot accommodate this, so much the worse for Cartesianism.

The natural conclusion is that minds must be individuated by something physical, and the most plausible candidate is the brain. On the physicalist view I prefer, a mind is identified by the physical system whose activity constitutes it — a particular brain over a particular stretch of time. Two brains, two minds. One brain, one mind. This handles the individuation principle straightforwardly, because brains are paradigm individuals: they occupy distinct places, can be counted, have determinate boundaries. The mental is real but not a separate substance; it is what a particular brain is doing. There is no extra "mental object" requiring its own criteria of identity.

In conclusion, Strawson's principle is correct: to talk coherently about individual minds we must know the difference between one and two. The principle is just an application of a general constraint on reference to particulars, and rejecting it is no more attractive than rejecting that constraint. Descartes cannot meet the principle, because his mental substance has no resources for individuation independent of the body. The lesson is not that we should give up talk of individual minds, but that we should anchor it in something that can be counted — and the obvious candidate is the brain.
