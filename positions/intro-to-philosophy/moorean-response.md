# The Moorean Response to Skepticism

I distinguish two things that often get bundled together under "Moore's reply to scepticism", and I take opposite views on them.

- **Moore's "Here is one hand" argument does *not* defeat skepticism.** Held up by itself, it begs the question. The skeptic's whole point is that perceptual claims like "here is a hand" are precisely what's in doubt; gesturing at hands and declaring the external world proven simply asserts what the skeptic disputes. As an argument it carries no dialectical weight against someone who has actually taken the First-Meditation doubts seriously.
- **The Moorean *shift*, however, does substantially weaken the skeptic's position.** Cast the disagreement as an inconsistent triad: (A) I know I have hands; (B) knowing I have hands requires knowing I'm not a BIV/dreaming/deceived; (C) I don't know I'm not a BIV/dreaming/deceived. All three have some initial plausibility; they can't all be true; one must go. The skeptic rejects (A). But (A) is *maximally* initially plausible, while (B) and (C) are abstract theoretical claims about the structure of knowledge — far less obvious than the proposition they're being used to overturn. Methodologically, when an inconsistent set forces a choice, you should reject the *least* plausible member, not the most. So the skeptic is making the worst available move. Cf. Huemer *Knowledge, Reality, and Value* §7.4 [98-121].

The shift doesn't *refute* skepticism in a knock-down sense — it doesn't show (B) or (C) is false, only that they're worse candidates for rejection than (A). But that's enough to make the skeptical conclusion epistemically unjustified: the skeptic owes us a reason why we should give up the most obvious thing in the set in order to keep the less-obvious things, and they don't have one. Skepticism becomes, at best, an unmotivated theoretical preference.

Why I reject the rivals:

- **Strict Cartesian/global skepticism** — fails for the reason just given: it asks us to reject the most plausible premise in an inconsistent set. (Also self-defeating in the standard ways, but the shift does the main work even if you set self-refutation aside.)
- **Moore-as-proof reading** — the bare "here is one hand" gesture is dialectically empty; the work is done by the comparative-plausibility argument, not by waving hands. Treating Moore's *Proof of an External World* as itself a refutation conflates the two.
- **Quietist / "don't engage with the skeptic" responses** — concede too much. The skeptic's argument *can* be answered on its own terms; you don't have to refuse the engagement.

In detail: see Huemer *Knowledge, Reality, and Value* §7.4 (the canonical modern statement of the shift, including the (A)/(B)/(C) framing and the initial-plausibility methodology) and §6.3.4 (the BIV hypothesis assessed as a bad theory — Moorean in spirit). The textbook Moore is in *Reading Philosophy* ch. 1 ('Proof of an External World').
