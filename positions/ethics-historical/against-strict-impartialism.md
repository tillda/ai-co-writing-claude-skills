# Against Strict Impartialism; Against Pure Agent-Relativity

Mill's "strictly impartial as a disinterested and benevolent spectator" requirement and the egoist's denial of agent-neutral value are two extreme answers to the partiality question. Both fail. The defensible position is in between: significant permission for partiality to oneself, family, and projects, plus genuine agent-neutral reasons that make some duties to strangers real (though not totalising).

The argument:

- **Strict impartialism is overdemanding.** Taken literally, it implies that a person who could save additional lives by giving more is morally comparable to a mass murderer, and that one should give until the marginal benefit to a stranger no longer exceeds the marginal benefit to oneself. Almost no one, including those who endorse the principle, lives by it. A theory that condemns every reasonably comfortable life as morally horrible should at least be suspected of having miscalibrated standards — when literally every admirable person counts as terrible by the theory's own lights, the theory is the more likely culprit than the universal verdict.

- **The intuitive case for partiality.** It seems permissible to use what food you have to feed yourself rather than give it to a stranger who would enjoy it more, and obligatory to feed your own children before strangers' — even at the cost of giving the children only modest goods while strangers go without basic ones. Common-sense morality permits *some* degree of partiality, not the most extreme degree. The egoist over-corrects; the strict impartialist under-corrects.

- **Pure agent-relativity fails Extreme Trolley.** The egoist's escape from impartialism is to deny agent-neutral value: nothing is good *simpliciter*, only good *for* particular agents. This avoids overdemandingness but breaks elsewhere. Consider a runaway trolley carrying a nuclear bomb toward New York — twenty million deaths if it arrives — which can be diverted onto a remote patch of land destroying a single empty cabin (the absent owner being mildly inconvenienced). The agent-relativist must say there is no reason to divert: the diversion serves twenty million strangers' interests and harms one stranger's, and on the agent-relative view there is no overall-better outcome to weigh. That verdict is plainly wrong. So agent-neutral value exists.

- **What the middle position commits to.** Once agent-neutral value is granted, some pull toward strangers is real: it would be better, *simpliciter*, if more people had food and fewer suffered, and that fact gives me a reason to act even when no project of mine is at stake. The reason is not totalising — it sits alongside agent-relative reasons that let me prioritise those close to me — but it is a reason. Singer's pond argument has force as a wake-up call against denying any duty to strangers. The strict impartialist conclusion (give until marginal-equal) is over-extracted from it.

- **Why I reject the rivals.** Pure egoism rules absurdly on Extreme Trolley. Strict utilitarian impartialism rules absurdly on every comfortable life. The middle ground — moderate partiality plus real but limited agent-neutral demands — accommodates the cases.

**Usage:** Additive to a Mill essay on impartiality or demandingness. Lead with Mill's own appeal to the disinterested spectator and his secondary-principles softening; the partiality / agent-relativity dialectic is the modern formulation of the trade-off and clarifies what Mill's position commits him to in the limit.
