# Humeanism Cannot Account for Akrasia; Aristotle Has the Resources

*Akrasia* has a stable phenomenology: the agent judges A best, feels tempted by B, finds A difficult and B easy, voluntarily chooses B, feels guilt afterward, and is judged by us to have acted irrationally. The Humean view that the strongest desire wins makes this phenomenology unintelligible. Aristotle's distinction between rational judgment and appetite has the conceptual room Hume lacks.

- **The Humean dilemma.** If B answered to the stronger desire, choosing B was rational, not weak. If A answered to the stronger desire and the agent chose B anyway, that is unintelligible on Humean terms. The asymmetries of difficulty, guilt, and rational criticism cannot be reconstructed from desire-strength alone, and the equal-strength rescue is a probability-zero coincidence that anyway fails to explain the asymmetries.
- **Aristotle's escape.** Once rational judgment is admitted as a different *kind* of motivation from appetite, not commensurable on a single dimension of strength, *akrasia* becomes intelligible: appetite carries the agent against the standing judgment, and the asymmetries (effort, guilt, blame) follow. This vindicates the broad Aristotelian framework against any Humean reduction even where the details of his intermediate-state account remain debated.

**Usage:** Use in Aristotle essays on *akrasia* or on the Socrates-denial; also deployable in Hume essays asking whether his moral psychology is adequate. Stay close to the phenomenology; do not let the discussion drift into modern theories of weakness of will.

**Applicable to:** akrasia, weakness of will, acting contrary to best practical judgment, Aristotle on incontinence, Socrates' denial of akrasia, reason vs desire in Aristotle, Humean moral psychology, *ethics-historical / 2. Aristotle*, *ethics-historical / 3. Hume*
