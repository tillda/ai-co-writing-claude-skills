2	## 1 INTRODUCTION
3	1
4	### 1.1 The field of metaethics
5	Metaethics addresses questions about the nature of evaluative statements and judgments, including questions about the meaning of evaluative discourse, our knowledge of value, the objectivity of value, and how value judgments provide reasons for action.
6	1
7	### 1.2 What is objectivity?
8	An objective property of a thing is one that does not constitutively depend on observers' attitudes towards that thing.
9	2
10	### 1.3 Five metaethical theories
11	There are exactly five metaethical theories: non-cognitivism, subjectivism, nihilism, naturalism, and intuitionism.
12	4
13	### 1.4 An alternative taxonomy of metaethical views
14	Metaethical theories may be divided into monistic and dualistic theories. Monistic theories may be further divided into reductionist and eliminativist theories; but these theories differ from one another only semantically. Only intuitionism is dualistic and differs fundamentally from all other theories.
15	7
16	### 1.5 A rationalist intuitionism
17	I will defend a form of intuitionism according to which terms such as 'good' refer to objective, irreducible value properties, which we know about on the basis of rational intuition, and our evaluative judgments give us reasons for action independent of our desires.
18	9
19	### 1.6 Background assumptions
20	I assume that there are objective facts and knowledge outside the area of ethics. I shall argue that ethics is no different.

22	# PART I: ALTERNATIVE METAETHICAL THEORIES
23	## 2 NON-COGNITIVISM
24	17
25	### 2.1 Classical non-cognitivism
26	17
27	Non-cognitivists hold that evaluative statements do not assert propositions; instead, they are more like imperatives or expressions of emotion.
28	### 2.2 How can we tell if cognitivism is true?
29	18
30	Non-cognitivism should be tested empirically, by examining how we use evaluative language.
31	### 2.3 The linguistic evidence for cognitivism
32	20
33	In ordinary language, evaluative statements behave in every discernible way like cognitive statements. Non-cognitivists have difficulty making sense of many ordinary statements involving evaluative terms.
34	#### *2.4 Hare on moral truth
35	25
36	Hare unsuccessfully tries to explain why we can call evaluative statements ‘true’ and ‘false’.
37	#### *2.5 Gibbard’s factual-normative worlds
38	30
39	Gibbard’s notion of factual-normative worlds offers no solution to the Frege-Geach problem.
40	#### *2.6 Blackburn’s solutions
41	34
42	Blackburn’s solution to the Frege-Geach problem rests on misinterpretations of language and fails to address several related problems for non-cognitivism.
43	#### *2.7 Timmons’ assertoric non-descriptivism
44	38
45	Timmons’ theory cannot account for moral error and cannot explain the distinction between realism and anti-realism.
46	### 2.8 The introspective evidence
47	44
48	Introspectively, moral judgments seem just like beliefs, and unlike emotions, desires, or other non-cognitive states.
49	## 3 SUBJECTIVISM
50	48
51	### 3.1 What is subjectivism?
52	48
53	Subjectivists hold that evaluative statements report the attitudes of observers towards the objects of evaluation. Different forms of subjectivism invoke, respectively, the attitudes of individuals, society, God, or hypothetical ideal observers.
54	3.2 Individualist subjectivism 49
55	Individualist subjectivism implies that Nazi moral statements are true, that moral disagreement is impossible, that I am morally infallible, and that arbitrary attitudes generate obligations.
56	3.3 Cultural relativism 51
57	Cultural relativism suffers from analogous problems.
58	3.4 The divine command theory 54
59	The divine command theory of ethics suffers from some of the same problems. In addition, there are questions about whether God exists and how we can know what he wants if he does.
60	*3.5 The ideal observer theory 60
61	If we are careful to make the theory non-circular, the ideal observer theory faces analogous problems to those facing individual subjectivism and cultural relativism.
62	3.6 The subjectivist fallacy 63
63	The most common arguments for subjectivism rest on transparent confusions between belief and truth, and between what causes a belief and the content of the belief.
64	4 REDUCTIONISM 66
65	4.1 What is reductionism? 66
66	Reductionists believe (i) that what it is for a thing to be good can be explained using non-evaluative expressions, and (ii) that we know moral truths on the basis of observation.
67	4.2 Analytic reductionism 67
68	Analytic reductionists believe that some non-evaluative expression is synonymous with ‘good’. This is refuted by G. E. Moore’s Open Question Argument.
69	4.3 The is-ought gap 72
70	4.3.1 Hume’s Law: an initial statement 72
71	It is impossible to validly deduce an evaluative statement from non-evaluative premises.
72	*4.3.2 Searle’s challenge 74
73	Searle’s attempted counter-example fails due to equivocation.
74	XII Analytical Contents
75	*4.3.3 Geach's challenge 76
76	Geach's attempted counter-example fails because it is invalid and one of its premises is evaluative.
77	*4.3.4 Prior's challenge 78
78	Prior's counter-example is uninteresting since it cannot provide a plausible model of how typical ethical knowledge is gained.
79	*4.3.5 Karmo's proof 79
80	Karmo has proven in general that there is no sound derivation of a non-trivial evaluative proposition from non-evaluative premises, where an evaluative proposition is one whose truth, once all the natural facts have been fixed, depends on which value system is correct.
81	4.4 Synthetic reductionism 83
82	Synthetic reductionists hold that, although the meaning of 'good' cannot be given using non-evaluative expressions, one can explain what goodness is using non-evaluative expressions.
83	4.4.1 Can moral facts be known by observation? 84
84	Even if moral properties are reducible, it would be fallacious to infer that we can know moral truths by observation. We cannot observe that a thing is good, because there is no distinctive way that good things look, sound, smell, taste, or feel.
85	4.4.2 Can moral facts be known by inference to the best explanation? 88
86	Even if some moral facts are explanatory, we cannot know moral truths by inference to the best explanation, because moral facts do not explain any observations that could not be explained as well by non-moral facts.
87	*4.4.3 Can moral claims be tested? 90
88	Moral theories do not generate any testable predictions without relying either on ad hoc posits or on the assumption that conscious beings have some independent access to moral truths.
89	*4.4.4 The unifying power of moral explanations 92
90	Moral explanations of some observations might offer the advantage of unifying seemingly disparate phenomena. But competing explanations of the same phenomena that either invoke different moral properties or posit unified
91	non-moral properties can achieve the same advantage.
92	4.5 The argument from radical dissimilarity 94
93	The simplest argument against reductionism is that moral properties just seem, on their face, radically different from natural properties.
94	4.6 Explaining moral beliefs 95
95	Reductionist accounts of how moral beliefs might be justified fail to apply to nearly anyone's actual beliefs.
96	## PART II: ETHICAL INTUITIONISM
97	### 5 MORAL KNOWLEDGE 99
98	#### 5.1 The principle of Phenomenal Conservatism 99
99	It is reasonable to assume that things are as they appear, in the absence of grounds for doubting this. Judgment in general presupposes this principle.
100	#### 5.2 Ethical intuitions 101
101	Intuitions are defined as initial, intellectual appearances. Ethical intuitions are intuitions with evaluative contents. They are not merely beliefs or products of beliefs. Not all intuitions are equally credible.
102	#### 5.3 Misunderstandings of intuitionism 105
103	Intuitionists do not take intuitions to be infallible or indefeasible, nor do they hold that all moral knowledge is intuitive.
104	#### 5.4 Common epistemological objections 107
105	I respond to the objections that (1) we need an argument that intuitions are generally reliable, (2) we have no way of checking intuitions, (3) intuition can be used to justify any claim, and (4) intuition is 'queer'.
106	#### 5.5 The implausibility of nihilism: a Moorean argument 115
107	The premises nihilists use to argue for their position are far less plausible than many of the moral claims those premises are supposed to refute. We should rather reject the nihilist's premises than reject all evaluative claims.
108	#### 5.6 Direct realism and the subjective inversion 117
109	Direct realists about perception hold that perceptual experiences render beliefs about the physical world justified, not by constituting signs of external states of affairs, but by constituting apparent direct awareness of external phenomena.
110	Intuitionists should take a similar direct realist view about ethics.
111	*5.7 The isolation of the moral realm* 122
112	A priori knowledge is explained by our ability to grasp abstract objects, or universals. An adequate grasp of one or more universals leads to reliable beliefs about the properties of and relations among those universals, even though universals do not cause our beliefs about them.
113	6 DISAGREEMENT AND ERROR 128
114	6.1 The prevalence of moral disagreement 129
115	There are many disagreements about value, both between societies and between individuals within a society.
116	6.2 The idiot’s veto 131
117	The ‘Idiot’s Veto’ is the idea that a claim is disqualified from counting as objective by the mere fact of some people’s disagreeing with it.
118	6.3 Can intuitionists explain disagreement? 132
119	Some object that intuitionists cannot explain why there are many disagreements about value.
120	6.3.1 The caricature 133
121	The argument from disagreement has sometimes rested on an absurd caricature according to which intuitionists hold that intuition immediately and infallibly resolves all moral questions.
122	6.3.2 The prevalence of non-moral disagreement 134
123	The prevalence of moral disagreement is rendered unsurprising by the fact that there are many examples of non-moral disagreements with features similar to those of moral disagreements.
124	6.3.3 A menagerie of error 137
125	There are many causes of error in both moral and non-moral matters, including bias, confusion, fallacies, hasty judgments, and so on.
126	6.3.4 Disagreement is predictable 140
127	Errors are particularly common in areas where there are strong and frequent biases, where people defer to their cultures, and where people defer to religion, and in all areas of philosophy. This makes error and disagreement in ethics unsurprising.
128	6.4 Can intuitionists resolve disagreements? 141
129	Some object that intuitionists have no way to resolve ethical disagreements.
130	6.4.1 Hypothetical disagreements 142
131	Intuitionists can offer no reasoned way of resolving a disagreement if it is stipulated that each party refuses to grant any premises to his opponent. But this shows nothing interesting.
132	6.4.2 Controversial moral questions 142
133	There are some actual moral controversies that intuitionists are unable to resolve. But this is true of all metaethical theories and is no evidence against intuitionism.
134	6.4.3 Foundational moral controversies 142
135	There may be disagreements about foundational moral principles that intuitionists cannot resolve. This worry is generally overstated and also fails to provide evidence against intuitionism.
136	6.4.4 Disputes in general 144
137	Some object that intuitionists cannot resolve any disagreements involving conflicting intuitions. But while intuitionists cannot resolve all disputes, nor can they force people to be rational, they can offer rational ways of attempting to resolve many such disagreements.
138	6.5 The self-refutation problem 146
139	Proponents of the argument from disagreement face the problem that many people disagree with each alternative metaethical theory and with the argument from disagreement itself.
140	6.6 Disagreement as an argument for realism 148
141	Disagreement poses a greater challenge to anti-realists than to realists.
142	6.6.1 Can anti-realists explain disagreement? 148
143	Anti-realists have more trouble explaining ethical disagreement than moral realists do.
144	6.6.2 Can anti-realists resolve disagreements? 149
145	Anti-realists can offer no rational way of resolving fundamental moral disagreements. Current practice in moral philosophy seems to presuppose intuitionism.
146	7 PRACTICAL REASONS 155
147	7.1 The Humean argument against realism 155
148	Motivating reasons are distinguished from normative reasons. Humeans believe that reasons for action depend on desires, that moral attitudes inherently provide reasons for action, and that no mere belief about an objective fact is sufficient for the having of a desire. They conclude that moral attitudes are not beliefs about objective facts.
149	7.2 The connection between motivating and normative reasons 157
150	The ‘ought implies can’ principle and the principle of charity in interpretation can each be used to establish a close tie between normative and motivating reasons.
151	7.3 A rationalist conception of motivation 161
152	People are motivated by appetites, emotions, prudential considerations, and impartial reasons. The latter two are not desires in the ordinary sense. Moral reasons are a species of impartial reasons.
153	7.4 Why believe the Humean conception? 166
154	7.4.1 The intuitive appeal of the Humean conception 166
155	The intuitive appeal of the Humean conception of reasons depends on one’s having only typical non-evaluative beliefs in mind. Hume’s central argument for his position fails.
156	*7.4.2 Smith’s argument 168
157	Smith’s argument for the Humean conception, relying on the notion of ‘direction of fit’, rests upon an equivocation.
158	7.5 Extending the Humean conception? 171
159	Modern-day Humeans reject Hume’s extreme position that no action can ever be rational or irrational. They propose several constraints on rational action.
160	7.5.1 The Foresight Constraint 173
161	The foresight constraint holds that one is rationally required to give some weight to one’s future desires. This cannot be sustained on a broadly Humean conception of normative reasons.
162	7.5.2 The Imagination Constraint 175
163	The imagination constraint holds that one rationally ought to give some weight to desires one would have if
164	one were to vividly and correctly imagine certain states of affairs. The Humean conception cannot support this.
165	## 7.5.3 The Consistency and Coherence Constraints 176
166	The consistency and coherence constraints hold that one rationally ought to modify an inconsistent or incoherent set of desires so as to render it consistent and coherent. The Humean conception cannot support this.
167	## 7.5.4 The Deliberation Constraint 179
168	The deliberation constraint holds that one rationally ought to deliberate about what to do and abide by the results of that deliberation. The Humean conception cannot support this. The Humean conception affords no grounds for rationally criticizing any choice.
169	## 7.5.5 Coda: the need for evaluative facts 181
170	The problems facing the Humean conception can be avoided by recognizing objective, evaluative facts. This points us towards intuitionism. Scanlon's attempt to avoid the need for non-natural evaluative facts faces problems of circularity and infinite regress.
171	## 7.6 The authority of morality 184
172	### 7.6.1 The authority of morality: a rationalist view 184
173	Rational moral judgments weigh self-interest among other factors, just as rational prudential judgments weigh present inclination among other factors. Consequently, morality takes precedence over prudence, and prudence takes precedence over current desire.
174	### 7.6.2 The arbitrariness of morality on the Humean view 187
175	For Humeans, moral imperatives stem from an arbitrarily selected subset of our desires with no more reason-giving force than any other desires.
176	### 7.6.3 The problem of weakness of will 188
177	Rationalists can make room for weakness of will and free will, while Humeans cannot.
178	### 7.6.4 How anti-realism undermines morality 192
179	Anti-realism undermines moral beliefs and moral motivation. It teaches us that we have no impartial motives for action and that there is no reason to act morally when this does not satisfy our own desires.
180	7.7 Why be moral? 196
181	On one interpretation, ‘Why be moral?’ is a nonsense question. The nature of moral evaluation makes moral action inherently rational. The question ‘Why be rational?’ is nonsensical.
182	8 FURTHER OBJECTIONS 199
183	8.1 The argument from weirdness 199
184	Mackie objects to moral facts on grounds of their ‘queerness’. But on no obvious interpretation is it true both that moral facts would be queer and that this queerness constitutes evidence against their existence.
185	*8.2 Troubles with supervenience 202
186	The evaluative properties of a thing seem to depend on its non-evaluative properties. Some find this relationship problematic.
187	*8.2.1 Mackie’s objection 202
188	Mackie finds the relation queer. This has no more force than his general argument from queerness.
189	*8.2.2 Objections to the notion of prima facie rightness 203
190	Strawson finds the notion of a type of action’s necessarily having a tendency to be right incoherent. Examples involving forces in physics and good moves in chess demonstrate the notion’s coherence.
191	*8.2.3 Blackburn’s objection 206
192	Blackburn finds it puzzling that the general principle of the supervenience of the ethical is analytic but that no particular ethical theory is analytic. I show that realists are better able to explain this alleged datum than Blackburn himself is.
193	*8.2.4 Jackson’s objection 207
194	Jackson raises a puzzle about why ‘good’ would refer to a non-natural evaluative property, even if such a property existed, rather than to a disjunction of natural properties. But Jackson begs the question against intuitionism.
195	*8.2.5 An analogy: the supervenience of logical properties 208
196	The supervenience of value properties on non-evaluative properties is analogous to the supervenience of logical properties on semantic properties. Non-demonstrative arguments provide a particularly close analogy.
197	8.3 How can we understand 'good'? 209
198	The unobservability of goodness, together with disagreements about what is good, pose a problem for how children could first learn the meaning of 'good'.
199	8.4 Is intuitionism too subjective? 210
200	Some have misinterpreted intuitionism as a subjectivist doctrine. But moral knowledge is no more subjective, on the intuitionist account, than any other kind of knowledge.
201	8.5 Do my arguments prove too much? 211
202	Some arguments against moral anti-realism seem to apply also to anti-realism about funniness, coolness, and the like. But on reflection, a subjectivist account of the latter properties is more compelling than a subjectivist account of value.
203	8.6 Evolution and ethics 214
204	8.6.1 The evolutionary objection to realism 214
205	Sociobiological theories of the source of moral attitudes cast doubt on the belief that these attitudes track objective facts.
206	8.6.2 The realist's burden 215
207	Moral knowledge may be a by-product of reason. Moral realists need not argue that evolution predicts the existence of moral knowledge, but only that it fails to predict the absence of moral knowledge.
208	8.6.3 How good are evolutionary accounts of ethics? 217
209	Existing evolutionary accounts of ethics are unimpressive, both because of their failure to account for the details of our moral intuitions and beliefs, and because the flexibility of the paradigm makes evolutionary hypotheses difficult to falsify.
210	8.6.4 An evolutionary account of moral perception 218
211	Accurate moral perception might have survival value by virtue of its facilitating peaceful cooperation.
212	8.7 Is intuitionism too revisionary? 219
213	Intuitionists may have to reject large portions of conventional morality, leading to suspicions that they have misunderstood ordinary moral concepts. But it may be that most people are habitually confused about ethics. Furthermore, intuitionism can be interesting even if it involves a reinterpretation of moral discourse.
