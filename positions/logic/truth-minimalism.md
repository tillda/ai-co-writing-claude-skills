# Truth Minimalism

I favour minimalism about truth — but a *modest* minimalism. The equivalence schema gives the basic necessary principle governing the truth predicate. Whether it is also *sufficient* for everything we want a theory of truth to do is a further question, and one I leave partly open.

## The core claim

- The truth predicate is governed by the equivalence schema: `<p>` is true iff p; `<p>` is false iff not p. That is what "true" and "false" mean.
- "is true" exists for a logical purpose: to let us generalise over propositions ("everything she said is true", "every instance of excluded middle is true") and to ascribe attitudes to propositions we cannot identify ("what Oscar said is true"). It functions as a *de-nominaliser* — a device for putting sentence-content back together once grammar has forced it into a noun phrase.
- Truth is not a robust metaphysical property whose hidden essence awaits discovery. The question "what is truth?" trades on a misleading analogy with predicates like "is magnetic" or "is diabetic", which name properties with deep underlying structure. The truth predicate has no such structure to uncover.
- The basic theory of truth is the (infinite) set of instances of the schema. No finite, compositional, reductive analysis is needed to capture everything truth-talk does.

## What minimalism does *not* deny

It is easy to overstate the position. Minimalism is compatible with — and accommodates — most of the intuitions that drive rival theories:

- That truths are made true by features of reality. "`<Snow is white>` is true *because* snow is white" follows once the schema is combined with ordinary explanations of why snow is white. That is the defensible kernel of the correspondence intuition.
- That there are real relations between truth, reference, satisfaction, meaning, verification, and successful action. Minimalism doesn't deny these; it insists they belong to other theories (of reference, semantics, psychology) which combine with the schema to deliver the facts. The theory of truth is a theory of truth, not a grand theory of language and inquiry rolled together.
- That structural / compositional principles relating the truth of a whole to the semantic properties of its parts may well hold. They can be added as legitimate *extensions* of the minimal theory; what minimalism resists is treating them as part of what truth *is*.

The distinctive minimalist thesis is that none of this needs to be built into the theory of truth itself.

## The argumentative path

- **Correspondence theory** trades on the obscurity of the "fact" relation. Sophisticated versions either collapse into identity (a true proposition just *is* the corresponding fact, so "correspondence" reduces to triviality) or require an infinite battery of compositional axioms no easier to formulate than the equivalence schema itself. No methodological gain over minimalism, and a lot of metaphysical baggage.
- **Coherence theory** faces the fairy-tale and plurality objections: a maximally coherent set of beliefs can still be false, and many incompatible coherent systems can coexist. Coherence is a fallible epistemic test for truth, not its nature.
- **Pragmatism** conflates truth with utility. Useful falsehoods and useless truths are real cases that the identification mishandles. The link between truth and successful action is something to be *explained*, not stipulated in the definition.
- **Primitivism** ("truth is an unanalysable quality") is the resort of someone who has given up. It adds nothing to minimalism except mystery.

Each rival inflates the schema with extraneous content while still relying on its instances. Minimalism keeps only what the predicate actually needs to do its work.

## Honest objections to minimalism

These are the genuine worries, not strawmen.

1. **Adequacy worry.** Can an infinite list of biconditionals really explain everything we want to say about truth — its role in inquiry, its normative pull, the link between true belief and successful action? The standard reply is that the schema explains these *in combination with* theories of belief, action, and the rest. Whether that combinatorial story actually goes through for every case is an empirical question about explanatory completeness; it is not something the schema alone can settle.
2. **Incompleteness about relations.** The schema is silent on the connections between truth and verification, reference, validity, assertion. That silence is a feature only if separate, independently adequate theories of those phenomena are available. Where they aren't, minimalism has handed off the problem rather than solving it.
3. **Cumbersomeness.** No finite axiomatisation captures all instances of the schema. The minimalist concedes this and argues that every adequate theory of truth has the same feature (correspondence and coherence accounts equally need infinite resources once spelled out). Concession granted; but the resulting theory is, strictly, unformulable — only implicitly specifiable.
4. **No necessary and sufficient conditions.** Minimalism does not give an eliminative definition of "true". This is genuinely surrendered. The defence is that meaning-specifications for predicates need not take that form — the schema fixes the use of the predicate well enough for ordinary purposes. Whether "well enough" is enough is contested.
5. **Normative pull.** "Truth is the aim of belief / inquiry." Some find this normative role hard to recover from what looks like a mere disquotational device. The minimalist reply is that the norm flows from the schema plus facts about the role of belief, but critics reasonably suspect something is being papered over.

## The Liar

The natural worry: doesn't unrestricted use of the schema generate the Liar paradox, forcing minimalism to bolt on ad hoc restrictions? No. The schema applies to *propositions*, and the sentence "L: this sentence is false" fails to express one. A proposition is individuated by its truth conditions, and the proposition L is supposed to express would have to be true iff false — there is no such proposition. So L lacks determinate content; the relevant instance of the schema never gets off the ground because there is no `<p>` to feed in. The paradox is a defect of the sentence, not of the schema. Minimalism is undisturbed.

## Considered view

Go with minimalism, but a non-triumphalist version. The equivalence schema is the basic, indispensable principle governing the truth predicate — every rival theory either presupposes it or has to recover its instances, and that alone is a strong methodological reason to put it at the centre of the theory.

What I leave open:

- Compositional and referential principles relating truth to sentential structure can be legitimate extensions of the basic theory — supplements that hold true without constituting what truth is.
- Whether the schema-plus-other-theories programme genuinely discharges every explanatory demand is to be checked case by case, not declared in advance.

The right slogan is *deflationism with humility*. The metaphysical extravagance of correspondence and the epistemic over-reach of coherence are both wrong directions; the schema captures the predicate's essential job. But "essential" is not "exhaustive", and the theory of truth may need supplementation. Just not in the directions the rival theories propose.
