# Rule Utilitarianism Doesn't Solve the Counter-example Problem

Rule utilitarianism is the natural Mill-friendly escape from the act-utilitarian counter-examples (Organ Harvesting, Framing, deathbed Promise): follow the rules whose general adoption would produce the most utility, rather than maximising utility case by case. The proposal looks attractive on its face — surely a society of rule-followers who don't kill healthy patients does better than one whose doctors maximise case by case. But the proposal collapses under a clean trilemma. There is no stable middle position between act and rule utilitarianism that does the work the rule-utilitarian wants done.

The argument:

- **Trilemma horn 1: any imperative counts as a "rule".** If a rule can be any general imperative, then "always perform the act that maximises utility" qualifies. That rule, applied universally, by hypothesis produces the most utility (it is what produces the most utility on every occasion, by definition). So rule utilitarianism endorses it. So rule utilitarianism is equivalent to act utilitarianism. The escape from Organ Harvesting collapses on the first move.

- **Trilemma horn 2: rules may carry exception clauses.** If we stipulate that rules exclude the act-utilitarian principle but may otherwise carry exception clauses, then the rule "do not kill healthy patients except when their organs save five others and you will not be detected" produces more utility than the simple ban. Rule utilitarianism endorses it, and we are returned to Organ Harvesting. Any case the act-utilitarian endorses can be encoded as an exception clause on the relevant prohibition; the ability to encode any case as an exception is what gives the framework its power, and is also what makes it indistinguishable from act-utilitarianism in practice.

- **Trilemma horn 3: rules must be exceptionless.** If we forbid exception clauses to block horn 2, the resulting view rules absolutely. "Never kill" rules out killing in self-defence. "Never lie" rules out lying to a murderer at the door. "Never break a promise" rules out breaking a frivolous promise to save a life. Common-sense morality recognises some exceptions to general rules — the question is which. The exceptionless rule view recognises none.

- **No principled way to allow some exception clauses but not others.** The rule-utilitarian who wants to allow "do not kill except in self-defence" but ban "do not kill healthy patients except when five others would be saved" needs a principle for distinguishing acceptable from unacceptable exception clauses. The natural candidate — that the exception must reflect a feature that figures in our other moral judgments — is itself not a utilitarian criterion; it is an appeal to moral intuition or to deontological structure. So either the principle is utilitarian (in which case any utility-maximising exception clause is acceptable, and we are back at horn 2) or it is not (in which case rule utilitarianism is no longer a pure utilitarian theory).

- **What this means for Mill's text.** Mill is sometimes read as act-utilitarian and sometimes as rule-utilitarian; the *Utilitarianism* text supports both readings at different points (the proof leans act, the discussion of "secondary principles" leans rule). The lesson of the trilemma is that Mill cannot have it both ways. If the rule-utilitarian reading is to do real work, it must specify which horn it occupies — and each horn is bad in its own way. The act-utilitarian reading at least owns its commitments; the rule-utilitarian reading is a moving target.

- **Why I reject the rivals.** Pure act-utilitarianism bites the counter-example bullets. Pure absolutist rule-utilitarianism rules out self-defence and life-saving lies. The exception-clause variant is just act-utilitarianism in disguise. The honest conclusion is that the counter-example problem is not solved by relocating the utility calculation from acts to rules; it requires admitting agent-centred constraints, which is to leave utilitarianism behind.

**Usage:** Additive to a Mill essay on act vs. rule utilitarianism. Mill's text doesn't settle the question; the trilemma is the structural test that makes clear why. Use the trilemma to show that the choice between act and rule is not a choice between two utilitarianisms but between (a) act-utilitarianism and (b) something that either collapses into act-utilitarianism or stops being utilitarianism.
