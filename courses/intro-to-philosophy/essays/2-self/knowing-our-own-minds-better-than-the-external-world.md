# Knowing our own minds better than the external world

**Question:** Explain Descartes' argument that we know our own minds better than the external world. Is his argument plausible?
**Answer:** Descartes argues that the doubt that threatens external-world beliefs leaves the contents of the mind untouched, and so mental claims have a security that perceptual claims lack; the argument is plausible at the narrow level of present occurrent seemings, but it overreaches when applied to fuller knowledge of the self.

Descartes's argument that we know our own minds better than the external world is one of the most influential moves in modern epistemology. It runs through the Second Meditation, building on the foundation laid by the cogito, and presupposes the doubts of the First. In my view, the argument is right about something narrow and important, and wrong about something broader and more interesting. It captures a genuine asymmetry between immediate awareness of mental life and perceptual claims about the world. But the asymmetry, properly understood, is much narrower than the textbook gloss "we know our own minds better" suggests, and even within its proper range it does not carry the weight Descartes wants it to carry — particularly the weight of supporting substance dualism.

The argument has the following shape. Start with the result of the First Meditation. Perceptual beliefs — that there is paper in front of me, that I am sitting by the fire — admit a reason for doubt, since I might be dreaming, or being deceived. Beliefs about extension, shape, and number are also threatened, since the deceiving God or demon could make me err even there. So under the most extreme doubt, no external-world claim is safe.

Now turn inwards. Even if I am dreaming, even if a deceiver is at work, certain things are not under threat. That I exist, while I am thinking, cannot be made false. And the thinking itself — the having of thoughts, the seeming-to-see, the seeming-to-hear, the willing, the imagining — these cannot be doubted in the way external-world claims can. The deceiver might make me wrong about whether there really is a fire in front of me; he cannot make me wrong about whether it seems to me that there is a fire. The seeming is just the having of an experience with a certain content, and there is no further question about whether the experience answers to anything outside it. So the contents of mind, in this restricted sense, escape the doubt.

From this Descartes draws his epistemic asymmetry. The mind is known to its possessor in a way the external world is not. The external world is mediated, fallible, doubtable; the mind is direct, secure, undoubtable. Descartes goes further: not only can he know that he exists, he can know what he is — a thing that thinks, doubts, wills, imagines, and has sensory experiences in the restricted sense. The wax passage, which I will not labour, develops the same asymmetry by a different route: when Descartes attends to a piece of wax, what changes are its sensible properties, but what remains is what the mind grasps; so the wax is, on inspection, better known by the understanding than by the senses.

Is the argument plausible? I want to separate three claims it can be taken to make.

The first claim is that present occurrent seemings are not subject to the kind of doubt that perceptual claims are. This is true. If it seems to me that there is a fire, the seeming itself is something I am incorrigibly aware of; the dreaming hypothesis does not threaten the seeming, only the fire. The same goes for present-tense thinking, willing, imagining: their occurrence is something I cannot mistake while they are occurring. This narrow asymmetry is real, and Descartes deserves credit for identifying it.

The second claim is that the mind, taken more broadly, is better known than the external world. This is much less plausible. Knowledge of one's own mind in the broader sense — knowledge of one's beliefs, motivations, moods, character traits, dispositions — is not incorrigible. We make mistakes about what we believe; we discover, in retrospect, that what looked like generosity was vanity; we are sometimes wrong about whether we love someone or merely depend on them; we systematically mislabel our own emotions. None of this fits the model of unmediated, indubitable self-knowledge. The Freudian point that some mental contents are hidden from their owner is only the most dramatic version of a phenomenon any honest reflection confirms. Self-knowledge is hard work and partial.

The third claim is the strongest: that the mind is *epistemically prior* to the external world in a way that supports the metaphysical doctrine that mind and body are distinct substances. This is the use to which Descartes wants to put the asymmetry. It is a non-sequitur. The fact that I can know something about my own mental states without knowing how the external world is does not show that mental states are properties of a non-physical substance. The same epistemic asymmetry would be available to a thinking creature whose mental states were processes in a brain. The brain has privileged access to its own representational contents in a way it does not have to the world the contents represent. This is a feature of being a representational system, not evidence that the system is non-physical.

A defender of Descartes might reply that even granting these limitations, the narrow asymmetry — present occurrent seemings — is enough for the argument's purposes in the *Meditations*, since what Descartes needs is a foundation, and seemings provide one. This is fair. The cogito and the seemings together do constitute a foundation in a foundationalist epistemology. But two qualifications matter. First, the foundation is much narrower than "we know our own minds better than the external world" makes it sound. It includes what is happening *now*, in the form of *seeming*, not the larger structure of selfhood. Second, the foundation does not by itself support the move to substance dualism; that requires further argument the Second Meditation does not deliver and the later *Meditations* do not deliver convincingly.

There is a positive way of putting the result. Foundational justification flows from how things seem. If it seems to me that I am thinking, then the seeming is itself a piece of evidence I cannot properly ignore, and I am justified in believing that I am thinking. The same structure applies to other appearances — sensory, intellectual, mnemonic. Descartes's argument is, in effect, an early version of this view, restricted to a narrow class of mental seemings. Generalised, it gives us the right answer to the question of where justification comes from. Restricted, as Descartes uses it, it gives us a foundation that is real but narrower than he wants and does no work for the dualism it is meant to support.

So the argument is plausible only on the narrow reading. Present occurrent mental contents are known to their owner in a way that external-world claims are not. That is a genuine asymmetry. But once we move beyond the present and the occurrent — to my character, my motives, my settled beliefs, my essence — the asymmetry vanishes, and self-knowledge looks much like other knowledge: partial, fallible, sometimes available, sometimes not. The fashionable Cartesian conclusion that mind is more transparent to itself than the world is to it should be replaced by a more modest one: present occurrent mental states are incorrigibly known to their bearer; everything else is on roughly the same footing as external-world claims.
