# Proposition Minimalism

A proposition is the meaning of a sentence — what it expresses, when it expresses something. There is no need for a robust metaphysical theory of what propositions *are* beyond that. Sentences express propositions via the conventions of use that govern their words and grammar (see `wittgensteinian-conceptualism.md`). When the conventions successfully pick out determinate content, a proposition is expressed; when they fail (Liar, truth-teller, vague sentences in the borderline range, vacuous conditionals), no proposition is expressed.

This is the minimalist analogue of truth minimalism (see `truth-minimalism.md`). The T-schema is all there is to say about truth; the use-theoretic mechanism by which sentences express what they do is all there is to say about propositions. We do not need a substantive theory of either.

The argumentative path:

- **The roles propositions play.** Propositions are what sentences express, the bearers of truth and falsity, the objects of belief, what agreement is agreement *about*, what is shared by sentences in different languages with the same content. Any account has to vindicate these roles — but vindicating the roles is not the same as positing a robust metaphysics behind them.
- **Robust ontologies overcommit.** Frege's third realm of abstract Senses requires entities with mysterious connections to mind and language. Russell's structured propositions require concrete bearers as constituents and trip on empty names, fictional contexts, and substitution failures. Lewis-Stalnaker propositions as sets of possible worlds require possible-worlds realism or robust abstractionism (see `primitive-modality.md`), neither of which I accept. Each of these views adds a heavy metaphysical layer to do work that is already done by sentence-meaning plus the conventions of use.
- **The minimalist account.** A proposition is what a sentence expresses, when it expresses something. The mechanism is the use-theory of meaning: the conventions governing words pick out content; the syntactic combination yields a determinate proposition (when it does). There is no further question "what is a proposition?" to answer; the question is misshapen, just as "what is truth?" is misshapen on truth minimalism.
- **The deficient-meaning recipe applies.** When the conventions of use fail to pick out determinate content — Liar (over-constrained), truth-teller (under-constrained), vague sentences (mismatch with continuous reality), vacuous material conditionals (empty satisfaction) — no proposition is expressed. This is the same recipe used in `liars-paradox.md`, `sorites-paradox.md`, and `conditionals.md`. Propositions are robust enough to fail to be expressed in characteristic ways.
- **Eliminativism overshoots.** Quine's elimination of propositions in favour of sentences cannot capture inter-language agreement (a French speaker and an English speaker can believe the same thing) or the content of pre-linguistic mental states. Minimalism keeps propositions, just without the metaphysical excess.

Crucial qualification: this is a minimalist account, not an eliminativist one. Propositions exist — we talk about them, they bear truth, they are objects of belief. The position is only that we do not need to specify their nature beyond the use-theoretic mechanism by which they are expressed.

**Usage:** Foundational position. Use as the headline thesis when the prompt directly asks "what are propositions?" or "do propositions exist?" Use as theoretical background — one or two paragraphs, not the conclusion — when the argument depends on rejecting a presupposed substantive theory of propositions (e.g. when arguing that Liar, sorites, or vacuous conditionals fail to express propositions, this position licenses the move). Cross-reference truth minimalism: the two are structurally parallel, and citing the parallel reinforces both.
