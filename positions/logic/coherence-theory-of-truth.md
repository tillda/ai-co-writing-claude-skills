# Coherence Theory of Truth

I reject the coherence theory of truth as an account of what truth *is*. Coherence with a belief system is, at best, a fallible epistemic test for truth; it is not the nature of truth.

**A note on scope.** Keep separate (i) the coherence theory of *truth* — a metaphysical claim that truth = coherence with a belief system — from (ii) the coherence theory of *justification* — an epistemological claim about how beliefs become justified. The second is a serious position in contemporary epistemology and is not what I am rejecting here. My target is (i): the constitutive claim about what truth itself consists in.

## The strongest version of the view

The view to defeat is not the crudest one. The defensible version says:
- Truth is constituted (not merely tested) by membership in a maximally comprehensive, mutually supporting system of propositions.
- "Coherence" is more than mere consistency: it requires positive entailment relations, explanatory connections, and inferential support among the propositions of the system.
- The relevant system is typically idealised — not anyone's actual beliefs now, but an ideally extended, internally optimised system at the limit of inquiry.
- Truth may come in degrees: a proposition is more or less true to the extent that it figures in, or is supported by, such a system.

## Best contemporary arguments for the view

1. **Inaccessibility of correspondence.** We never step outside our belief system to compare a belief directly with the bare world. Whatever we take as evidence for correspondence is itself another belief. If truth must be the kind of thing we can in principle access, the only honest candidate left is coherence within the system we are stuck inside.
   Response: this is very close to the mistake of confusing truth for justification - if we step or not outside some belief system is irrelevant to what actually is true outside that (that is what predicate "true" means).

2. **Holism about meaning and justification.** The content of any single belief is fixed by its inferential relations to other beliefs; truth-conditions cannot be specified for an isolated proposition outside a network. If meaning is holistic, the property that tracks correctness of meaning — truth — should be holistic too.
    Response: again, confusing truth for justification. Properly formed (proposition expressing) sentences are immune to this.

3. **Obscurity of the correspondence relation.** Correspondence theorists owe an account of what relation a true sentence bears to "the facts". Every attempt either reduces to triviality ("P is true iff P") or invokes a "fact" relation that no one has illuminated. Coherence at least specifies a relation we can describe: inferential support among propositions.
    Response: this is true, but the equivalence schema does most of the heavy lifting regarding "correspondene" (=its minimalist version)

4. **Inquiry-as-practised looks coherentist.** Working scientists do not check beliefs one-by-one against the world; they test for fit with the rest of the corpus, prefer revisions that preserve more of the existing network, and treat radical incoherence as a defeater. If the actual practice of successful inquiry is coherence-driven, perhaps coherence is what truth amounts to.
    Response: again confusing truth for justification in a big way! How can so many philosophers make this mistake? (Verificationism, most of continental philosophy)

## Why I still reject the view

1. **Coherence and truth come apart.** Two complementary cases make this vivid. *Plurality:* for any maximally coherent system S, alternative maximally coherent systems S′, S″, … can be constructed that disagree with S on substantive matters; the theory either calls them all true (so contradictions are true) or appeals to something outside coherence — typically a tacit "which one matches the world" — to break the tie. *Fairy-tale:* an exhaustive, internally self-supporting body of Sherlock Holmes lore — every fact about his habits, his address, and his cases hanging together perfectly with every other — would on the theory be a set of true beliefs; Holmes would still not exist. Both show the same thing: coherence is silent on whether the system is anchored to reality, and truth is not.

2. **The disquotation objection.** Anyone who understands "true" in ordinary English accepts two trivial principles: "It is true that P" entails "P", and "P" entails "It is true that P". The coherence theory implies that "P" is biconditional with "P coheres with system S". Combining these gives "P iff P-coheres-with-S" — but the right-hand side is about relations among our beliefs, while the left-hand side is about the world. "Cats eat mice" is true because of cats and mice, not because of how our belief-set hangs together.

3. **The theory is underspecified.** Two open questions, neither resolved. *Which relation?* Mere consistency lets in too much (any consistent fiction). Strict logical entailment is too strong (real bodies of knowledge are not closed under entailment, and demanding it makes almost nothing cohere with anything). "Mutual support" or "explanatory unity" is vague enough that the theory becomes a placeholder for a relation no one has cashed out. *Whose system?* Mine collapses into private relativism. Ours faces the plurality objection across cultures and eras. The ideal-limit version makes truth-claims about today depend on facts about an inquiry that may never terminate, and that we have no grip on. Until both are settled, the view is not yet a theory; it is the promise of one.

4. **Self-undermining.** The coherence theory is itself a metaphysical thesis about what truth is. If correct, it is correct only insofar as it coheres with our overall belief system. But "the coherence theory of truth is correct" notoriously fails to cohere with most of what we believe about how language, science, and ordinary discourse work. By its own lights it is at best low-grade, perhaps not true at all.

5. **Conflating test with nature.** The strongest pro-coherence considerations — that we revise to preserve fit, that successful inquiry is holistic, that we cannot step outside our beliefs — show only that coherence is a useful epistemic *indicator* of truth. None of them shows that truth is *constituted* by coherence. The coherence theorist needs the strong constitutive claim but only earns the weaker evidential one. That gap is the whole disagreement.

## Considered view

Coherence is a fallible test for truth and a respectable standard for justification. As a theory of what truth itself *is*, it confuses our route to truth with truth itself. The minimal account — `<p>` is true iff p — captures everything the truth predicate actually does in our language without inviting the plurality, fairy-tale, or specification problems. If pressed for a metaphysics behind the disquotational schema, I lean to a deflated correspondence: truth requires that things be the way the proposition says they are, and that requirement is settled at the world, not at the belief system.
