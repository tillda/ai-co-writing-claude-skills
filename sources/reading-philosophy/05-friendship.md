# 5 Friendship

## Introduction to the Problem

Friendship is a central part of human life, but certain aspects of it can be hard to make good sense of philosophically. One reason for this is that philosophers tend to approach their topics in an abstract and even 'impersonal' way, whereas friendship is highly specific and personal. While it may be possible to love or care about humankind or people 'in general', friendship is necessarily a relation to an individual person, and moreover it's impossible to stand in this relation unless that other person has a special sort of care for you as well. This specificity makes it difficult to explain the source or ground of friendship in a way that makes our particular friendships seem justified or well motivated.

To see this, imagine a person who is a 'qualitative duplicate' of one of your friends: this person is not that friend, but is like her in all their evident characteristics. If we suppose, as is natural, that you care about your friend because of what she is like (perhaps because she is funny, kind, generous, and so on), then this suggests that you would also care in the same way about her duplicate, who shares all these admirable traits. Yet that suggestion is incredible. Of course you haven't met this duplicate of your friend, but we don't believe that if you did, you'd immediately want to be friends with her just because she has all the characteristics that attract you to this other friend. Nor do you see your friend as replaceable or 'fungible': you would regard it as a loss if your friend were somehow to be replaced by such a duplicate, since even though that person would share all the characteristics you admire in your friend, she would still be a different person from her, and thus not immediately an object of the same kind of affection. In these ways and others there seems to be something contingent or 'chancy' about friendship: to some extent it just happens that we are friends with certain people, and not with others, and this situation can't be fully explained in any principled or rule-governed way.

This last aspect of friendship is especially interesting when considered in connection with another of friendship's characteristics, namely the kind of 'partial' or unequal treatment we show towards friends as opposed to non-friends. To take a simple example, you will be more inclined to pick a friend up at the airport, or buy her a gift for her birthday, than you would be to do these things for someone who is not your friend. In addition, as Sarah Stroud argues in the second reading in this chapter, we seem inclined to believe the best about our friends, and in general to think about our friends in favourable ways in which we don't, in general, think about other people. But for the reasons just discussed, there may be nothing about our friends in virtue of which they deserve this treatment more than anyone else: we treat our friends in special ways, and think especially well about them, because they are our friends, and not because of any abstract considerations that could be entirely separated from this contingent fact. And that raises a puzzle: Is it just or fair that we treat our friends in these special ways? Is it reasonable that we tend to think better of friends than non-friends? Theories of justice or rationality that answered these questions negatively would seem very austere, and hard to square with the basic structure of human life. But that may not be enough to show those theories incorrect.


The readings in this chapter explore the nature of friendship and discuss its significance in ethics and epistemology (the philosophical study of knowledge). The first reading, by the Greek philosopher Aristotle, distinguishes three main forms of friendship, and identifies a particular kind of love or attraction that he thinks is the source of each one. Though Aristotle thinks friendship is a response to the good or attractive features in a person, this observation is not enough to resolve the questions raised above about the 'chanciness' of friendship, since as we have seen these features would not justify having that person, rather than someone else just like them, as a friend. Aristotle does not address this question directly, but the first part of the commentary on his text suggests how he might think about it: Aristotle denies that the goodness of a human life can be understood in terms of abstract rules or principles, and his account begins instead with an exploration of what makes human beings happy, which may of course turn on many contingent or chancy matters.

The second reading, by Sarah Stroud, concerns the tendency described above to be biased or 'partial' in our beliefs about our friends, and in the way we evaluate evidence about our friends' character and behaviour. Stroud argues that being a good friend requires this sort of partiality, and that this puts friendship at odds with the understanding of rationality offered in most philosophical theories. She then considers how to address this tension: should we favour rationality over friendship, or friendship over rationality, or is there some way to respect the bounds of each?

## Introduction to Aristotle

Aristotle (384–322 BC) was a Greek philosopher and a student of Plato. He is surely one of the greatest and most influential philosophers, and his ideas had enormous influence on philosophical and scientific thinking in Antiquity, the Middle Ages, and the Renaissance. He wrote dozens of books in many different areas of philosophy and science, ranging from ethics and political theory to metaphysics, logic, aesthetics, rhetoric, and even astronomy and biology. These writings continue to be studied and discussed by scholars working in all areas of philosophy.

The extracts in this chapter come from Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics, his most important work of ethical theory. (It probably has this title because the text was edited by Aristotle's son Nicomachus.) The commentary explores the overall orientation of Aristotle's ethics, especially as developed in this work. Our extracts come from one of two chapters of the Nicomachean Ethics where Aristotle discusses the nature of friendship, and explores its place in a happy and fulfilling human life. In reading the text, you should bear in mind that the Greek word philia, translated here as 'friendship', has a broader meaning than the latter term usually has in contemporary English: philia encompasses not just voluntary relationships between peers, but also familial relationships like those between husband and wife and parent and child, as well as everyday relationships like those between business partners or neighbours. To be a 'friend' in this broad sense is, as Aristotle puts it, to 'wish well' to another person – that is, to care about that person's happiness. A friendship, then, is any relationship in which people wish each other well in this way, and are both aware of this mutual goodwill.

## Commentary on Aristotle

It may seem surprising that a philosophical book on ethics would contain an extended discussion of friendship. One reason for this surprise is that we tend to think of ethics as concerned primarily with moral rules; or with the nature and extent of our rights, duties, and obligations; or what makes it right or wrong to act in various ways. This is not the only philosophical conception of ethics, however, and it is not one shared by Aristotle. In his Nicomachean Ethics, Aristotle's main aim is to give an account of what he calls the 'good life' for human beings, or the kind of life that allows a person to attain the thing that Aristotle claims everyone is really after, namely happiness. The word Aristotle uses that is translated as 'happiness' is the Greek eudaimonia, literally 'living in a way well favoured by a god', but Aristotle does not take this religious etymology very seriously. Nor does he think this happiness is just a matter of feeling happy, though good or pleasant feelings are evidently part of it: Aristotle agrees that a person cannot be happy if he or she feels miserable or uncomfortable all the time, but denies that merely feeling joy or other pleasant emotions is enough to be truly happy. What happiness involves, for Aristotle, is flourishing or living well in the ways that human beings are meant to, and enjoying the good feelings that result from this.

In developing this concept of flourishing or living well, Aristotle draws deliberate parallels between human beings and living things of other kinds: we can tell that there is such a thing as real human flourishing, he argues, because other animals, and plants as well, can flourish or fail to flourish based on how their lives go. Understood in this broad way, flourishing requires such things as good health, proper nourishment, and an environment that allows the development of one's natural capacities. All these things will take different forms in different species (the health of a tree is different from that of a horse, and they are nourished in very different ways, etc.), but there is some structure in common to them all.

Though Aristotle's ethical theory isn't, therefore, primarily about formulating moral rules or evaluating the rightness and wrongness of actions, it isn't as if it has nothing to say about these matters. This is because action is a part of human life in a way that it isn't part of the life of, say, a plant; and moreover human action can be good or bad – can contribute or fail to contribute to our flourishing – in a wider range of ways than the actions of non-human animals. What Aristotle calls the ‘virtues’ (literally, ‘excellences’) that contribute to a good human life thus include what philosophers today call ‘moral’ virtues such as courage, justice, honesty, and generosity, all of which characterize the goodness and badness of the ways humans act.


Seen against the background of this sort of ethical theory, Aristotle's concern with friendship as an ethical topic should make more sense. His aim is not primarily to explain the rules or moral principles governing friendship and friendly behaviour. Rather, as he suggests in the first chapter of this reading, Aristotle's concern with friendship is part of his concern with virtue, and thus with what it takes to be happy in the course of a good human life. And it seems obvious, as he says at the start of the reading, that friendship is a part of this: all people, and many non-human creatures as well (he mentions birds specifically), seek friendship in a wide variety of circumstances, and are unhappy if they cannot obtain it. (Remember what was said in the introduction to this reading about the broad meaning of ‘friendship’ in Aristotle's sense.) Given the assumption that friendship is part of human flourishing, Aristotle's question then becomes: What is friendship, exactly, and how does it contribute to a good human life?

Aristotle's answer to this question begins in Chapter 2, in the passage marked  . Here, he identifies what he thinks are the three most important characteristics of friendship. To do this, in each case he notes a way that friendship between human beings is different from other forms of love. (Here it is worth noting that the word translated in our text as 'love' is the Greek verb *philein*, which has the same root as *philia*, or 'friendship', and so it lacks the sexual and romantic connotations of the ordinary English word.)

What are the three characteristics of friendship that Aristotle identifies in Chapter 2?

First, friendship involves not just loving another person in the way one might love a TV show or a glass of wine, but loving a person and wishing them well – that is, caring about that person's own happiness and fulfilment, or having what Aristotle calls 'goodwill' towards him or her. Second, in friendship this goodwill is mutual: two people cannot be friends unless each of them cares about the good of the other. And third, this mutual goodwill must be recognized by each person: that is, it's not enough for friendship that two people bear goodwill towards each other; in addition, each friend must be aware that this is the case.

Beginning in Chapter 3, Aristotle builds on this original definition by distinguishing three different forms of friendship, the first two of which (described in the passage marked  ) he takes to be less 'perfect' or 'complete' than the third (described in ).

The three kinds of friendship Aristotle discusses correspond to three kinds of good or lovable things that he distinguishes earlier in the Nicomachean Ethics. First, things can be loved because of the way they are pleasant, or the way we enjoy what they are like. (An example of this would be the enjoyment of a fine meal, a funny movie, or a concert.) Second, things can be loved because of the way they are useful, or the way they enable us to obtain other things we love. (For example, a person might value having a reservation at a restaurant, or a ticket to a movie or a concert, because they look forward to the enjoyable activities this makes possible.) And finally, things can be loved because they are good 'in themselves', independent of what further desirable things they are related to. (Examples of this would be health and happiness.) Importantly, for a thing to be good 'in itself' in this third way does not rule out its also being pleasant or useful; the point is just that this is not what makes the thing good, and is not the main reason why it is loved.

Since being a friend to someone involves loving them in some way, Aristotle's three kinds of friendship correspond to these three ways he thinks things can be loved. First, two people might be friends because of their 'utility', or the ways that they are useful or beneficial to one another. Second, a friendship might be based in pleasure or enjoyment, as when two friends find each other funny, or good-looking, or simply pleasant to be around, and so on. And third, in what Aristotle describes at $\boxed{\mathrm{C}}\rightarrow$ as the 'perfect' and 'most true' form of friendship, friends might love one another just because of each other's goodness. Below we'll reflect more on these forms of friendship, including why Aristotle thinks the third one is superior to the other two.


To begin, we should note that when Aristotle describes certain forms of friendship as based in pleasure or utility, he does not mean that these friendships are exploitative or entirely self-serving. After all, as we saw in his Chapter 2, Aristotle thinks that friendship involves mutual love, and the relevant form of love is a concern for someone else's good – more specifically, a concern for 'what is good for [the friend's] sake'. So there cannot be a friendship if either party cares about the other only as a means to his or her own self-interested happiness. Rather, Aristotle's language of loving someone 'because of pleasure' or 'because of utility' identifies the source of the friendship, but not what the friends primarily care about in their friendship: it might be because two people first find each other enjoyable, or are able to benefit from their relationship, that they then come to care in this further way for each other's own happiness, and thus come to have a friendship in Aristotle's sense.

Despite this clarification, it is still clear that Aristotle thinks friendships based in pleasure or utility are inferior in some important respects to the third, 'perfect' form of friendship he describes. His reason for this seems to be that even though these friendships involve mutual goodwill, these friends still wish each other well because of something other than who they themselves are. If you love someone because she is pleasant to be around, then while you may truly love that person, and not just the pleasure of being around her, still what you love about her is the pleasure of her company; and if this changes, so that you no longer find her pleasant to be around, then your love for her will lessen. The same goes for friendships based in utility. That is why Aristotle says these friendships are 'easily dissolved': people very often stop being pleasant or beneficial to one another, and so if characteristics like these are the basis of their friendship, the friendship may not survive these changes.

As we have seen, the passage marked  contrasts these first two kinds of friendship with a third, which Aristotle describes as the 'perfect' and 'most true' form of friendship. Once again, it's important to see that in characterizing the third form of friendship in these ways, Aristotle doesn't mean to say that the first two kinds of friendship are therefore bad, or not really forms of friendship after all. His claim is that they are less perfect forms of friendship than the third one; this doesn't mean that they aren't still very good, and an important part of a happy human life.

As Aristotle describes it in the passage marked  and then continuing to Chapter 4, this most perfect form of friendship has several important characteristics.

What are the characteristics of 'perfect' friendship that Aristotle identifies beginning at ?

For Aristotle, the main characteristics of perfect friendship seem to be the following:

(1) Perfect friendship is always between people who are good and virtuous. 

(2) Those who are most truly friends care about their friends' goodness and well-being for their (i.e. the friends') sake, and not just because they find their friends pleasant or beneficial. 

(3) These friendships are long-lasting – specifically, they last as long as the friends remain good, and Aristotle thinks that a person's being good is unlike being useful or pleasant in that it's in the nature of goodness to endure. 

(4) These friendships are useful, since each friend is good, and contributes to the happiness of the other. 

(5) These friendships are also pleasant, both because goodness itself is pleasant, and because people find pleasure in being around those who are similar to them. 

(6) And finally, as Aristotle says at the end of this chapter, these friendships are also rare and take time to develop.

The last five characteristics on this list should not be too surprising given what we have already discussed, but the first may seem a bit puzzling.

Given what we have discussed so far about Aristotle's ethical theory and his conception of friendship, what do you think are some reasons he might have had for thinking that perfect friendship is possible only between virtuous people?

A full exploration of this question would be too much for us here, but here are some ways we might try to answer it. First, Aristotle thinks that friendship is based in love, and love is a response to something that a person finds lovable, and therefore good, in another. As we have seen, he distinguishes three ways in which we find things lovable: a thing may be loved because it is pleasant or enjoyable, because it is useful or beneficial, or because it is good in itself. This perfect form of friendship involves a love for a person as she is in herself, and not just the ways she is pleasant or beneficial to oneself. This means that to the extent that a person isn't herself good, she isn't the proper object of this sort of friendship – to love such a person 'as she is in herself' would involve a kind of mistake or illusion, since she isn't herself good, and so isn't herself deserving of this kind of love. Second, it seems that in order to have this sort of love for a good or virtuous person one must also be good oneself, as someone with a bad moral character is likely to be repelled by, rather than attracted to, a person who exhibits the moral virtues, except insofar as that person's virtues are somehow useful or pleasant. And third, since this form of friendship involves caring about someone else's well-being for that person's sake, it involves a kind of 'selflessness' that requires a degree of moral virtue: not because perfect friendship is a matter of altruism or self-sacrifice, but because someone who is too self-centred or governed by moral vices might not be able to prioritize another person's well-being in this way. Certainly there is more that could be said here, but that is one way to think of Aristotle's position.

Do you agree with Aristotle that 'perfect' friendship is possible only between the morally virtuous? If so, how would you defend this idea? If not, how would you challenge it? Take the time to write out some considerations that could be raised on either side of this issue, and think of how a philosopher might respond to them.

Aristotle's description of these three forms of friendship continues into his Chapter 4, and includes an interesting claim that is marked in our text, which should be noted here because it will be the focus of the next reading in this chapter. Aristotle claims that perfect friendship, because it is based in recognition of a person's virtue, is 'proof against slander' – that is, he thinks that loving someone in this particular way will be a reason not to believe bad things that other people say about the friend. Moreover, he suggests, something like this is demanded by friendship of this sort; that is, a person cannot really be a true friend to another without trusting that person, and being disposed to take this person's side against what is said about them by others. Having noted this claim, we can raise two questions about it, which we will consider in more detail in connection with the next reading:


1. Do you agree with Aristotle that it is a ‘demand’ or ‘requirement’ of true friendship that you be inclined not to believe bad things that are said about your friend by other people?

2. If you do agree with this, then what do you think explains this demand, and how do you think it relates to the more general demand to form our beliefs in ways that are guided by reason and evidence?

## Introduction to Stroud

Sarah Stroud is Associate Professor in the Department of Philosophy at McGill University in Montreal. The focus of her research is in ethics, moral psychology, and the philosophy of action. In this essay, Stroud offers a moral analysis of friendship, especially the ways in which friendship influences the beliefs we form about our friends and the ways we respond to evidence about their character. She argues that it is constitutive of friendship to be ‘partial’ in our beliefs about our friends; that is, a good friend will tend to believe good things about his or her friends, and disbelieve bad things about them, simply because of their friendship. (We saw Aristotle refer to a similar idea in Chapter 4 of the extract from the Nicomachean Ethics.) Yet as Stroud explains, mainstream theories in epistemology have tended to think that belief should always be formed impartially. At the end of her essay, Stroud suggests three ways philosophers could respond to this apparent tension between the demands of friendship and those of reasonable belief.

## Sarah Stroud, ‘Epistemic Partiality in Friendship’ (extracts)

> Analytic moral philosophers have been taking a renewed interest in friendship in recent years. They have been especially liable to consider friendship in connection with debates over partiality and impartiality in ethics. Many important styles of ethical theory hold up impartiality and equal treatment—under one or another interpretation—as central moral concepts and ideals. Yet friendship and other close relations between persons seem to involve partiality and differential treatment. We care more about what befalls our friends than about what happens to strangers, and we are more motivated to advance our friends’ interests than those of strangers. We seem even to have special responsibilities toward our friends which we don’t have toward strangers. If friendship necessarily involves such partiality, then there is a tension, at least, between the constitutive elements and dispositions of friendship and those of morality: they seem to pull in opposite directions.
>
>
>
> Contemporary moral theorists have responded to this tension in a variety of ways. Some have sought to bring the apparent partiality of friendship under the wing of a more fundamental impartiality, thus reconciling friendship and the moral. Others have insisted on the primacy of friendship, and partiality, over impartial moral conceptions—either to the detriment of particular moral theories, such as utilitarianism, or against the claims of morality in general. This article does not directly contribute to those debates about the relation between friendship and morality. Rather, it pursues an analogous yet, so far as I know, novel line of inquiry, drawing attention to a type of partiality which has gone virtually unmentioned in that body of literature and which I believe has interesting implications of its own. I shall argue here that friendship involves not just affective or motivational partiality but *epistemic* partiality. Friendship places demands not just on our feelings or our motivations, but on our *beliefs* and our methods of forming beliefs. I shall also argue, however, that this epistemic partiality is contrary to the standards of epistemic responsibility and justification held up by mainstream epistemological theories. We may thus have a further interesting problem on our hands. What if friendship goes against not just our moral ideals, but our epistemic ideals? […]

### I

> Let me begin with a methodological plea for some phenomenological analysis. It is best, I think, to start by really attending to friendship, its features and its texture, before we consider its relation to other things, such as impartial moral (or epistemic) demands. We should first try to excavate the distinctive demands of friendship from the inside, as it were, by conducting an unprejudiced inquiry into the nature of that relation and what it involves and requires. With that analysis on the table, we can subsequently ask how friendship stands from various evaluative perspectives, such as those of morality and epistemology, but the two parts of our inquiry should be conducted independently to the extent possible. This sequential way of proceeding, I believe, is preferable to taking as our very point of departure a comparison of friendship with one of those other modes of normative assessment. If we take that approach, we are likely to end up with a less detailed and nuanced picture of friendship than we otherwise might. Worse, our theoretical commitments in that other domain may creep in and corrupt the picture we paint of friendship. So I propose that we put blinkers on now and take a close look at friendship, postponing any worries about the epistemological (or moral) status of what we may find. […]
>
> That friendship imposes special demands on our actions is, of course, well recognized. [...] That friendship demands particular patterns of emotion and motivation [...] is now also common ground in discussions of friendship and its relation to morality. However, it has not been similarly recognized, or underlined, that friendship also places distinctive demands on our beliefs and our belief-forming procedures. That is what I want to bring out in this article: the cognitive dimension of friendship's demands.

![img-0.jpeg](_images-ocr/chapter5/p12-img-0.jpeg)

> In order to bring this element of friendship to light, I want to approach the demands of friendship from an unusual perspective. I would like to highlight the demands which hold on you, qua friend, in your interactions with third parties. (It is, I think, a neglected insight that the demands of friendship extend beyond your conduct vis-à-vis your friend and importantly include how you act toward third parties when your friends are at issue.) It is commonly accepted that loyalty is one of the constitutive elements of friendship. One particular aspect of that loyalty, it seems, is sticking up for your friend. If someone pokes fun at one of your friends, or makes a joke at his expense, or says something false or misleading (and derogatory) about him, it seems to be your responsibility as a good friend not to join in the fun; not even simply to remain silent and withhold comment; but, on the contrary, to stick up for your friend. One might say you owe it to your friend to defend him and his reputation in the court of public opinion. The behavior of someone who simply sits silent and uneasy while others are indulging in cruel gossip about Miss X is not that of a true friend of Miss X: it is disloyal to join in the joke, or to let the false accusation pass unchallenged. There thus seems to be a demand of friendship to stick up for your friend. [...]

> The demand of friendship to which I've just drawn attention has to do with how a true friend acts in situations like these. But I now want to suggest that an analogous demand holds in the domain of belief. Suppose again that someone tells a damning story about a friend of yours. Your friend appears in a bad light in this tale; he is portrayed as having acted badly, even disreputably. The speaker is clearly rather shocked and disapproving of your friend's behavior; she has obviously formed an unfavorable judgment of your friend's actions and perhaps even his overall character. And it must be confessed that your friend comes off looking rather bad from the information conveyed. We need not suppose that this is one of the cataclysmic cases occasionally discussed in the literature in which your friend is accused of espionage or murder. Instead, let's imagine that the speaker is attributing to your friend more everyday sins. For added verisimilitude, we can suppose that the allegations are of disreputable behavior in a context in which people often act badly: when sex is concerned, say, or when professional competitiveness is in play. Suppose, for instance, that a third party reports that your friend Sam recently slept with someone and then cruelly never returned any of that person's calls, knowingly breaking that person's heart. Importantly, though, in contrast with the examples of sticking up for your friend to which I alluded before, let's stipulate that this story is not something which you know to be false. Rather, what is being presented to you is new information about your friend.
>
> The question I now want to pose is, how, as a good friend, should you respond to this new information? I don't mean what actions you should take in this situation, but rather what you should make of this new information. What conclusions should you draw from it? How should you update your set of beliefs about your friend's actions and character in light of it? We need to ask whether the fact that the person under discussion is your friend raises any special issues in this connection: whether, as a good friend, you ought to react differently to this information than you otherwise would, or than a detached observer would. I shall suggest an affirmative answer. Indeed, as we shall see more fully later, I would go so far as to say that you owe your friends something other than an impartial and disinterested review of the evidence where they are concerned. But let's start with the more modest claim.

![img-2.jpeg](_images-ocr/chapter5/p13-img-2.jpeg)

> I think a little introspection will confirm that as a matter of general tendency we do react differently to reports like these when they concern our friends. As I shall detail shortly, our ways of dealing with evidence seem systematically to shift when that evidence bears on the actions and character of our friends: we seem in a variety of respects to adopt differential epistemic practices when our friends are at issue.4 Furthermore, I want to claim not just that we do (as it happens) react differently in these ways, but that these differential doxastic practices are characteristic of—part of our intuitive picture of—the good friend. I do not intend the phenomena I am about to describe merely as interesting empirical facts about how we in fact react when our friends are at issue, reactions which may be widespread but which are entirely optional from the point of view of friendship. Rather, to the extent that they are compelling at all, I think these phenomena present themselves as elements of our intuitive picture of the good friend, just as do certain patterns of motive and action.5 Just as a certain way of behaving concurs with our intuitive picture of the good friend, so also, I wish to claim, does a certain way of believing where our friends are concerned. A good friend does not defend her friend outwardly (which we earlier agreed a good friend would do) while inwardly believing the worst of her friend. We do not conceptualize the good friend as manifesting a split of this kind between behavior and belief when it comes to her friend. Rather, the good friend is prepared to take her friend's part both publicly and, as it were, internally. What I wish to suggest, in short, is that the differential doxastic practices of the good friend are a constitutive feature of friendship. [...]

> Let me now outline these differential responses, whose existence I think a little introspection will confirm. They can be divided into two broad categories. The first locus of difference is the cognitive activities we engage in when processing new data about our friends. What is distinctive in this domain is that we tend to devote more energy to defeating or minimizing the impact of unfavorable data than we otherwise would. To start with, we are more liable to scrutinize and to question the evidence being presented than we otherwise would be; we spend more time and energy doing this than we otherwise would. For example, we are more likely to ask ourselves various questions about the person telling the story, the answers to which could discredit the evidence being presented. We might ask ourselves: Is this person generally accurate, fair-minded, and trustworthy? Is she malicious, either in general or toward my friend in particular? Is it somehow in her interest that this story be true, or be thought true? We will spend more mental energy generating and assessing such possible discrediting factors than we typically do when we hear gossip about someone who is not a friend (just think how rarely we do these things in those cases).
>
>
>
> Furthermore, we will go to greater lengths in the case of a friend to construct and to entertain alternative and less damning interpretations of the reported conduct than we would for a nonfriend. This takes effort: these alternative explanations may not be obvious, and we may need to devote considerable mental resources to working them up and considering their merits. In the case of a nonfriend, we would be unlikely even to devote the time and energy necessary to develop these other options and put them on the table. In these various ways, then, we deploy a different or at least a heightened array of cognitive activities in response to unfavorable information which concerns our friends.
>
> i→ In addition to these differences of method, friendship also yields different doxastic outcomes: different beliefs. I want to claim that not just the cognitive procedures we deploy in assessing evidence but the set of beliefs we end up with will systematically differ when the subject of the story is our friend, and in particular that the latter will be more favorable than they otherwise would be. This is the second locus of the differential epistemic practices connected with friendship: where our friends are concerned, we draw different conclusions and make different inferences than we otherwise would (or than a detached observer would). I noted above that we spend more energy generating, that is, coming up with, alternative—and less damning—explanations of the reported conduct when the story we're told is about a friend. But that isn't all, for we are also likely to give such alternative constructions greater credence than we would for a nonfriend. And at the end of the day we are simply less likely to conclude that our friend acted disreputably, or that he is a bad person, than we would be in the case of a nonfriend. Friendship seems to alter not just the procedures we use to process new information but the conclusions we end up drawing.
>
> Let us now look more closely at these differential epistemic strategies and doxastic outcomes. Perhaps some slightly more fine-grained phenomenology will bring out their scope—for it is important to do this before we proceed further. We should ask: Do these distinctive epistemic responses take the form of willfully believing the false, or denying the incontrovertible, when it comes to our friends? Do they (or must they) involve a total imperviousness to damning evidence concerning our friends' conduct? Intuition says 'no.' What seems to
>
> |  be characteristic of the good friend is not a stubborn denial of obvious, incontrovertible facts about her friend but something more subtle—which we should now try to bring out. To that end, let’s remember that we are talking here about information that paints your friend’s actions and character in a bad light. Human actions, however—and a fortiori people’s characters—are not transparent. They require interpretation, and are open to various construals and contextualizations. They can be seen in different lights, put in different perspectives, filed under different labels and concepts. (For example, one person might classify as ‘refreshingly forthright’ behavior that someone else would simply call ‘obnoxious.’) A person’s actions can be placed in different patterns, and linked to different underlying character traits. (Anna might see Bernie’s behavior as demonstrating his spirit of adventure, while Carol finds it further illustration of his self-destructive streak.) And even when two people describe or classify someone’s action in the same way, or attribute the same character trait to him, they may nonetheless attach different degrees of importance to those attributions. That is, the judgments on which they agree may be more or less dominant in their respective overall impressions of the action or the person. So even when Dominic and Emily agree that Fiona’s comment was obnoxious, Dominic may attach a great deal of weight to that negative verdict, whereas Emily sees it as decisively outweighed by the fact that the comment in question served effectively to puncture Guy’s pretensions. Carol may see Bernie’s self-destructive streak as a minor detail, whereas for Hannah it is the most salient—and scary—thing about him.Beyond the incontrovertible, then, many interpretive avenues open up. And ‘the proper bias of a close friend’^{6} finds a fertile field of operations in this rather murky and uncharted terrain. What is characteristic of the good friend seems typically to come in at this level of interpreting the reported actions and placing them in perspective, rather than at the level of denying the base-level facts and events being related by the teller of the story.^{7} In general, a good friend is likely to interpret what she hears in a less damaging way than is a stranger. As we noted earlier, she is more likely to look for alternative interpretations to the obvious, and damning, ones and—furthermore—to draw different inferences about her friend’s character than would a stranger. This need not be a matter of flatly denying the obvious. It is rather a matter of extending more interpretive charity to your friends than you naturally would to strangers—of offering your friends more leeway. The result, though, is that your beliefs about your friends will be slanted in their favor in various respects.Let us see how this might go in a particular case. Recall the little story you were told about your friend Sam. Others who heard that story might conclude, ‘that certainly was insensitive’ (or even sadistic) or ‘what an inconsiderate jerk’ (or womanizer) or ‘I think he does it deliberately—the little devil positively *enjoys* breaking people’s hearts.’ As a good friend, however, your conclusions will likely differ. As a good friend, you will tend to file this incident—and Sam’s behavior generally—under other labels. Your reaction to this story might be, for instance, ‘There’s never any artifice with Sam. You know where you stand with him: if he doesn’t want to see you, he makes that clear. There’s no false politeness, no pussyfooting, no hypocrisy, no stringing you along—Sam’s too genuine for any of that.’ In a similar vein, what  | | --- | other people might classify as compulsive womanizing on Sam’s part, you might see as irrepressible but fickle enthusiasm and appetite for female charm in all its many varieties.
>
>
>
> These attributions pick up on the same data that other people are observing but put a different spin on them, highlighting certain aspects of Sam’s behavior and placing his actions in a different overall pattern. As we noted earlier, it is not that the good friend necessarily doubts or denies the purportedly raw data with which she is presented (although she’ll look for openings that might permit her to do this). The good friend is not blind to the data she receives about her friend, whether through direct observation or testimony. She sees the data, all right; she just puts a different cast on them. As the previous paragraph suggests, her distinctive doxastic stance toward her friend is signaled especially by the different *vocabulary* she will tend to use at the interpretive level. It is not as if the good friend uses the same concepts other people do and just sticks a negation sign in front of what they affirm. (Still less will she typically affirm the opposite of what they say, positively insisting that Sam acted with sensitivity and tact in that episode.) Rather, the bias of the good friend will normally take the form of casting what she sees or hears in a different light, shading it differently, placing it in a different optic, embedding it in a different overall portrait of her friend. Where our friends are concerned, in short, we become spin doctors. […]

Let me close this section by articulating two hypotheses about friendship which might help make it intelligible that we should adopt these differential epistemic practices where our friends are concerned. The background assumptions I am about to offer up constitute neither a complete nor an uncontroversial theory of friendship, but they may be useful all the same, in suggesting a broader framework within which the features I’ve tried to bring out would make sense.

> The first element of this background theory is that—following Aristotle—I am assuming that friendship is in some important sense based on your friend’s character and on esteem for his merits. We needn’t mean narrowly moral character, or specifically moral merits (Aristotle certainly wouldn’t have, given his broad conception of virtue), but I think some such constraint is required in order to respond to the common intuition that we want our friends to love us for who we are. On this conception of friendship, however, it makes sense that we need, as it were, to maintain a favorable opinion of our friend’s character. If friendship were in no way contingent on viewing one’s friend as a good person, the differential doxastic practices I’ve tried to bring out would look inexplicable, unmotivated: there would be nothing internal to the friendship relation that would make sense of our apparent tendency toward biased shading of data that concern our friend’s actions and character. But if, by contrast, friendship *is* importantly contingent on continued esteem for one’s friend’s merits and character, then it is not surprising that we would massage our beliefs about our friend’s character in a favorable direction and downplay any information which might threaten that esteem.
>
> The second remark I want to offer is that friendship is or involves a kind of commitment. Like other commitments, our friendships structure our deliberations, operating as (defeasible) fixed points or parameters within which we resolve the issues with which we are presented. For, in general, we don’t approach new situations and questions with a clean deliberative slate; we don’t resolve every issue from scratch. Quite the contrary: having commitments means not deciding every issue solely on its merits, if we mean by that dispassionately adjudicating an issue ‘from nowhere,’ as an objective and wholly unencumbered being would do. This can be seen by looking at a paradigm commitment, namely, a promise. If you have promised to do *x* at *t*, there is a clear sense in which you do not decide simply on the merits what to do at *t*. For whatever the merits of doing something other than *x* at *t*, your promise serves to constrain your deliberative field and thus to displace those merits from your deliberations. Similarly, our friendships function as commitments. To be someone’s friend is to have cast your lot in with his and, indeed, with his good character; and this properly affects how you respond to new situations and new data. Your friend need not prove each day, from scratch, that he is a good person: friendship is not contingent on being continually renewed by objective proofs of that proposition. A commitment to your friend’s merits is more something you bring to the various situations which confront you than something you take away from the information you receive. This is reflected in our epistemic partiality toward our friends. […]

### II

The phenomenological analysis of Section I suggested that the good friend manifests a kind of epistemic partiality or bias toward her friends. Her beliefs and her belief-forming practices seem to shift in her friends’ favor when her friends’ actions and character are at issue: she seems to be partial toward her friends in her epistemic comportment. I want now to ask two things: whether the good friend is indeed doxastically biased, or partial, and how we should evaluate the doxastic characteristics of the good friend from an epistemic point of view. […]

> [I]f we think back to the specific differential practices and beliefs the good friend adopts, they do not seem to be ones we could endorse from a purely epistemic point of view. Rather than being truth conducive, they seem to lead her into a distorted conception of reality. The fact is that most people act very badly from time to time, and have some more or less serious character flaws. So from an objective point of view your friends are very likely to possess these features as well. Friendship, however, seems to involve a reluctance (although not, as we noted, an absolute refusal) to come to such conclusions about your friends. In that sense friendship involves, if not a blind spot, at least less than perfect vision where your friends' sins and flaws are concerned; the good friend's set of beliefs is to that extent necessarily out of kilter. As a good friend, your belief set is slanted: you actually believe your own spin. [...]
>
>
>
> m→ In numerous respects, then, the doxastic stance of the good friend seems eminently subject to epistemic criticism. The good friend's comportment does not live up to our epistemic ideals, and may not even satisfy basic epistemic norms. Many of her beliefs about her friends appear epistemically unjustified or unwarranted—she does not have sufficient epistemic reasons to hold them—and her doxastic practices do not conform to the standard criteria for epistemically responsible believing or epistemic rationality. The beliefs associated with friendship thus appear to contravene the general standards articulated by mainstream epistemological theories. And we will of course find no exception for friendship in those theories: they do not propose different epistemological standards depending on the subject matter at issue. Rather, the canons of epistemically responsible belief formation are content invariable. We thus have a genuine clash between the demands of friendship and the demands of epistemology as understood by mainstream contemporary theories.
>
> n→ I shall take this conclusion as the springboard for the final part of the article, where I consider what we should do in light of this conflict. But before doing so, I need to forestall a challenge which, if successful, would eliminate the conflict altogether. For one could dispute the two claims I've made about the good friend's epistemic practices: that they genuinely constitute partiality or bias toward her friends, and that they contravene epistemological standards. One could argue that the good friend is merely applying general, impartial epistemological principles to the processing of new information about her friends, and that the conclusions she draws are in fact fully justified. Such a strategy, if successful, would vindicate the friend's doxastic stance in epistemic terms and cancel the charge of bias. For it would supply what seemed to be lacking: sufficient epistemic justification for what looks like a biased way of proceeding.
>
> The most obvious route to an epistemic justification of the good friend's distinctive ways of forming and updating beliefs about her friends would be to emphasize the large body of information and evidence that she possesses about her friends as the basis for her different conclusions concerning their conduct. This strategy sees the friend's apparently differential epistemic practices simply as reflections of the very different evidential stances she occupies vis-à-vis her friends on the one hand and nonfriends on the other. According to this argument, it is only right and proper, in evidentialist terms, that you should be so much slower to draw a negative conclusion concerning your friend than you would be about a stranger. For you have so much more evidence of your friend's good character, which must be weighed in the epistemic balance along with the more damning considerations which are now being presented to you. Your differing conclusions when your friends are at issue are perfectly epistemically legitimate, for they are merely applications of the evidentialist principle.
>
> Before explaining why I don't think this argument will ultimately succeed, let me give it all the credit it deserves. There are obviously differences in the prior informational and evidential bases that we can bring to bear when we hear something about a stranger and about a friend. After all, we know a lot about our friends—at least in comparison with people we don't know well. And it seems reasonable to think that those differences in information base can account for some of what might appear to be differential epistemic practices across the two types of cases—and, indeed, to hold that those differences sometimes make the friend's epistemic position superior. For the additional information we have about our friends undoubtedly sometimes provides us with opportunities for justified inferences concerning them which are not available to people who know our friends less well. Our extra knowledge sometimes allows us to draw a more accurate conclusion.
>
> Suppose, for instance, that someone disapprovingly reports that your friend left a party early without thanking the hosts. You may happen to know (because he is your good friend) that he had just received upsetting news—which fact places his behavior in a completely different light. In this case, your conclusions seem closer to the truth than those of your interlocutor; the additional information you possess seems to improve your epistemic position. And even when you don't know any further specific facts about the incident in question, as you did in this last example, you may possess general knowledge about your friend which can help to place his actions in a different light. Suppose you know that your friend's shyness around women whom he finds attractive takes the form of a seeming standoffishness and indifference. This information allows you to entertain a different, and more accurate, interpretation of what may have seemed to the woman in question to be a haughty brush-off.

![img-4.jpeg](_images-ocr/chapter5/p19-img-4.jpeg)

> So an appeal to differential information can certainly do some work in explaining and vindicating differences in the conclusions we draw about our friends. But I am very doubtful that it can do all the work required under the strategy we are now considering. For our differential epistemic practices where our friends are concerned seem not to track evidentialist principles but rather—at times—to run counter to the evidence. Those shifts in practice do not always seem to constitute an epistemic improvement. Think back to the particular phenomena limned in Section I. We noted that the good friend does not flatly believe the manifestly false, or refuse to believe the incontrovertible, about her friend. But her selection of conclusions and inferences seems, all the same, out of kilter with the objective weight of the considerations she considers. The good friend tends to seize on factors that would discredit negative evidence, to fasten on possible hypotheses that present her friend in a more favorable light. She withholds belief in propositions amply warranted by the evidence at hand and which would be natural inferences for a disinterested observer to make. So the present hypothesis does not fit the data very well. There is also a further problem for the evidentialist vindication of our beliefs about our friends. In order to justify resisting unfavorable construals of your friend's actions, you would presumably cite your knowledge of your friend's character as your basis for doing so. But think how much
>
> of that ‘knowledge’ of your friend’s character may already have been corrupted and slanted by the interpretive heuristics of the good friend. It is simply not clear that your beliefs about your friend’s character constitute evidence in a sense that supplies epistemic justification. […]
>
> Let us now consider the implications of the conflict between friendship and our epistemic standards with which we are presented. The arguments thus far—if successful—have, it seems, led us to the following conclusions. Friendship positively demands epistemic bias, understood as an epistemically unjustified departure from epistemic objectivity. Doxastic dispositions which violate the standards promulgated by mainstream epistemological theories are a constitutive feature of friendship. Or, to put the point as succinctly—and brutally—as possible, friendship requires epistemic irrationality.
>
> These are obviously uncomfortable and unwelcome results. They are particularly so because friendship seems to be an indispensable component of a good life. The importance and even necessity of friendship for a good life guarantees that each of us has very strong reasons to form and to have friendships. Conversely, if satisfying a certain set of demands or ideals would preclude friendship, it follows that we all have very strong reasons not to live up to those demands or ideals. That is why it is distressing even to contemplate the possibility that friendship is incompatible with some other important set of standards, be they moral or epistemic. In light of our overwhelming reasons for engaging in friendships, it seems those other ideals and demands, however much we cherish them, must come in second if they make friendship impossible. We have no realistic option but to pay the price of admission to friendship, however high that price might be from some other evaluative point of view. If there is a fight here, friendship must—and will—win.
>
> This suggests one possible moral we might draw from our present predicament: that rationality isn’t everything. If the canons of epistemic rationality, or the standards for justified belief, are incompatible with friendship, then—one might say—so much the worse for epistemic rationality or justified belief. If friendship constitutively involves epistemic irrationality—and, conversely, epistemic rationality precludes friendship—then we ought to opt for epistemic irrationality. According to this line, we have most reason overall to be epistemically irrational—at least in the context of a friendship. This response is perfectly coherent, for there is nothing contradictory in the idea that we have most reason overall to do something which is unacceptable from a particular evaluative standpoint. For example, if we arrived at the conclusion that the dispositions constitutive of friendship were incompatible with our moral ideals, one possible reaction would be, well, so much the worse for morality. This kind of response seems then to be available in both the epistemic domain, which is our concern here, and the moral domain.
>
> A second possible response, which also seems available in both domains, is more cautious. It says that given the clash in requirements which we have uncovered, there is simply no answer to the question of what you ought to do overall. There is what you ought to believe from an epistemic point of view, what you ought to believe as a friend, but no adjudication of those competing claims which gives us what you ought to believe simpliciter. This response is thus analogous to Sidgwick's view that there is a ‘dualism of practical reason’;^{11} it holds that the demands of friendship are simply incommensurable with those of epistemic rationality (or morality for that matter). These two reactions, as I noted, are options for both the epistemic version and the moral version of the conflict. What is interesting is that there is another response which seems attractive in the moral domain but which may seem unattractive, even dubiously available, in the epistemic case. I want to close by highlighting and then questioning this alleged asymmetry. Our present predicament, recall, stems from having found that dispositions condemned as irrational or unreasonable by standard epistemological theories are nonetheless essential ingredients of friendship. Because friendship is indispensable for a good life, it follows that complying with the standards promulgated by mainstream epistemological theories would preclude living a good life. We all, then, have most reason overall *not* to exemplify the epistemic ideals, or even to satisfy the epistemic requirements, which those theories propose. We must embrace what those theories consider to be epistemic irrationality. Notice, however, that it doesn’t follow that we must embrace epistemic irrationality. We’ve concluded only that we must embrace something which those theories classify as epistemic irrationality; and those theories could be wrong. […] If standard epistemological theories condemn as irrational something which is indispensable for a good life—so that we all have compelling reason *not* to comply with the demands of those theories—then perhaps we should question whether those theories offer an adequate account of epistemic rationality after all. Why accept a conception of epistemic rationality on which it is something which we have very strong reasons to avoid? It might be better to rethink the assumption that epistemic rationality requires the kind of epistemic objectivity or impartiality from which friendship seems necessarily to depart. To do this would be to adopt a less purist, more fluid approach to epistemological theorizing, on which epistemological theories are in principle open to criticism from data like these. As in the moral case, rather than concluding that friendship is epistemically irrational, we could instead conclude that our previous ideas of epistemic rationality were too narrow. I am sure, however, that this suggestion will be resisted, for in the epistemic domain the situation seems exactly the reverse of what we found in the moral domain. Most of us believe that moral theories are in some way answerable to what we have most reason to do overall, or to the possibility of living a good life. Most of us take considerations of these kinds to constrain the search for an acceptable account of morality's demands. Epistemological theorizing, however, initially seems more autonomous. We don't typically think of the search for the correct standards of epistemic virtue as being in any way constrained by considerations having to do with a good life. We don't normally view it as the business of an epistemological theory to be compatible with important human goods, such as friendship. In the epistemic domain, most theorists would embrace the 'ideal of purity,' holding that epistemology's concerns are specific, distinctive, and not to be corrupted by considerations stemming from the believer's interests or the pragmatic utility of holding a certain belief. Few, I think, would endorse the opposite view. Indeed, many would view it as only dubiously available in the epistemic context.
>
>
>
> If friendship proves to be incompatible with an otherwise compelling account of epistemic rationality, we don't feel the same pressure to revise our beliefs about epistemic rationality. But should our minds be closed to that option? If the epistemologist wishes to stand by his purist approach, he will have to relinquish any claim to overridingness for the epistemic standards he is busy formulating. Given the very strong reasons we have to engage in friendships (which by hypothesis contravene his standards), the epistemological theorist will have to concede that his standards are not authoritative with respect to what we ought to believe all things considered. This is a nontrivial price to pay: many epistemologists no doubt assume that in formulating standards for epistemic rationality they are limning the rationality of belief simpliciter. Furthermore, there might in fact be good reasons to think that mainstream epistemologies are too narrow in evaluating the friend's doxastic practices unfavorably. For example, if we take a leaf from the moral theorist's book and shift evaluative focal points, we might see some epistemic merit in the contribution that the good friend's beliefs make to social learning and to the accuracy of the social distribution of opinion concerning her friend. Like a defense lawyer, the friend who consistently advocates the more charitable hypothesis serves an important social epistemic function: without her input, negative views (which propagate rapidly through gossip) might become entrenched with little resistance, leading to a decrease in the overall accuracy of the social set of beliefs about her friend. A move to a social perspective could thus highlight recognizably epistemic values that transcend the narrower confines of evidentialism. But the epistemological purist, who feels no pressure to reconsider his epistemic standards in light of what he considers to be foreign considerations, might never even consider such a move.
>
> Let me close by saying that while I have not advocated any one response to the present predicament over others, I do think the problem sufficiently interesting that the full range of possible responses should be on the table for our consideration.

## Commentary on Stroud

In the opening paragraph of her essay, Stroud introduces the concept of ‘partiality’ that she will be focusing on. As she explains, partiality in this sense is the opposite of equal treatment – to be partial to a person is to treat him or her favourably, and differently than you might treat someone else. Following a number of other recent philosophers, Stroud notes that partiality seems to be an important characteristic of friendship: we treat our friends differently than we do other people who are not our friends, e.g. by confiding in our friends, giving them gifts, making efforts to spend time with them, and so on. Moreover, she says, it seems in some cases that there is a responsibility to be partial towards our friends in such ways, so that we would be acting badly, or in violation of a duty, if we were to treat our friends just as we treat strangers. (It would also seem bad, but in a different way, to treat strangers as though they were our friends.) Thus there is a tension between the partiality characteristic of friendship and the ‘impartiality and equal treatment’ recommended by many moral theories; unless the tension turns out to be unreal, then either those theories are mistaken or incomplete, or there is something unethical about friendship.

Think of an example of the kind of partiality in a person’s actions or feelings that seems like an ordinary characteristic of a healthy friendship, and of the kind of moral rule or principle that this partiality might be in tension with. What do you think is the right way to respond to this sort of tension?

As Stroud explains at [b] → in her second paragraph, the essay we are reading explores a tension similar to this one. She argues that the partiality characteristic of friendship is not limited to our actions and feelings, but includes some of our beliefs, and the methods by which those beliefs are formed. Specifically, as Stroud will argue in the next section, it is part of friendship that we tend to think the best of our friends, and find ways to think well of them even when the available evidence makes them look bad. Yet this ‘epistemic partiality’ appears to be in tension with most philosophical theories of knowledge and justification, which do not suggest that the way we form our beliefs should be affected by who the beliefs are about. (Note that the philosophical term ‘epistemic’, from the Greek episteme, means having to do with knowledge or understanding; and similarly the term ‘doxastic’, which Stroud will also use, comes from the Greek doxa, meaning belief or opinion.) This is the tension Stroud will discuss.

In the paragraph marked [c] → at the start of section I, Stroud makes a brief remark about the approach she will take in her essay. In calling her analysis of friendship ‘phenomenological’, she means that she is going to begin by describing friendship as a phenomenon – that is, as friendship appears to us ‘from the inside’, in our everyday interactions with friends and non-friends. Stroud wants to offer this analysis first, instead of beginning with an evaluation of friendship from the perspective of ethics or epistemology, so that her account of the nature of friendship will be as accurate as possible, and undistorted by prior philosophical commitments about what kind of actions are good or what kinds of beliefs are justified.


With this in mind, in the paragraph marked $\boxed{\mathrm{d}}$ Stroud begins her analysis of friendship. She starts by considering how the demand to be loyal to our friends means that we should respond when other people say bad things about them: when this happens, she says, you owe it to a friend to stick up for him or her, not merely to sit quietly but rather to stand up and defend your friend's reputation. And beginning at $\boxed{\mathrm{e}}$ in the next paragraph she asks whether this point can be extended further, such that our duties to our friends in circumstances like these are not just to say things in our friends' defence but also to think the best of our friends in the face of what others say about them.


Imagine that you hear a negative story about your friend, and don't know for sure whether it's true or false. What conclusion should you draw from the story? What should you believe about your friend, and about whether the story is true? Does it matter that the person in question is a friend of yours, as opposed to someone who is not your friend?

In the passage marked $\boxed{\mathrm{f}}$, Stroud begins answering these questions with what she calls the 'modest claim' that as a matter of fact, we do tend to form our beliefs differently in response to what we hear people say about our friends than to what we hear about those who are not our friends: as she says, 'our ways of dealing with evidence seem systematically to shift' when the actions and character of our friends are at issue. But this is not enough for Stroud's purposes, since she needs to show not just that we happen to believe the best about our friends, but also that doing this is important to friendship, because it's part of our 'intuitive picture' of what a good friend should do. That is what Stroud begins to argue at $\boxed{\mathrm{g}}$, where she claims that just as it's part of being a good friend to stick up publicly for your friends when others say bad things about them, the good friend should do the same sort of thing inwardly, forming her beliefs about her friends in a different way than she forms her beliefs about other people.


In the next few paragraphs, Stroud explains in greater detail two aspects of this epistemic partiality. The passage marked $\boxed{h ightarrow}$ discusses the first aspect, namely the distinctive 'cognitive activities', or kinds of *thinking*, we engage in when our friends are at issue. Then, beginning at $\boxed{i ightarrow}$, Stroud argues that these activities result in our forming different *beliefs* about our friends than we do about other people: due to the different ways we scrutinize evidence, consider alternative interpretations of the facts, and so on, we end up believing different things about our friends than we do about other people.

What is the difference between what Stroud calls 'cognitive activities' and what she calls 'doxastic outcomes'? Look at the text and commentary, then try to explain the difference in your own words. Once you've done this, think of a case from outside the domain of friendship where different cognitive activities, applied to the same information, might lead to different doxastic outcomes.

Beginning in the passage marked $\boxed{1 ightarrow}$, Stroud clarifies an important point in her account of epistemic partiality. It could seem from what she has said as if Stroud is arguing that people are supposed to be wilfully blind when it comes to the bad conduct of their friends, or totally impervious to whatever paints them in a bad light. But that is not her claim. Rather, as Stroud goes on to explain, the epistemic partiality characteristic of friendship is much less dramatic than this. It doesn't involve ignoring simple facts or denying what is obvious, but centres instead on subtle differences in interpretation of the sort that there is always room for when a person's character and conduct are at issue. According to Stroud, we tend to interpret facts about our friends in positive ways when this is possible, to apply favourable concepts to our friends, to focus more on their good characteristics than their bad ones, and so on. This is not, she thinks, any kind of deep irrationality or wilful blindness. Still, it is a kind of epistemic partiality, since we don't apply these same standards to those who are not our friends.

In the passages marked $\boxed{k ightarrow}$ and $\boxed{l ightarrow}$, Stroud relates her analysis of epistemic partiality in friendship to two other philosophical ideas which, she thinks, can help us make better sense of why we should be partial to our friends in this way. The first is drawn from the passage in Aristotle's *Nicomachean Ethics* that was the first reading in this chapter (see the passage in that reading marked $\boxed{d ightarrow}$): if, as Aristotle claimed, friendship arises as a response to your friend's good characteristics, then maintaining a friendship would require maintaining a positive view of your friend, and thus friendships will tend to be supported by the phenomena Stroud has described. The second idea is that friendship is a kind of commitment: much as once you have promised to do something you are thereby committed to do it, and so don't reconsider whether or not to do it unless there is some pressing reason to abandon your promise, so once you are a friend to someone you are thereby committed to think well of him or her, and don't reconsider this assessment without a very good reason.


Do you agree with these ideas about friendship? That is, do you think it correct that friendship is a response to a person's good characteristics, as Aristotle argued, or that friendship involves a kind of 'commitment'? And do you agree with Stroud that these ideas would help to explain why we are epistemically partial to our friends?

Section II of Stroud's paper discusses the implications of her phenomenological analysis of friendship for philosophical theories of knowledge and justified belief. She begins by highlighting an aspect of epistemic partiality that most epistemological theories would regard as unreasonable, namely that many of the beliefs and cognitive activities of a good friend will not be 'truth conducive'; that is, given that our friends are probably no less likely to act badly than anyone else, if we are disinclined to believe that our friends act badly, then some of our beliefs about our friends will tend to be false. We will, as she puts it, have a kind of 'blind spot' where our friends' defects are concerned, and thus an inaccurate conception of their character.

In the paragraph marked $\boxed{\mathrm{m}}$, Stroud spells out explicitly how these characteristics of friendship are in tension with the conceptions of rationality that are put forward by most philosophical theories of knowledge. A simple way to put the point is that philosophers assume that justified beliefs, and reasonable practices for forming beliefs, must be truth conducive, and must involve responding to the objective evidence for and against what we believe. Moreover, these theories do not suggest that there are exceptions to these principles when our beliefs concern our friends; rather, they assume that reasonableness is 'content invariable', such that there is no difference between the way we should form beliefs about our friends and the way we should form beliefs about anyone else.

Before discussing the ways she thinks philosophers can respond to her argument, Stroud considers an important objection beginning in the passage marked $\boxed{\mathrm{n}}$. Briefly, the objection is that instead of involving a kind of epistemic partiality that epistemological theories would criticize as irrational, our tendency to believe good things about our friends might just be a matter of responding rationally to all the evidence we have about their good character. That is, since you know your friends better than you know non-friends, and have more information about how they


tend to act, perhaps you are drawing on this information when you disregard the negative things that other people say about them. By contrast, with someone you don't know very well, you don't have this kind of background information to go on to counterbalance what you hear about them from others.

What is Stroud's response to this objection?

Though Stroud admits that our greater familiarity with our friends is certainly part of the story of why we are epistemically partial to them, in the paragraph marked $\boxed{\mathrm{O} ightarrow}$ she explains why she thinks this is not enough. This is that the cognitive activities described in section I are not just a matter of responding in an objective and evidence-based way to our prior knowledge of what our friends are like. Rather, as Stroud has argued, a good friend will tend to reason differently about her friends than about people who are not her friends, for example by giving information about a friend's good character greater weight than information about her bad traits, searching for alternative explanations for a friend's questionable behaviour, thinking of her friends with concepts that paint them in a good light, and so on. Moreover, Stroud points out that even our background 'knowledge' of our friends will have been coloured by our past partiality, so drawing on this information will still make us epistemically partial. Because of this, Stroud concludes that the objection is not wholly successful.


Do you agree with Stroud that our tendency to believe good things about our friends is not just a matter of our responding objectively to the large body of evidence we have about their good character? Think of some examples that support Stroud's position, and others that can be used to challenge it. How might Stroud respond to these challenging examples?

The last part of Stroud's essay discusses several ways philosophers might respond to the tension she has identified between the characteristics of a good friend and the conception of rationality recommended by mainstream epistemological theories. As she says in the passage marked $\boxed{\mathrm{P} ightarrow}$, Stroud does not think we can respond by rejecting friendship as unreasonable, or insisting that given the importance of being reasonable we should stop being epistemically partial towards our friends. She thinks this because, as she writes, friendship is too important in our lives, and epistemic partiality too much a part of what it is to be a good friend, for us to give up on these things for the sake of some abstract ideal of rationality. Because of this, Stroud thinks we must address the tension in some other way.


The three responses Stroud considers are in the passages marked $\boxed{q} ightarrow$, $\boxed{r} ightarrow$, and $\boxed{s} ightarrow$, respectively.

Read the passages marked $\boxed{q} \rightarrow \boxed{r} \rightarrow$, and $\boxed{s} \rightarrow$, and explain in your own words the three responses Stroud considers to the tension between friendship and epistemic rationality.

The first response (at $\boxed{\mathbf{q}}\rightarrow$) is the opposite of the one that Stroud began by rejecting: it accepts that there is a tension between the demands of friendship and those of reasonable belief, and concludes that the importance of epistemic rationality is outweighed by the importance of being a good friend. As Stroud explains, the idea that there can be a tension between what we have reason to do from one perspective and what we have reason to do from another, where one of these perspectives somehow outweighs the other, is familiar in philosophy – indeed, it is central to the readings in the earlier chapter on dilemma (Chapter 4). So it may not be so strange that the reasons provided by friendship could outweigh those provided by epistemic rationality: friendship might make us epistemically irrational in some of our beliefs about our friends, but that wouldn't mean that these beliefs are irrational overall, since they are justified by the importance of being a good friend.

The second response that Stroud considers (see $\boxed{\mathbf{r}}\rightarrow$) is similar to a position that E. J. Lemmon can be read as advancing in 'Moral Dilemmas' (see Chapter 4). Like the first response, this one grants that there is a conflict between the demands of friendship and those of epistemic rationality, but does not accept that either set of demands is more important than the other. According to this position, when the epistemic partiality of a good friend conflicts with her epistemic duties, there is just no fact of the matter about what she ought to believe: similar to what Lemmon suggests, this position would say that whatever a person believes in a case like this, her belief will be irrational from a certain perspective; and moreover, there is no other, 'higher' perspective that determines whether that belief is rational overall.

The last few paragraphs of Stroud's essay (see $\boxed{\mathbf{s}}\rightarrow$) describe a third response, which she thinks should be taken seriously even if it seems very radical and counterintuitive at first. Stroud argues that just as many moral philosophers have taken the importance of friendship to a good life to show that, contrary to some influential moral theories, morality does not require us to act impartially and treat people only in the way demanded from an 'objective' point of view, her argument might support a similar conclusion about epistemic rationality. That is, it might be that epistemic rationality is not, after all, a matter of believing only what is supported by an impartial review of the evidence, but that is permits – or demands – the forms of epistemic partiality that influence our beliefs about our friends. If this is right, then the tension between the demands of friendship and those of epistemic rationality would not, after all, be a real one: it would turn out to be epistemically rational to be partial towards our friends. Stroud identifies one reason philosophers would have for resisting this, namely that epistemic rationality is usually conceived as 'autonomous' with respect to practical concerns in a way that moral principles are not. Still, she thinks it is an option that deserves to be taken seriously along with the others she has considered.


Assume for the sake of argument that Stroud is correct in claiming that we are epistemically partial towards our friends. This suggests that friendship involves forming beliefs in ways that many philosophers would regard as irrational. What do you think is the right response to this puzzle? Do you favour one of the three responses Stroud discusses at the end? Do you think she is too quick to dismiss the possibility of rejecting or revising the epistemic partiality involved in friendship, or that our partiality towards our friends is just a matter of our taking into account the background evidence we have about them? Do you think there is a further response that Stroud should have considered?

List some of the advantages and disadvantages of each of these ways of responding to the apparent tension between friendship and epistemic rationality.
