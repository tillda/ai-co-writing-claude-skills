# 'For whether I am awake or asleep, two and three added together are five, and a square cannot have more than four sides. It seems impossible that such transparent truths should incur any suspicion of being false.' How does this claim contribute to the argument of Descartes' First Meditation?

This claim marks the intermediate resting point in Descartes' escalation of doubt. It identifies what the dreaming argument leaves untouched and motivates the next, more radical move. In my view its function in the First Meditation is structural rather than substantive: Descartes does not really endorse it as a final position. He uses it to set up the deceiving-God and malicious-demon arguments, which are designed precisely to overturn it. To miss this is to misread the meditation as a whole.

To see this, we should locate where the claim appears in the sequence of doubts. Descartes begins by attacking beliefs grounded in the senses. He notes that the senses sometimes deceive in cases of distance and small objects. But this only undermines a narrow class of beliefs. Plain perceptual judgements about the immediate surroundings — that I am sitting by the fire, that I am holding a paper — survive. To extend the doubt he turns to dreaming. Since I cannot tell from the inside whether I am dreaming now, every present perceptual belief is doubtful. Even my belief that I have hands could be a dream image.

This is where the claim about arithmetic and geometry enters. Descartes notices a limit to the dream argument. Dreams, he thinks, are like paintings: even the most fanciful painting must combine real colours and basic shapes. So the very general categories of extension, shape, and number must apply even within a dream. From this he concludes that the disciplines that deal with the simplest and most general things — arithmetic and geometry — are immune to the dream. Two and three make five whether I am awake or asleep. A square has four sides in any world. These transparent truths, Descartes says, cannot be doubted by the dream argument.

Notice the logical role of this claim. It is not an independent thesis in the meditation. It is a concession Descartes makes to himself in the voice of the meditator, midway through the escalation of doubt. He has just shown that the dream argument leaves the formal disciplines untouched. If he stopped here, his sceptical project would fail. He has set out to find some reason for doubt in every former belief. Arithmetic and geometry are former beliefs. So if these are immune, the project is incomplete.

The claim therefore functions as a problem to be solved. Descartes uses it to motivate the next argument. The very next paragraph introduces the deceiving God hypothesis. How do I know, he asks, that an omnipotent God has not made me such that I go wrong every time I count the sides of a square? The deceiving God scenario is designed to reach exactly the beliefs that the dream argument cannot reach. It targets the formal disciplines themselves. And when Descartes worries that the goodness of God might block the supposition, he replaces God with a malicious demon — a being who could corrupt my reasoning faculties so thoroughly that even my arithmetic judgements are unreliable.

So the contribution of the claim is twofold. First, it diagnoses the limit of the dream argument. Descartes is honest about how far that argument reaches and how far it does not. Second, it sets up the demon. Without the recognition that something escapes the dream, the demon would be unnecessary. The whole structure of the First Meditation depends on this transitional moment.

There is a deeper point. The claim also tells us something about Descartes' criterion of certainty. He treats arithmetic and geometry as paradigm cases of clarity. They are 'transparent' truths, and it 'seems impossible' that they should incur any suspicion of being false. This phrasing matters. Descartes is laying down what an ideal foundation for knowledge would look like — a class of beliefs that survive every form of doubt. By the end of the meditation he will have shown that even this paradigm is doubtful. The criterion remains, but no belief yet meets it. The Second Meditation will then offer the cogito as the first thing that does. So the arithmetic-and-geometry claim is part of a longer search for an indubitable foundation. It identifies the last candidate before the demon, and its eventual fall is what makes the search radical.

We might object that Descartes is wrong to think the dream argument leaves arithmetic alone. Why couldn't I just be dreaming that two and three make five? In a dream where my faculties are confused, I might feel certain of arithmetic propositions that are false. The objection has force. If we push it, the demon argument becomes redundant — the dream argument on its own would already reach the formal disciplines. Some commentators take this line. But I think Descartes' way of handling the case is more careful. The point is not that dreams change arithmetic; it is that dreams operate on the materials of waking life, and those materials include the basic categories. A dreamed arithmetical error is still an error of judgement, not a falsification of arithmetic itself. To attack the judgement-forming faculty as such requires more than the dream — it requires a story like the demon, in which the very mechanism that produces my apparent insight is corrupted.

A different objection is that the claim is not really plausible even on its own terms. We sometimes go wrong in arithmetic when we are tired or distracted. So the formal disciplines are not strictly immune to error. But this objection misses what Descartes means by 'transparent truths'. He does not deny that we make mistakes in calculation. He claims that the propositions themselves — that two and three make five, that a square has four sides — cannot be doubted on the strength of the dream argument. The mistakes are diagnosable as mistakes. The transparent truth is the corrected proposition, not any particular calculation.

So the claim about arithmetic and geometry is doing real work in the structure of the meditation. It tells us where the dream argument fails. It motivates the introduction of the deceiving God and the malicious demon. It identifies the paradigm of clarity that Descartes is searching for. And it sets up its own eventual overturn, which makes the doubt of the First Meditation radical rather than merely sceptical of perception. To read it as Descartes' settled view of mathematics would be to miss the meditation's argumentative arc. Descartes raises arithmetic and geometry as the last refuge before the demon, precisely so that he can take that refuge away.
