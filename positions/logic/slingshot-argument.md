# The Slingshot Argument (Skeptical of the Argument, Sympathetic to its Target)

The slingshot argument purports to show that if every true sentence corresponds to a fact, then every true sentence corresponds to *the same* fact — so correspondence theory either has only one fact (collapsing into triviality) or is incoherent.

I am a deflationist: I reject correspondence anyway (see *Truth Minimalism*). So in some sense the slingshot is preaching to the converted. But the slingshot is not the right argument for the conclusion. Its premises are precisely what a sophisticated correspondence theorist would reject, and the argument therefore begs the question against the only versions of correspondence worth refuting. Reject correspondence, yes; but reject it for the right reasons.

## The argument in outline

Two principles drive the standard slingshot (Davidson's version is canonical):

- **Substitution.** Co-referring singular terms can be substituted *salva facto* in "the fact that …" contexts. If "a = b", then "the fact that Fa" = "the fact that Fb".
- **Logical equivalence.** Logically equivalent sentences pick out the same fact. "The fact that p" = "the fact that q" whenever p ⇔ q.

Given these, plus a few definite-description manoeuvres, any true sentence is provably about the same fact as any other true sentence. Correspondence collapses.

## Why the argument doesn't earn its conclusion

1. **Both principles are exactly what a correspondence theorist denies.** A fine-grained correspondence theorist (Russell, Armstrong, structured-fact theorists) will say "the fact that snow is white" and "the fact that snow is white and 2 + 2 = 4" are *different* facts, even though the sentences are logically equivalent. The whole point of structured facts is that they have constituent structure that mirrors propositional structure. A theorist for whom facts have constituents will reject *Logical equivalence* outright. The slingshot's premise is what's at issue.

2. **Substitution in fact-contexts is referentially opaque.** "The fact that the morning star is bright" and "the fact that the evening star is bright" can be argued either way: extensionally identical, or — if facts are individuated by mode of presentation — distinct. The substitution principle assumes the question against the second view.

3. **The collapse is sleight of hand if we keep facts fine-grained.** Once *Logical equivalence* fails, the slingshot machine grinds to a halt. The "all-the-same-fact" conclusion is reached only by feeding in fact-individuation principles that strip facts of any structure that could distinguish them. This is question-begging.

The slingshot is therefore an argument against a *very specific* version of correspondence — one where facts are coarse-grained truth-conditions individuated by logical equivalence. That view is bad. But that view is bad because of the collapse, and "the slingshot shows the collapse" is just to restate the verdict, not derive it.

## What is right about the slingshot

The slingshot does flag a genuine pressure point: any correspondence theorist who wants to talk about "the fact that p" has to do real ontological work to keep their facts from collapsing into one. Either:

- They accept fine-grained, structured facts, in which case they need a *separate* theory of fact-structure that does no less than ape the syntax of propositions — at which point "correspondence" is doing no explanatory work the schema isn't already doing.
- Or they accept coarse-grained facts, in which case the slingshot really does collapse them.

That dilemma is the genuine philosophical content of the slingshot. It isn't the formal argument; it is the awkward methodological position the formal argument flags. The correspondence theorist must either adopt a baroque metaphysics of fact-structure, or accept the collapse. Neither is attractive.

## Why correspondence still fails (the real reasons)

Independent of the slingshot, correspondence theory faces the problems already documented in *Truth Minimalism*:

- The "fact" relation is obscure; every attempt to spell it out reduces to triviality (`<p>` is true iff p) or to compositional axioms no easier to state than the equivalence schema.
- Correspondence inflates the schema with metaphysical baggage that does no extra explanatory work.
- The intuition behind correspondence — that truth depends on how the world is — is fully captured by the schema combined with ordinary explanations of what makes p the case.

These reasons defeat correspondence without depending on the slingshot's contentious premises.

## Honest objections

1. **"You're conceding the slingshot's conclusion while rejecting its argument — isn't that having it both ways?"** No. The conclusion ("correspondence collapses") is true. The argument for it (the slingshot) is unsound, or at best question-begging. Many true conclusions have been argued for badly. The criticism that the conclusion is right doesn't validate the proof.

2. **"Davidson didn't intend the slingshot as a knockdown — only as a pressure on naive correspondence."** Granted. Read as a reductio of *coarse-grained* correspondence, the slingshot succeeds. Read as a refutation of correspondence per se, it fails. The danger is in the second use, which is how the argument often gets recruited in introductory presentations.

3. **"Fine-grained facts are themselves dubious metaphysics."** Possibly so. But that is a substantive complaint about Russellian / structured-fact ontology, not something the slingshot establishes. If structured facts are bad, they are bad on their own terms — not because the slingshot disposes of them.

## Considered view

The slingshot's conclusion is broadly correct: there is no respectable version of correspondence that survives serious scrutiny. But the slingshot does not earn that conclusion. It works only against versions of correspondence theory weak enough to be dispatched by simpler objections, and a sophisticated theorist can block it by adopting fine-grained facts. The genuine reason to reject correspondence is the obscurity and explanatory redundancy of the "fact" relation, not the slingshot's compressed reductio.

The slogan: the slingshot is a useful diagnostic — it shows correspondence theorists what kind of fact-ontology they cannot have — but it is not the argument that defeats correspondence. The argument that defeats correspondence is the equivalence schema doing all the work the correspondence theorist wanted "fact" to do, with none of the metaphysical commitments.
