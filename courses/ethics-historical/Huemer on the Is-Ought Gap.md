# Huemer on the Is-Ought Gap

A synthesis drawn from chapter 4 of Michael Huemer's *Ethical Intuitionism*, which contains his most sustained treatment of Hume's Law. Where the companion document on the Humean Conception of Reasons traces the points on which Huemer attacks Hume, this one traces the one major Humean doctrine that Huemer endorses and defends. Huemer thinks Hume was right about the is-ought gap, and he thinks the modern attempts to refute Hume's Law (Searle, Geach, Prior) all fail. He also thinks the defence has to be done carefully, and he uses Karmo's proof to do it. The interesting twist is that Huemer's reasons for endorsing Hume's Law are not Hume's reasons; the gap matters to Huemer because it is a lemma in the argument that moral knowledge ultimately requires intuition.

## 1. What Hume's Law actually claims

Hume's original passage from the *Treatise* is well known: in every system of morality the author proceeds in the ordinary way of reasoning, establishing the being of God or making observations about human affairs, and then suddenly the copulations "is" and "is not" give way to "ought" and "ought not", and this change is imperceptible but of the last consequence. Hume demands that the deduction of the new "ought" relation from the old "is" relations be observed and explained.

Huemer is careful (section 4.3.1) to extract the right doctrine from this passage. Hume is not merely saying that from "x is the case" one cannot infer "x ought to be the case". He is making a general point: no collection of facts about what is the case entails any claim about what ought to be. Equivalently, any non-evaluative description of the world can consistently be combined with any value system, or even with nihilism. The barrier is between two kinds of vocabulary, descriptive and evaluative, and the claim is that no purely descriptive set of premises can deductively yield a purely evaluative conclusion.

Huemer treats this as a doctrine about logical derivability, not about plausibility or probability. A nihilist who admits all the natural facts but refuses to value anything is not committing any logical error. He may be unreasonable, but he is not contradicting himself.

## 2. The initial justifications

Huemer offers two reasons (section 4.3.1) for thinking Hume's Law is true, before he turns to defending it against challenges.

The first is intuitive. It is hard to see, on the face of it, how a mere description of how the world is, in non-evaluative respects, could rule out an otherwise possible value system. A nihilist or an alien with a radically different evaluative scheme could agree with us about every natural fact and still part company on every evaluative one without contradicting himself. The intuition is not that nihilism would be plausible, only that it would be coherent.

The second is structural. Hume's Law is a special case of a more general logical principle: one cannot validly deduce a conclusion that applies a predicate P from premises that nowhere contain that predicate. This is true for any predicate, not just evaluative ones. You cannot infer that something is red from premises that nowhere mention redness. The is-ought gap, on this reading, is not a special quirk of evaluative discourse; it is the application to evaluative predicates of a feature shared by all predicates.

Huemer also flags what he takes to be a familiar mistake. Suppose I argue that communism is bad because it caused tens of millions of deaths, widespread poverty, and human suffering, and is reasonably expected to continue doing so. The argument seems sound and its premises are non-evaluative. Does it bridge the gap? No. It contains an implicit evaluative premise that life is good and that poverty and suffering are bad. Without that suppressed premise, the conclusion does not follow. Most ordinary moral arguments are like this: they look like they bridge the gap until you write out the missing evaluative premise that does the work. The communism argument illustrates the doctrine, not a counter-example to it.

## 3. The role Hume's Law plays in Huemer's larger argument

Section 4.3.1 contains a passage that is easy to miss but that determines what Huemer is and is not committed to by his endorsement of Hume's Law.

Huemer denies that Hume's Law refutes ethical reductionism. The Open Question Argument does that work in section 4.2; Hume's Law does not. What Hume's Law does for Huemer is serve as a lemma in his larger argument that moral knowledge cannot be accounted for without something like ethical intuition. The full argument has four limbs: we cannot know moral truths by observation; we cannot know non-trivial moral truths by deducing them from non-moral truths; we cannot know non-trivial moral truths by conceptual analysis; we cannot know moral truths by scientific reasoning or inference to the best explanation. Hume's Law is the reason for the second limb.

This positioning has an important consequence for how exceptions to Hume's Law should be handled. Suppose someone produces a valid deductive inference with non-evaluative premises and an evaluative conclusion, but in a form that could not actually be the route by which anyone comes to know the conclusion. Such an inference would be an exception to Hume's Law as traditionally stated. But it would not damage Huemer's argument. What Huemer needs is that there is no way of coming to know non-trivial moral truths by deduction from non-moral truths. Logical curiosities that span the gap without being epistemically usable do not undermine that claim.

This is why Huemer's response to Prior's challenge will look concessive. He will admit that Prior's example technically bridges the gap, while insisting that it does not damage the use he makes of Hume's Law. The reader should keep this distinction in mind throughout. Huemer is defending an epistemic version of Hume's Law, and his arguments about Searle and Geach are best read as showing that those alleged exceptions also fail to provide a route to moral knowledge from non-moral knowledge.

## 4. Searle's challenge

John Searle's famous derivation (section 4.3.2) starts from Jones uttering the words "I hereby promise to pay you, Smith, five dollars" and proceeds through a series of steps culminating in "Jones ought to pay Smith five dollars". The claim is that with enough background facts about social conventions added to the premises, the inference becomes valid, and the gap is bridged.

Huemer's reply turns on a distinction between two senses of "undertaking" an obligation. In the strong sense, to undertake an obligation is to successfully place oneself under an obligation, such that it logically follows that one is in fact under one. In the weak sense, to undertake an obligation is to purport to place oneself under an obligation, or to agree to accept such an obligation. The weak sense has no logical entailment to the existence of any actual obligation.

The analogy Huemer offers is illuminating: suppose someone calls me collect and I agree to accept the charges, but the phone company makes a mistake and never actually charges me. My acceptance of the charges does not entail that I receive any charges. Similarly, an agent might "accept" an obligation in the weak sense without thereby coming under any actual obligation. Whether the acceptance succeeds in placing him under an obligation depends on whether the moral facts cooperate. If nihilism is true and there are no obligations at all, then promises in the weak sense never generate obligations in the strong sense.

The nihilist, confronted with Searle's derivation, can grant the natural facts at every step. He can grant that Jones uttered the words. He can grant that Jones thereby made a promise in the weak sense. What he denies is the move to "Jones placed himself under an obligation" understood in the strong sense. Conventions of language and practice can settle that uttering certain words counts as making a promise; conventions cannot, by fiat, settle that promises generate real obligations. To assume otherwise is to assume the very thing the nihilist is denying.

Searle might reply that the weak sense does not capture our concept of promising. Huemer's response is to bite that bullet on the nihilist's behalf: if the strong sense is needed for genuine promising, then on the nihilist's view no one ever really promises, only purports to. That is unattractive English, but it is not incoherent, and it is not refuted by anything Searle has shown.

What this shows is that Searle's derivation only works if you assume that social conventions can generate normative facts, which is precisely what an opponent of Hume's Law needs to establish independently. The derivation either smuggles an evaluative premise (that promises in the weak sense really generate obligations) or trades on an ambiguity in "undertaking". Either way, it does not bridge the gap.

## 5. Geach's challenge

Peter Geach's challenge (section 4.3.3) is more ingenious. It runs:

1. If Evan were to promise to adopt some practice, then he would adopt it.
2. If Evan were to utter sentence W, he would be promising to adopt the practice of doing something wrong twice a day.
3. Nobody should adopt the practice of doing something wrong twice a day.
4. Therefore, if Evan were to utter W, he would adopt the practice of doing something wrong twice a day.
5. Therefore, Evan should not utter W.

Premise (3) is evaluative, but Geach observes that it is analytic: by definition, no one should do what is wrong, so no one should do something wrong twice a day. So Geach claims to have shown how to go from non-evaluative statements plus tautologies to a non-trivial evaluative conclusion.

Huemer raises two objections, either of which is sufficient.

The first is that the argument is invalid. The inference from (3) and (4) to (5) presupposes the form: "S ought not to do A; if S did B, S would do A; therefore S ought not to do B". This form is invalid, and Huemer constructs a counter-example. Imagine Jon, a judge, about to sentence Mary, a minor marijuana dealer. Jon is determined, out of irrational hatred for drug users, to sentence Mary to either life imprisonment or death; he could sentence her to a brief term but in fact will not. Now consider:

- John ought not to sentence Mary to life imprisonment. (True, because the just sentence is a brief term.)
- If John were to refrain from sentencing Mary to death, he would sentence her to life imprisonment. (True, given John's psychology.)
- Therefore, John ought not to refrain from sentencing Mary to death.

The conclusion is plainly false; what John ought to do is sentence Mary to a brief term. The inference form is invalid in general because it can happen that what one should do is to do B and refrain from A, but what one actually would do upon doing B is to do A as well. Geach's inference relies on this invalid form, so the derivation fails before it even reaches its evaluative conclusion.

The second objection is that premise (1) is not really non-evaluative. The relevant universally quantified reading of "If Evan were to promise to adopt some practice, then he would adopt it" includes the substitution instance: "If Evan were to promise to act wrongly, then Evan would act wrongly". Whether this conditional is true depends on what Evan would actually do upon making such a promise, which is a natural fact, but also on whether what he would do counts as wrongdoing, which is an evaluative fact. Suppose Evan, upon promising to act wrongly, would steal a piece of candy from his niece and order a hamburger. Whether the conditional is true now turns on whether stealing the candy or ordering the hamburger is wrong. If the actions are wrong, the conditional is true; if not, it is false. So the truth-value of the conditional, holding the natural facts fixed, depends on the value system. By the criterion Huemer takes from Karmo (section 4.3.5), that makes the statement evaluative.

Geach's argument either contains an invalid inference step or smuggles an evaluative premise into the supposedly non-evaluative ones. Either way, no derivation from purely non-evaluative premises to a non-trivial evaluative conclusion has actually been produced.

## 6. Prior's challenge

A.N. Prior's challenge (section 4.3.4) is the cleverest of the three because it cannot be answered by finding a hidden evaluative premise. It is also the one Huemer effectively concedes. The argument runs:

(A) 1. Tea-drinking is common in England.
    2. Therefore, tea-drinking is common in England, or all New Zealanders ought to be shot.

The inference is valid by disjunction introduction. The premise is non-evaluative. The conclusion contains "ought". Hume's Law, on its strict logical reading, is refuted.

Huemer considers two responses. One is to deny that the conclusion is genuinely evaluative. But this opens up a different inference:

(A') 1. Tea-drinking is not common in England.
     2. Tea-drinking is common in England, or all New Zealanders ought to be shot.
     3. Therefore, all New Zealanders ought to be shot.

If the disjunction in (A') is non-evaluative, then (A') spans the gap. Either way, something gets through.

Huemer's response is not to dispute the technical point. He grants that one of (A) and (A') technically bridges the gap. The reply is that this has little bearing on the problem of moral knowledge. Argument (A) gives us an "evaluative conclusion" only in a thin sense; the conclusion does not entail that anything has any evaluative property and cannot guide action. It is uninteresting. Argument (A') gives us an interesting evaluative conclusion, but it provides no model of how we could come to know that conclusion. Premise (1') of (A') is empirically discoverable, but premise (2) of (A') is the problem: there is no apparent way to know that disjunction without already relying on an evaluative judgment about whether all New Zealanders should be shot.

What we would want, to use Prior's strategy as a route to moral knowledge, is an argument combining the empirical accessibility of (A) with the interesting conclusion of (A'). But Prior's example does not show how to construct such an argument, and combining the premises of (A) and (A') would just produce a contradiction.

This is where the careful framing of section 4.3.1 pays off. Huemer is not committed to the strict logical version of Hume's Law that says no valid inference of any kind can bridge the gap. He is committed to an epistemic version: no useful route from non-evaluative knowledge to non-trivial evaluative knowledge passes through deductive inference alone. Prior's example is a logical exception, but not an epistemic one. It does nothing to show that we can come to know moral truths by deduction from non-moral truths.

## 7. Karmo's proof

The Prior concession leaves the question whether the epistemic version of Hume's Law can be given a rigorous defence. Huemer's answer (section 4.3.5) is that Toomas Karmo has done so.

Karmo's strategy is to define an evaluative statement contextually rather than absolutely. Some statements are uncontroversially evaluative ("Pleasure is good", "All New Zealanders ought to be shot"). Some are uncontroversially descriptive ("Tea-drinking is common in England"). Some sit in between, with their evaluative status depending on what the world is like. The Prior disjunction "Tea-drinking is common in England, or all New Zealanders ought to be shot" is non-evaluative if tea-drinking is common in England (because the disjunction is then true regardless of the value system) and evaluative if tea-drinking is not common in England (because its truth then turns on whether the second disjunct is true).

Karmo formalises this as follows:
- A "value system" is a maximal consistent conjunction of uncontroversially evaluative statements.
- A "possible world" is a maximal consistent conjunction of uncontroversially descriptive statements.
- The Determinacy Assumption: a possible world together with a value system are consistent with at most one assignment of truth-values for all statements.
- A statement S is evaluative in possible world w if, given w, the truth-value of S depends on which value system is correct.

The five conditions for an interesting argument bridging the gap are then:
i) Premises actually true.
ii) Premises non-evaluative in the actual world.
iii) Premises entail conclusion.
iv) Conclusion evaluative in the actual world.
v) Conclusion non-trivial.

Karmo proves no such argument can exist. The proof is by reductio. Suppose such an argument exists with premises P₁, P₂, ... and conclusion C. By condition (iv) and the non-triviality of (v), there are value systems v₁ and v₂ such that the actual world conjoined with v₁ entails C, while the actual world conjoined with v₂ entails ¬C. Since the premises entail C, ¬C entails that at least one of the premises is false. So the actual world conjoined with v₂ entails that at least one specific premise Pᵢ is false. But the actual world conjoined with the correct value system entails Pᵢ is true (by condition (i) and Determinacy). So the truth of Pᵢ, holding the actual world fixed, depends on which value system is correct. That makes Pᵢ evaluative in the actual world, contradicting condition (ii).

The proof is rigorous in a way the original Humean intuition is not. It shows precisely why no such argument can exist: a non-trivial evaluative conclusion is by construction one whose truth varies across value systems given the actual world; a valid inference forces the truth of the premises to track the truth of the conclusion; and so the truth of at least one premise must also vary across value systems given the actual world, which is exactly what it means for that premise to be evaluative in the actual world. The gap is genuine, and Karmo's framework explains why.

## 8. Maitzen's objection and Huemer's reply

Stephen Maitzen has objected to Karmo's taxonomy. Consider the statement:

(B) Every evaluative statement is false, or torturing babies is wrong.

According to Karmo, this is evaluative in every possible world: there is always a value system that makes it true (any system forbidding baby-torture makes the second disjunct true) and another that makes it false (a system endorsing baby-torture makes both disjuncts false). But Maitzen argues that in any world where nihilism (the thesis that every evaluative statement is false) holds, (B) is non-evaluative, since by hypothesis nihilism makes every evaluative statement false but (B) itself holds (its first disjunct is true). So (B) cannot be evaluative in such a world. This, Maitzen claims, immediately falsifies Karmo's taxonomy.

Huemer's reply is that nihilism, in Karmo's sense, is logically impossible. Two reasons. First, nihilism itself looks like an evaluative statement; in denying all evaluative statements, it would have to deny itself. Second, in any possible world both "Torturing babies is wrong" and "It is not the case that torturing babies is wrong" are evaluative statements in Karmo's sense (the truth-value of each depends on the value system). At least one of them must be true. So it is logically impossible that all evaluative statements be false.

Maitzen could of course mean nihilism in a different sense, perhaps the thesis that nothing has an evaluative property, but that thesis would not generate the objection, because it does not entail that the statement "torturing babies is wrong" lacks evaluative character. Karmo's taxonomy is a stipulative definition of "evaluative", and the question is only whether his stipulation captures the philosophically interesting class. Huemer thinks it does. The traditional class of statements considered evaluative falls within Karmo's class, so Karmo's proof shows that the traditional class cannot be known by deduction from the non-evaluative.

## 9. Why Hume's Law matters for the moral epistemology debate

Putting it together, Huemer's view is that Hume's Law is true in the form that matters for the theory of moral knowledge. The three modern challenges (Searle, Geach, Prior) fail or are concessive in ways that do not damage that form. Karmo's proof rigorously establishes the gap for the relevant class of statements. The doctrine is therefore secure.

The payoff is structural. If we cannot come to know non-trivial moral truths by deducing them from non-moral truths, then moral knowledge has to come from somewhere else. The Open Question Argument blocks the route through conceptual analysis (section 4.2). Observation is unavailable for paradigmatically evaluative truths (Karmo's framework defines evaluative statements precisely as those whose truth, given the natural facts, still varies with the value system). Inference to the best explanation faces well-known difficulties of its own. By a process of elimination, what is left is something like ethical intuition, which Huemer defends in chapter 5 as a faculty of intellectual seeming directed at evaluative content.

This is why Huemer's relationship to Hume here is very different from his relationship to Hume on motivation. On motivation, Hume is wrong, his argument for the Humean Conception is invalid, and the Conception itself cannot accommodate authority of morality, weakness of will, or the rationality of basic moral judgments. On the is-ought gap, Hume is right, and his being right is a crucial premise in the argument that ethical intuitionism is the only remaining account of how we know moral truths. The two relationships are independent because the doctrines are independent. Hume's Law is a logical thesis about derivability; the Humean Conception of Reasons is a psychological thesis about motivation. One can hold either without the other, and Huemer holds the first without the second.

## 10. Where the defence is strongest, where it is contestable

The strongest part of Huemer's defence is the Karmo proof. It is rigorous, it isolates the relevant class of statements via a clean stipulative definition, and it shows precisely where any putative gap-bridging argument must fail. The Maitzen objection turns out to be about whether Karmo's notion of "evaluative" is the philosophically interesting one, not about whether Karmo's argument is valid given his stipulation. Once the stipulation is granted, the proof is decisive.

The next strongest part is the response to Searle. The strong/weak distinction in "undertaking an obligation" is exactly the right diagnostic tool, and the collect-call analogy makes the structural point cleanly. Searle's derivation only works if conventions can generate normative facts by fiat, and that is precisely what an opponent of Hume's Law has to establish independently rather than presuppose.

The response to Geach is two-pronged and either prong is sufficient, which makes it a robust answer. The invalid inference form is shown by a simple counter-example, and the hidden evaluative content of premise (1) is shown by Karmo's criterion. A reader who finds one of the two responses unconvincing still has the other.

The most contestable part is the handling of Prior. Huemer effectively concedes that Hume's Law in its strictest logical form is false. The fallback is the epistemic version, which says that no useful route from non-evaluative knowledge to non-trivial evaluative knowledge runs through deduction alone. This is what Huemer needs for his larger project, and Karmo's proof secures it. But a reader who took Hume to be defending the strict logical version may feel that Huemer has won the war by abandoning the original battlefield. The reply is that the strict version was never what made Hume's Law philosophically interesting. The interesting question is always whether moral knowledge can be deduced from non-moral knowledge, and on that question Huemer holds the line.

A separate contestable point is the asymmetry in Huemer's overall position. He defends Hume's Law because it serves intuitionism, and he attacks the Humean Conception of Reasons because it serves anti-realism. A sceptical reader might wonder whether the selection is principled or partisan. Huemer's reply, scattered across the chapters but explicit at points, is that the two doctrines are independent and that he is not endorsing or rejecting them as a package; he is following the arguments where they go. The Karmo proof goes through; Hume's argument for the Humean Conception does not. The differing verdicts reflect the differing strengths of the arguments, not a package deal.

Read alongside the companion document on Huemer's objections to the Humean Conception of Reasons, this defence of Hume's Law shows the precise shape of Huemer's relationship to Hume. He treats Hume as a partial ally on the logical structure of moral discourse and a deep opponent on the psychology of motivation. The is-ought gap is one of two pillars of his moral epistemology (the other being the Open Question Argument), and it is a pillar he inherits, with modifications, from Hume.
