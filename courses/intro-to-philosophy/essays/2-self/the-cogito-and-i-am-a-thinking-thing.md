# The cogito and 'I am a thinking thing'

**Question:** How does the proposition 'I am, I exist' contribute to Descartes's Second Meditation argument that he is a thinking thing?
**Answer:** It supplies the only point that survives radical doubt — the meditator's own existence whenever he thinks — and so provides the fixed term to which Descartes attaches the question of *what* this "I" is.

**Question:** Explain and evaluate the argument Descartes gives in the Second Meditation for the claim that he is a thinking thing.
**Answer:** Descartes argues that nutrition, movement, and sense-perception depend on the body and so fall under the doubt, while thinking is inseparable from him; this argument shows at most that thinking is the property he cannot suppose himself to lack, not that he is *only* a thinking thing.

**Question:** 'I am a thing that thinks.' Explain and evaluate the reasoning which leads Descartes to this conclusion.
**Answer:** The reasoning runs from the cogito to the question "what am I?" and subtracts every property whose claim on him is contingent on the body, leaving thought; the move is sound up to a point but smuggles in a substantive metaphysical conclusion when it shifts from "thought is what I cannot doubt of myself" to "I am, in the strict sense, only a thinking thing".

The Second Meditation begins where the First leaves off. The meditator is in a deep whirlpool of doubt and decides to push the doubt to its limit, asking whether anything at all survives. The cogito is the answer to that question, and from the cogito Descartes builds his account of the self as a thinking thing. In my view, the cogito itself is robust at the level it operates — it secures the existence of the thinker. The further conclusion that the thinker is a thing whose nature is *only* thinking is not yet earned by what the doubt has shown, and the move from one to the other is where Descartes's argument is weakest.

Take the cogito first. Descartes notes that even on the supposition that everything he sees is illusion, that his memory tells lies, that he has no body, no senses, no place in the world, one thing cannot be made false. If a deceiver is deceiving him, he must exist to be deceived. If he is convincing himself of something, he must exist to be convinced. So the proposition "I am, I exist" is necessarily true whenever it is put forward by him or conceived in his mind. The argument has the form of a self-confirming thought: the very act of thinking the proposition makes the proposition true. This is its strength. It does not depend on any premise the doubt could threaten.

This is the contribution to the Second Meditation that the first specific is asking about. "I am, I exist" is the firm point Descartes was looking for, the Archimedean lever. Without it the meditator has nothing to build on. With it, he has a single secure subject around which to organise the next question: what is this "I" whose existence is now established? The role of the proposition is therefore not directly to deliver the conclusion that the meditator is a thinking thing, but to anchor the inquiry in a survivor of doubt. The thinking-thing conclusion comes from a further argument that asks what properties of the surviving "I" the doubt has and has not undermined.

That further argument runs as follows. Descartes considers what he formerly took himself to be — a man, with a face, hands, a body, capable of nutrition, movement, sense-perception, and thinking. He then re-runs the doubt against each of these properties. Nutrition and movement clearly depend on the body. The deceiver hypothesis lets him doubt that he has a body, so under the doubt he cannot claim to nourish or move. Sense-perception in the full sense — really seeing, really hearing — also depends on the body, since seeing requires eyes and hearing requires ears. So under the doubt he cannot claim to sense in the full sense. Thinking, however, is different. Thinking is something the deceiver cannot make him lack while the deceiver is at work. Even doubting is a form of thinking, so the very effort to doubt that he is thinking presupposes that he is. Thinking therefore survives the doubt where the bodily properties do not. From this Descartes concludes that he is, in the strict sense, only a thing that thinks.

Each step of the argument deserves separate evaluation. The first half is solid. Thinking is the only one of the listed properties that the deceiver cannot strip away from the meditator while he is thinking. So if we are looking for a property the meditator can be sure he has, given the supposition of total deception, thinking is the answer. There is no quarrel with this point as far as it goes.

The trouble begins at the conclusion. The argument is supposed to show that the meditator is a *thinking thing*, meaning at minimum that thinking is essential to him, and at maximum — and this is the move Descartes goes on to use in the Sixth Meditation — that he is *only* a thinking thing. The doubt, however, does not show this. The doubt shows that thinking is the only property he can be sure he has under the supposition of total deception. That is an epistemic point about what the meditator can know about himself given the doubt. It is not a metaphysical point about what he essentially is.

To see why these are different, consider an analogy. Suppose I cannot, in some restricted state, verify whether the person standing in front of me is wearing a hat. I can verify that they are wearing a coat. From "I can verify that they are wearing a coat" to "they are wearing only a coat" is obviously a non-sequitur. The latter requires that I have some independent reason to think the hat is not present, not just that I cannot verify it. The same shape of inference is at work in Descartes's argument. From "I can verify that I am thinking" to "I am only a thing that thinks" requires the assumption that whatever cannot be verified under the doubt is not part of my essence. That assumption is not innocent; it is precisely the view that the doubt is supposed to deliver, not assume.

This is the substance of two famous objections, which the prompt's specifics do not directly call for but which sharpen the assessment. Hobbes's worry was that a corporeal thing might think, and the cogito does not rule this out. Arnauld's worry was that the meditator's nature might also include extension, even though he cannot, under the doubt, perceive himself as extended. Both objections home in on the same gap. The cogito establishes thinking. It does not establish that thinking is the *only* thing that I am, or that I am a non-physical substance whose essence is exhausted by thought.

Descartes's reply is that he is not yet claiming to know what he in fact is, only what he can know himself to be under the doubt. But this is the point at which the rhetoric of the *Meditations* runs ahead of the argument. By the end of the Second Meditation Descartes has moved from "thinking is what I cannot doubt of myself" to "I am, in the strict sense, only a thing that thinks". The shift is from epistemic claim to metaphysical claim. Without further argument — which the Second Meditation does not provide — the metaphysical claim is not earned.

There is a smaller but related point about the move from thinking to a thinking *thing*. The cogito, on a strict reading, supports something like "thinking is occurring". The further inference to "I, a substance, exist as the bearer of this thinking" depends on conceptual material — the substance/property distinction — that the doubt has not validated. The Second Meditation treats this inference as immediate; a more careful reconstruction would treat it as a separate step requiring separate defence. This complaint is more famously associated with Lichtenberg's later remark that we should say "it thinks" rather than "I think", but the underlying point goes back to objections raised within Descartes's own century.

So the assessment is mixed and the mixture is structured. The cogito is good philosophy: a robust argument for the existence of the thinking subject. The next move — "I am a thing that thinks" — is also defensible if read minimally: thinking is the property the meditator cannot suppose himself to lack. The third move — "I am, in the strict sense, *only* a thing that thinks" — is not defensible without further argument that the Second Meditation does not supply. Recognising these layers shows what the proposition "I am, I exist" contributes to the Second Meditation. It contributes the fixed term that allows the inquiry into self-nature to begin. What the inquiry then concludes is correct in its modest form and overreaches in its strong one.
