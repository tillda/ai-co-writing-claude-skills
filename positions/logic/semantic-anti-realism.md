# Semantic Anti-Realism (against)

Dummett's anti-realism runs roughly: meaning is constituted by use; we can manifest understanding only of conditions we can recognise to obtain; therefore, recognition-transcendent truth-conditions are unintelligible; therefore, we should adopt warranted-assertibility (or proof) conditions in place of classical truth-conditions; therefore, bivalence fails for any sentence whose conditions could obtain undetectably.

I reject this. The argument deserves engagement — it is the most serious philosophical challenge to bivalence on the table, far more serious than dialetheism — but the chain from manifestation to bivalence-rejection breaks at multiple links. Bivalence stands; classical logic stands.

This position connects to my classical-logic commitments and to my deflationism: bivalence is a near-trivial consequence of the schema combined with the meaning of "not" (see *Classical Logic*), and the deflationary picture of truth does not depend on epistemic accessibility (see *Truth Minimalism*).

## Dummett's argument, unpacked

The anti-realist case has a standard structure:

1. **Meaning is use.** A theory of meaning must specify what speakers know in knowing the language.
2. **Manifestation requirement.** Whatever speakers know about meaning must be manifestable in their linguistic behaviour. Knowledge that cannot be manifested is no part of meaning.
3. **Acquisition requirement.** Linguistic competence is acquired through training in a public practice; only conditions speakers can be trained to recognise can enter into the meaning they acquire.
4. **From these:** speakers cannot grasp truth-conditions that transcend recognition. So the *meaning* of a sentence cannot consist in such conditions.
5. **Replace:** truth-conditions with assertibility-conditions (or proof-conditions, in mathematics).
6. **Consequence:** for sentences whose conditions can obtain undetectably, neither truth nor falsity is determined; bivalence fails for these sentences. Classical logic, which presupposes bivalence, cannot apply universally.

This is a tightly knit argument. To resist, one must break a specific link — not just declare the conclusion unwelcome.

## Where the argument breaks

Four points of resistance, roughly in order of decreasing concession:

1. **Manifestation does not require verification-by-the-speaker.** Even granting the manifestation requirement, what speakers must be able to manifest is *understanding* — their grasp of how the sentence is used, which conditions count for and against assertion, what follows from the sentence and what it follows from. None of this requires the speaker to be able to verify whether the sentence is in fact true. I can manifest perfect understanding of "there are continents on the far side of Andromeda we will never observe" by deploying the sentence correctly in inferences — applying excluded middle to it, embedding it in conditionals, conjoining it, denying it. Understanding can be manifested through inferential behaviour without verification of the truth-value. The leap from manifestation to verification-conditions is unwarranted.

2. **Recognition-transcendent truth is graspable through compositional understanding.** I understand "no event happened in this region of space-time" by understanding "event", "region", "space-time", and the negation. The whole is grasped through its parts plus the syntax. I do not need to be able to verify whole sentences to grasp them — I need only grasp their components and how they combine. Compositionality dissolves the manifestation worry for any sentence whose components are independently manifestable, even when the whole sentence concerns conditions I can't verify.

3. **From "we can't verify" to "no determinate truth value" is a non-sequitur.** This is the crucial slip. Even granting (against (1) and (2)) that meaning is verification-tied, that gives us at most an *epistemic* limit on what we can know. It does not yield a *metaphysical* claim that the sentence has no determinate truth value. The world is not constrained by what we can find out about it. To slide from "we can't tell" to "there is no fact of the matter" is to identify the world with our cognitive reach — which is exactly the contentious idealist commitment the anti-realist owes us a separate argument for. Dummett's argument from manifestation does not deliver that further step.

4. **Self-undermining.** If anti-realism is right and recognition-transcendent truth is unintelligible, then the meta-claim "there is no recognition-transcendent truth" is itself either intelligible only as itself recognition-immanent (in which case it is just the report of our verificationist practice, with no metaphysical bite) or it is making a recognition-transcendent claim about the world's structure — which the position forbids. The anti-realist needs the metaphysical reading to make their position substantive, but the metaphysical reading is exactly the kind of claim their argument rules out. Either the position collapses into a description of our practice (and is consistent with realism about truth) or it is incoherent on its own terms.

Each link can be challenged independently; I think (3) is the cleanest break, but (1) and (2) are also strong, and (4) shows the position has trouble even articulating itself stably.

## Why the connection to bivalence is overplayed

Even if the anti-realist's positive picture were right, the move from "verification-conditions" to "rejection of bivalence" is not automatic. A verificationist could equally hold:

- That every meaningful sentence has determinate verification-conditions even when those conditions can't always be checked, in which case bivalence over verification-conditions still holds.
- That the relevant logic is intuitionist (which abandons LEM but retains bivalence in a restricted form via "true = provable").
- That bivalence holds at the level of *meaning* even when it fails at the level of present knowledge.

So Dummett's package — verificationism *plus* rejection of bivalence — is one option among several even within the anti-realist family. The bivalence-rejection is not entailed by the manifestation-and-acquisition argument; it comes from a further move (typically the identification of truth with proof or warranted assertibility, plus the observation that not every well-formed sentence is decidable). That further move can be questioned without disputing the rest of the picture.

## The relation to classical logic and the schema

My positive case for bivalence does not depend on rebutting Dummett. It rests on the meanings of "not" and "true" (see *Classical Logic*): for any proposition-expressing sentence, the schema plus the semantics of negation force bivalence by a short reductio. Dummett's argument would have to show that the schema fails or that "not" has different semantics. He doesn't show either; he attacks the *intelligibility* of recognition-transcendent truth-conditions, which is a distinct issue.

So the burden of proof is symmetric or, I would say, against the anti-realist: bivalence is the default, supported by a short argument from meaning; the anti-realist must give us positive reasons to abandon a default that is independently well-motivated. The manifestation argument is not such a reason.

## Honest objections

1. **"Mathematical anti-realism (intuitionism) has independent appeal."** True, especially for mathematics where there is a real question about whether undecided sentences have determinate truth-values. I'm sympathetic to intuitionism *in mathematics* as a philosophy of mathematical practice — but that is a local position about mathematical objects and their construction, not a general theory of meaning. Dummett's mistake is to inflate intuitionism into a general semantics. The local appeal does not vindicate the global view.

2. **"Manifestation is more compelling than you allow."** Granted, manifestation has some pull: a notion of meaning untethered from any behavioural manifestation does look idle. My response is that manifestation is satisfied by *inferential* behaviour, which is what most sophisticated meaning-theories rely on anyway. So manifestation can be honoured without verificationism.

3. **"Anti-realism about specific subject-matters (the past, the future, other minds) deserves separate consideration."** Yes — there are local anti-realisms (about the past, about ethical facts, about modality) that have independent motivations not flowing from Dummett's manifestation argument. My rejection here is targeted at the *general* semantic anti-realism that makes a claim about meaning per se. Local anti-realisms about specific domains are different debates and should be judged on their own merits.

4. **"You're presupposing realism in formulating the argument."** The familiar tu quoque: any rebuttal of anti-realism in classical terms helps itself to bivalence and so begs the question. Reply: the schema is not a *realist* commitment in the contentious sense. It is a deflationary platitude; both realists and anti-realists of various stripes accept some version of it. If the anti-realist denies the schema, that is a substantial concession the manifestation argument was supposed to *avoid* having to make. So the argument isn't question-begging; it just relies on a shared starting point.

## Considered view

Reject Dummettian semantic anti-realism. The manifestation argument has real philosophical force at the level of *meaning*, but it does not carry across to the metaphysical claim that bivalence fails. The slip from "we can't always tell" to "there is no fact of the matter" is the central flaw, and no anti-realist argument I know of has plugged it. Bivalence holds because of what "not" and "true" mean; that argument is short, classical, and independent of disputes about manifestation.

The slogan: take Dummett seriously as a theory of *meaning* (where there is real work for verification-conditions to do as part of an inferentialist semantics), but resist his slide from semantics to metaphysics. Bivalence is not vulnerable to manifestation arguments.
