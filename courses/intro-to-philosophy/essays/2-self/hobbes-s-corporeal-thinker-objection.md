# Hobbes's corporeal-thinker objection

**Question:** In connection with the Second Meditation, Hobbes said that it was possible that something that thinks should be something corporeal. Do Descartes' arguments succeed in ruling this possibility out?
**Answer:** No: the Second Meditation establishes that thinking is occurring and that the meditator can identify himself as a thinker, but the move from "I cannot doubt that I think" to "I am not a corporeal thing" requires a step the cogito does not earn, and Descartes's later defences of that step do not survive scrutiny.

Hobbes's objection is one of the sharpest early responses to the Second Meditation, and in my view it is correct. The cogito establishes the existence of the thinking subject. The further argument in the Second Meditation establishes that thinking is the property the meditator cannot suppose himself to lack while doubting. Neither result rules out that the thing doing the thinking is, in fact, a corporeal thing. The dualist conclusion Descartes wants — that the thinker is, in the strict sense, only a thinking thing and not a corporeal one — requires an additional step that the *Meditations* never properly secures.

Let me state the structure of the disagreement. Descartes argues, in effect, like this:

1. I cannot doubt that I think.
2. I can doubt that I have a body.
3. So whatever I cannot doubt about myself is not the body.
4. So thinking is essential to me, and the body is not.
5. So I am, in the strict sense, only a thinking thing — a non-corporeal substance.

Hobbes's objection targets the move from step 3 to step 5. Even granting that there is something I cannot doubt about myself — namely, that I think — and granting that I can doubt that I have a body, it does not follow that thinking is not done by a corporeal thing. What follows is only that under the doubt I cannot verify that I have a body. The doubt is an *epistemic* condition; what it shows about what I can and cannot know about myself does not by itself license metaphysical conclusions about what I am.

To see why this is not a quibble, distinguish two questions. The first is whether, under the doubt, I have access to information about my own bodily nature. The second is whether my actual nature includes corporeality. Descartes's argument establishes a negative answer to the first. The deceiver hypothesis lets me suppose I have no body, so I cannot use the property "is corporeal" as part of what I know about myself under the doubt. But this is a fact about what I can know, not about what I am. The thing that survives the doubt — the thinking subject — might in fact be a corporeal thing whose corporeality the doubt has temporarily put out of view.

An analogy makes the point clearer. Suppose I cannot, in some restricted state, verify whether the person standing in front of me is wearing a hat. I can verify that they are wearing a coat. Concluding that they are wearing only a coat — that they are not in fact wearing a hat — does not follow from this. The same shape of inference is at work in Descartes's argument. From "I can verify that I am thinking" to "I am only a thing that thinks" requires the assumption that whatever cannot be verified under the doubt is not part of my essence. That assumption is precisely what is at issue.

Descartes's published reply to Hobbes invokes the doctrine of clear and distinct perception. He argues that he can clearly and distinctly perceive himself as a thinking, non-extended thing, and clearly and distinctly perceive corporeality as something independent of thought. From this he concludes that thought and extension are really distinct, and so the thinker is not a corporeal thing. The reply is not convincing for two reasons.

First, the criterion of clear and distinct perception, by the time of the Second Meditation, is not yet validated. Descartes establishes the reliability of clear and distinct perception only in the Third Meditation, after proving God's existence. In the Second Meditation it is being used as a guide to truth without authority. Hobbes can perfectly reasonably refuse to grant clear-and-distinct perception any privileged access to the meditator's actual nature.

Second, even granting clear-and-distinct perception, the Cartesian inference is open to a more pointed objection. Clearly and distinctly perceiving myself as a thinking, non-extended thing requires that my self-conception, while doubting, presents me as non-extended. But this is not the same as my actually being non-extended. A creature whose self-representation, while in a doubting state, leaves out its corporeal nature could still be a corporeal thing whose self-representation is selective. Descartes treats the absence of bodily content from the meditator's self-representation as evidence that the body is not part of his nature. But absence in self-representation is not absence in nature. The brain that produces my self-representation is not itself an item my self-representation typically includes; that does not show that my self-representation is being produced by something non-physical.

There is a related point that comes from physicalism. If consciousness is what the brain is doing — a process running on neural hardware — then it is no surprise that the meditator, attending to the contents of his consciousness, finds thinking and not extension. Consciousness presents its own contents; it does not, ordinarily, present the hardware that produces those contents. We do not see neurons; we see the world (or seem to see it) and we have thoughts and experiences. So the meditator's reports about himself are perfectly consistent with the thinker being a corporeal thing whose corporeality is invisible from the inside. Descartes mistakes a fact about the structure of self-presentation for a fact about the structure of self-being.

Hobbes's objection, then, is not merely a sceptical complaint. It identifies a real gap in the Second Meditation argument. Descartes's reply does not close the gap. The clear-and-distinct-perception manoeuvre presupposes a reliability claim it has not yet earned, and even granted reliability it confuses the way I appear to myself under the doubt with the way I am.

What does the cogito actually license, then, on the question of corporeality? Strictly, it licenses three things. There is thinking. The thinker exists. Thinking is the property the thinker cannot suppose himself to lack while thinking. None of this entails that the thinker is not corporeal. None of it entails that the thinker is corporeal either. The cogito is silent on that question. Settling it requires further information — about the relation between mental processes and physical processes, about the available metaphysical accounts of consciousness — that the *Meditations* does not provide.

It is worth noting that the considerations supporting the corporeal-thinker possibility have only strengthened since Hobbes wrote. Modern neuroscience identifies tight correlations between brain states and mental states; selective brain damage produces selective mental impairment; pharmacological intervention reshapes thought. None of this proves that thinking is corporeal in the simple sense, but it makes the hypothesis that thinking is what brains do far more plausible than the hypothesis that thinking is what immaterial substances do, and it puts the burden on the substance dualist to explain why a non-physical thinker is needed at all. Descartes's arguments do not meet that burden, and on the textbook understanding of those arguments — the Second Meditation taken together with the *Replies* — he never does.

So the answer is no. Descartes's arguments do not rule out the corporeal-thinker possibility. The cogito is robust, and the conclusion that thinking is the property the meditator cannot doubt is reasonable, but the leap to non-corporeality is unwarranted. Hobbes's diagnosis is correct, and a physicalist conclusion is consistent with everything the *Meditations* actually establishes.
