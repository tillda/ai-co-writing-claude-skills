# Strawson's individuation principle for minds

**Question:** Strawson states the principle: 'If we are to talk coherently about individual consciousnesses or minds...we must know the difference between one such item and two such items.' Is this principle acceptable? Does it make a problem for Descartes?
**Answer:** The principle is acceptable — talk about countable items requires identity conditions for those items — and it does make a problem for Descartes, because Cartesian minds, as non-spatial immaterial substances, can be individuated only by piggybacking on the persons whose minds they are.

Strawson's principle is a familiar requirement in disguise. Whenever we use a count noun — "chair", "argument", "song" — we have to know what makes one chair different from two, what makes the *same* chair persist over time, and so on. These are identity conditions. Without them, we cannot count, cannot re-identify, cannot say truly that there are two of something rather than one. So if "mind" is a count noun, and we want to talk about "this mind" and "that mind", we have to know what makes one mind a different mind from another. The principle is an instance of a more general logical demand. It is not optional. In my view, the principle is acceptable on those grounds, and it does cause serious problems for Descartes — though to see what kind of problem and how serious, we have to look at the available resources for individuating minds and ask whether the Cartesian can make use of them.

The principle is acceptable for the following reason. Talk about individuals presupposes that they are countable, and countability presupposes identity criteria. For most kinds of entity we have such criteria ready to hand. Two chairs occupy different regions of space. Two arguments have different premises or different conclusions or different inferential structures. Two songs differ in melody, lyrics, or composition. The criteria can be more or less precise, and there are sometimes hard cases at the margins, but in all these cases the criteria are workable enough to support normal counting and re-identification. If "mind" works as a count noun in our discourse — and Cartesian dualism explicitly treats it as one, since the doctrine speaks of "your mind" and "my mind" as separate substances — then minds need such criteria. The principle is uncontroversial as a formal demand.

A possible worry is that the principle is too strong. Perhaps we can talk coherently about minds without having explicit identity criteria, the way ordinary speakers manage with concepts like "thought" or "feeling" without having a worked-out theory of when two thoughts are the same thought. This is a fair point but does not threaten the principle as Strawson uses it. Strawson does not require explicit, formal criteria. He requires that we have at least some grasp of the difference between one and two. For ordinary count nouns, we do have this grasp, even when we cannot articulate the criteria. The pressure on the Cartesian is that for minds, taken as Cartesian substances, even this informal grasp fails.

Now to Descartes. The Cartesian conception of the mind is the conception of an immaterial substance whose essence is thought. Cartesian minds are not in space. They are not made of material parts. They have no shape and no location. The standard means of individuation we use for spatial objects are therefore unavailable. We cannot say that one mind is over here and another over there, since neither is anywhere. We cannot say that one mind is taller than another, or older, or larger. We cannot count minds the way we count bodies, by surveying a region and tallying the items it contains.

What individuation is available to the Cartesian? A few candidates suggest themselves, and each fails or only succeeds at the cost of giving up substance dualism.

The first candidate is qualitative content. Perhaps minds can be individuated by what they are thinking — different thoughts make different minds. This will not work. Two minds may at a given moment have qualitatively identical thoughts (suppose two people simultaneously work out the same arithmetic problem), and yet there are still two minds. Conversely, one mind has many different thoughts at different times, and yet remains one mind. Qualitative content of thoughts does not match up with mind-individuation.

The second candidate is the body the mind is associated with. Two minds are different because they are causally united with different bodies. This works in practice but at a cost. It makes mind-individuation parasitic on body-individuation. A Cartesian mind is then individuated not by its own essential properties but by something external to it — the body it happens to be attached to. This is awkward for a doctrine on which mind and body are independent substances. Worse, it strands the Cartesian when it comes to disembodied minds, which Cartesian doctrine is committed to allowing as possible. If minds are individuated by their bodies, what individuates a disembodied mind from another? Cartesianism cannot answer this.

The third candidate is the person whose mind it is. Two minds are different because they belong to different persons. This is, in fact, how we ordinarily distinguish minds. But, as Strawson develops elsewhere in his discussion of personhood, this is fatal to the dualist project. The Cartesian was supposed to be analysing the person as a composite of mind and body. If the mind can only be individuated via the person, then the concept of the person is more basic than the concept of the mind, and the dualist reduction collapses. The Cartesian was building "person" out of "mind" and "body", but the building blocks turn out to require the finished product.

So the principle creates a real problem. Either Cartesian minds have no individuation criterion — in which case "this mind" and "that mind" do not refer to anything determinate and the dualist's substantive talk of separate minds is empty — or they have criteria that are parasitic on bodies or persons, in which case the dualist's metaphysical priorities are inverted. Either way, substance dualism is in trouble.

A defender of Cartesianism might respond that minds are simply *primitive* unities and that no further criterion is needed beyond "this very mind is this very mind". The response is unconvincing. Saying that minds are primitive does not solve the problem; it merely refuses to engage with it. We can say the same for any kind of thing. Bare primitive identity is not enough to support the count-noun usage that the Cartesian is otherwise eager to deploy. If minds are primitively distinct, we can ask: how many of them are there? The Cartesian is then committed to giving an answer in terms that do not appeal to bodies or persons, and there is no such answer.

A more sophisticated defender might appeal to soul-stuff in some thin sense — different minds are different by being different "spiritual atoms", on analogy with the way different physical atoms are different. This re-imports a kind of sub-personal individuation that is implausible. Apart from being a piece of metaphysics nothing in our experience supports, it makes minds more like physical things than the Cartesian wanted them to be, and it raises the obvious follow-up question of how spiritual atoms are themselves individuated.

The conclusion is therefore twofold. The principle is acceptable: it states a general requirement on count-noun talk, and "mind" in Cartesian discourse is a count noun. And the principle does make a problem for Descartes: Cartesian minds, taken as non-spatial immaterial substances independent of bodies and persons, have no available individuation criterion, while the criteria that do work — bodies, persons — undermine the substance-dualist priorities the Cartesian needs. The pressure is exactly the kind that should make us prefer a view on which persons are the primitive units and minds are predicable aspects of them, not a view on which minds are stand-alone substances.
