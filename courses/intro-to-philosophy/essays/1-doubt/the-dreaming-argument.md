# "Suppose then that I am dreaming, and that these particulars — that my eyes are open, that I am moving my head and stretching out my hands — are not true. Perhaps, indeed, I do not even have such hands or such a body at all" (Descartes, First Meditation). How does Descartes employ this supposition as a premise in an argument for the rationality of doubt? Is that argument persuasive?

Descartes uses the dreaming supposition to drive a sceptical argument with a clear shape: I cannot tell from the inside whether I am dreaming now, so for any present perceptual belief, I cannot rule out that it is false. In my view the argument is persuasive against ordinary perceptual beliefs, though not as radical as the deceiver argument that follows it. The dream argument shows the rationality of doubt about the external world — but it leaves a residue, namely the seemings themselves, which remain whether or not the world matches them.

The argument runs as follows. (1) I have often had dreams in which I seemed to be sitting by the fire, holding a piece of paper, with my eyes wide open. (2) On those occasions, the experiences felt no different from the way they feel now. (3) There are no sure signs by which I can distinguish dreaming from waking. (4) So for any present perceptual belief — that my hands are in front of me, that I am here, that there is paper before me — I cannot rule out that I am merely dreaming it. (5) If I cannot rule that out, the belief is doubtful. (6) So all my present perceptual beliefs are doubtful.

The premise that does the most work is (3). Premise (1) is uncontroversial: most people remember vivid dreams that felt indistinguishable from waking life at the time. Premise (2) is also uncontroversial in the relevant sense — when we are inside a dream, we usually do not know we are dreaming. The disputed step is whether there are no sure signs at all by which we can tell. Descartes thinks there are none.

We might be tempted to reply that there are signs. We can pinch ourselves, try to read a complex passage of text, check whether the world behaves coherently, or notice the absurdity of certain dream events. But this reply does not work. The pinching and reading checks only distinguish certain kinds of mundane dreams from waking. A more thoroughgoing dream — say, a long dream in which all my faculties function normally — is not refuted by these tests. I might dream that I pinch myself and feel the pain. I might dream that I read a page of text. The signs that distinguish ordinary sleep from waking are themselves available within a sufficiently elaborate dream. Once the supposition is pushed that hard, no sign is available that the dream cannot mimic.

So the conclusion follows: doubt about ordinary perceptual beliefs is rational. To say a belief is rational to doubt is just to say that the alternative — confident assent — is unwarranted given my evidence. And given that I cannot rule out the dream hypothesis, the alternative is unwarranted.

But the argument has limits. There is a residue that survives even when the dream is granted. The point is sometimes put this way: even if I am asleep and seeing nothing, I certainly seem to see, hear, and feel. The seeming itself is real. This is not a refutation of the dream argument. Rather, it shows what the argument does and does not undermine. It undermines beliefs about the external world — about hands, fires, papers. It does not undermine beliefs about the present contents of my own consciousness. I cannot doubt that I am having an experience of seeming to see a paper, even if I doubt whether the paper is there. So the dream argument is local in its target. It targets beliefs about the world beyond the mind.

Descartes himself notices a second limit. Even within a dream, he thinks, certain very general features must hold. Dreams are like paintings: even the most fanciful painting combines real colours, shapes, and proportions. So extension, number, and shape — and along with them arithmetic and geometry — escape the dream argument. Two and three make five whether I am awake or asleep. A square has four sides in any world. This is why Descartes does not stop at the dream. He goes on to introduce the malicious demon precisely because the dream argument leaves these formal disciplines untouched.

Is the argument persuasive against the targets it claims? I think it is. The standard objection is that we have a sense of waking confidence — a kind of clarity that dreams lack — and that this confidence is itself a sign of waking. But the objection is unconvincing. Confidence is also produced by dreams. People feel certain in their dreams that they are awake, and only realise the truth when they wake up. So the felt confidence is not a reliable sign. If our way of telling waking from dreaming is itself something the dream can imitate, then we have no test. And if we have no test, premise (3) holds.

A more philosophically interesting reply would say that the argument sets the standard for knowledge too high. We do not need to rule out every possible alternative to know that we have hands. We need only to be confident, on good evidence, that the alternative is not actual. This is a serious response, but it is a different argument — it concedes the dream argument on its own terms and then attacks the standard of knowledge it presupposes. The Moorean reply travels along this line. So even those who reject the sceptical conclusion typically grant that, given Descartes' own demand for certainty, the dream argument succeeds in showing that ordinary perceptual beliefs cannot meet that demand.

So the dream supposition functions as a premise in this way. From the bare possibility that I am dreaming, plus the absence of any test that could rule it out, Descartes derives that I am not justified in confidently asserting any present belief about the external world. The argument is local — it leaves the seemings and the truths of arithmetic alone — but within its target it is persuasive. Doubt about the external world, on Descartes' standard, is rational.

The dream argument is therefore a powerful but bounded sceptical move. It earns its place as one of the two most discussed arguments in the First Meditation. It does not on its own reach radical doubt; that requires the demon. But it shows clearly that the certainty we ordinarily ascribe to perception cannot survive serious scrutiny.
