# 'I am finally compelled to admit that there is not one of my former beliefs about which a doubt may not properly be raised.' How does Descartes reach this conclusion? Are all his former beliefs undermined by the doubts he puts forward, or are some left untouched?

Descartes reaches radical doubt by escalating through three arguments, each one stronger than the last. He starts with the senses, then turns to dreaming, then finally to the supposition of an all-powerful deceiver. In my view the conclusion is genuine: by the end of the First Meditation, nothing among Descartes' former beliefs is left untouched. Even arithmetic and geometry fall under the deceiver's reach. The demon argument does the heavy lifting, and the dream argument prepares the ground.

Descartes opens with the most familiar source of doubt: the senses sometimes deceive us. Things look smaller in the distance, and a stick in water looks bent. So we should not trust completely what has deceived us before. But this argument is too narrow on its own. It only undermines beliefs about distant or small objects, while leaving plain perceptual beliefs ('I am sitting here, by the fire') untouched. To deny these would require taking ourselves to be mad, which Descartes refuses to do — a sane investigator cannot proceed on the assumption that he is insane. So the first argument fails to reach the simplest of our perceptual beliefs.

The dreaming argument is the next move and it is much more powerful. Descartes points out that he has often dreamed familiar scenes — sitting by the fire, holding a paper — that turned out to be illusions. There are no sure signs by which waking can be distinguished from sleep. So for any present belief about his surroundings, it is possible that he is merely dreaming it. The argument can be set out like this. (1) For any sensory belief I have, I cannot rule out that I am dreaming. (2) If I cannot rule that out, the belief might be false. (3) So every sensory belief is doubtful. The reasoning extends beyond a few cases of misperception. It cuts at the entire class of beliefs I take from sense experience.

But Descartes thinks the dream argument has a limit. Even within a dream, certain very general features must hold. Dreams are like paintings: even fanciful paintings must combine real colours and shapes. So even if everything I now perceive is a dream, the basic categories — extension, shape, number, size — must still apply to something. From this he draws the conclusion that physics, astronomy, and medicine are doubtful, but arithmetic and geometry are not. Two and three make five, whether I am awake or asleep. A square has four sides in any world. These transparent truths seem to escape the dream argument.

This is where the third move comes in, and where the doubt becomes radical. Descartes asks how he knows that an omnipotent God has not made him such that he is deceived even when adding two and three. If God could create the world, he could equally have created a mind that goes wrong every time it counts the sides of a square. The natural reply is that God is good and would not deceive us. But Descartes presses this. God allows us to be deceived occasionally — that much is plain from ordinary error. So deception by God is not flatly inconsistent with his nature. And if it is not, then for all I know God might deceive me universally. Even if there is no such God, the situation is no better: I would owe my mind to chance or fate, and a less perfect cause could leave me even more prone to error. Either way, my faculties might be defective in a way I can never detect from the inside.

To press the point home Descartes invents the malicious demon scenario. Suppose some powerful and cunning being has employed all his energies to deceive me. Then the sky, the air, my hands, the very space around me might all be illusions. Crucially, the demon's reach extends to arithmetic and geometry as well. He could make me go wrong every time I add or count, and I would have no way to detect the error. The reasoning is straightforward. If a deceiver of unlimited power is possible, no class of belief is safe from him, because he can affect not only my perceptions but the very faculties by which I evaluate them. The argument can be summarised: (1) For all I know, a deceiver of unlimited power could be making all my beliefs false. (2) If that is so, every belief is doubtful. (3) So every belief is doubtful.

Are some beliefs left untouched? Descartes himself is clear that they are not. He concludes that there is not one of his former beliefs about which a doubt may not properly be raised. The dream argument left arithmetic and geometry standing, but the demon argument knocks those down too. We might be tempted to think that 'two and three make five' is so transparent that no deceiver could falsify it. But the worry is not that the proposition could be false. The worry is that my judgement that it is true could be the product of a defective faculty. A demon need not change the truth-value of arithmetic; he need only mislead me about it. The point is epistemic, not metaphysical, and that is what makes it so cutting.

A famous worry remains. The cogito, which Descartes will reach in the Second Meditation, seems to survive even the demon — because the very act of being deceived presupposes that I exist. But this is not a counterexample to the First Meditation's conclusion. Descartes' claim there is about his former beliefs, the beliefs he held before the meditation began. The cogito is a new discovery, reached by reflection inside the meditation, not a former belief that survived. So the conclusion of the First Meditation stands: every belief he previously held has been called into doubt.

There is also a worry about whether the demon argument is persuasive. One might think that an all-good God would not allow universal deception, and that the no-God horn of the dilemma is also weak — chance might equally produce reliable faculties as defective ones. These are real objections, but they show only that the demon scenario is not certain, not that it is impossible. Descartes only needs the bare possibility of deception to undermine certainty. Once that bare possibility is granted, the conclusion follows.

So Descartes reaches radical doubt by stacking three arguments in increasing strength. The senses argument doubts beliefs based on unreliable perception. The dream argument doubts beliefs based on present sense experience. The demon argument doubts everything else, including the truths of arithmetic. Nothing among his former beliefs is left untouched.
