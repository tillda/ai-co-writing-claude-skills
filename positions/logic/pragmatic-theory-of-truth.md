# Pragmatic Theory of Truth

I reject the pragmatic theory of truth as an account of what truth *is*. Utility, successful inquiry, and convergence are at best fallible *marks* of truth; they are not what truth consists in.

**A note on scope.** Keep separate (i) the pragmatic theory of *truth* — that truth = utility in belief, or what would be endorsed at the limit of inquiry, or warranted assertibility — from (ii) pragmatism *about inquiry* — a respectable methodological view that beliefs should be tested against their consequences. My target is (i). The serious version is the long-run / idealised one; the crude "what is presently expedient" reading is not seriously defended in current debate.

## The strongest version of the view

- A proposition is true iff it would be endorsed by an idealised, error-corrected inquiry carried indefinitely far — equivalently, iff it would be warrantedly assertible under ideal epistemic conditions.
- "Useful" is cognitive utility: explanatory fertility, predictive success, robustness under further evidence — not psychological comfort.
- We have no truth-check outside inquiry; the only sense to be made of "matching the world" is what idealised inquiry settles on.
- The theory need not deny realism in any first-order sense; it claims only that the *concept* of truth, made precise, just is the concept of what survives idealised inquiry.

## Best contemporary arguments for the view

1. **Truth's grip on us is practical.** We care about truth because true beliefs guide successful action; "true" labels whatever property explains that link.
   Response: confusing truth with the *value* of truth. That a property is good to track does not make it identical with the goodness-of-being-tracked.

2. **No view from nowhere.** Every check on a belief is itself an episode of inquiry; the only sense of "matching reality" available to us is inquiry's verdict at the limit.
   Response: again confusing truth with our *access* to truth. Inaccessibility of a vantage point is not absence of what is seen from it.

3. **Convergence is the only metaphysics needed.** Whatever work "facts" do for the correspondence theorist can be done more economically by the limit-of-inquiry idiom.
   Response: confusing truth with the *probability that a belief is true*. Idealised convergence is what raises that probability; "P is likely true" is not the same property as "P is true". Limit-talk also smuggles truth into the word "ideal" — the very thing being analysed.

4. **Naturalistic respectability.** Pragmatism explains why creatures like us evolved a truth-concept: beliefs are states whose accuracy matters for survival, and "true" labels the accuracy that pays.
   Response: confusing truth with the evolutionary *function* of the truth-predicate. We evolved colour vision because surfaces have stable reflectance properties; that does not make red = whatever-aided-foraging.

5. **Anti-metaphysical economy.** Correspondence is saddled with unilluminating relata (facts, propositions). Pragmatism trades these for a relation we already understand — working-out in practice.
   Response: the deflationary (minimalist) account delivers exactly this economy without identifying truth with utility. The lesson is to *deflate* correspondence, not replace it.

## Why I still reject the view

1. **Useful falsehoods, useless truths.** Suppose God rewarded everyone who believed in Santa with eternal life. Belief in Santa would then be massively useful — yet this would not make Santa pop into existence. Equally, vast stretches of mathematical, historical and astronomical fact are true and make no detectable difference to anyone's flourishing. Cats eat mice not because it serves us to think so, but because of cats and mice.

2. **The disquotation objection.** "It is true that P" entails "P", and "P" entails "It is true that P". Pragmatism says "P is true" iff "P is useful to believe" (or "P would be endorsed at the limit"). Combining gives "P iff P-is-useful-to-believe" — manifestly false. The right-hand side concerns our cognitive economy; the left-hand side concerns the world.

3. **The long-run dodge postpones, not solves.** Idealisation to the limit blocks easy useful-falsehood counterexamples by stipulating they would be unmasked given infinite investigation. But "ideal" presupposes truth-tracking — the very property being analysed — and it makes today's truth-claims hostage to a counterfactual no one has any grip on. A placeholder, not an analysis.

4. **Whose utility, when?** The theory needs a determinate right-hand side. *For whom?* Mine collapses into private relativism; ours reproduces plurality across communities; ideal inquirers' hands the answer to a never-realised idealisation. Until this is fixed, the view is the promise of a theory.

5. **Conflating marks with nature.** All the strongest motivations show only that utility and convergence are *symptoms* of truth, not its substance. The pragmatist needs the constitutive claim but only earns the symptomatic one. The reason useful beliefs tend to be true, and convergent inquiry tends to track truth, is that reality rewards accuracy — i.e. there is a truth, prior to inquiry, that explains why inquiry pays off in the first place.

## Considered view

Useful beliefs tend to be true because reality rewards accuracy in the long run; convergent inquiry tends to track truth because it is *responsive* to a world not itself made of inquiry. Treating utility and convergence as our best evidence for truth is sane epistemology. Treating them as the *nature* of truth confuses what truth is *for* with what truth *is*. The minimal account — `<p>` is true iff p — captures everything the truth predicate actually does in our language. If pressed for a metaphysics behind the schema, I lean to a deflated correspondence: truth requires that things be the way the proposition says they are, and that requirement is settled at the world, not at the inquiry approaching it.
