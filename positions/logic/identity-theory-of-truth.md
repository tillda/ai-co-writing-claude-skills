# Identity Theory of Truth (against, in both readings)

The identity theory of truth holds that a true thought is identical with the fact that makes it true. McDowell defends a version; some readings of Frege ("a fact is a thought that is true") attribute it to him. The theory is offered as an alternative to correspondence: if true thought *is* the fact, no relation between them needs explaining.

I reject the theory in both of its readings — the trivializing one and the substantive one — though for different reasons in each case. As a notational variant of correspondence, it collapses into deflationism and adds nothing. As a substantive metaphysical claim, it forces a choice between two unhappy positions: idealising the world, or worldifying the mind.

## The trivializing reading

On the deflationary-friendly reading, the identity theory is correspondence taken to its logical limit. The "correspondence relation" between thought and fact is so tight that the gap closes to zero: a true thought *is* the fact it would have corresponded to.

This reading is consistent with my own deflationism. The minimalist can grant: when the thought that p is true, there is nothing in the world *except* the fact that p that the thought matches up with — so call them identical, if you like. Saying so adds nothing the schema doesn't already say. "The proposition that p is true iff p" already gives us everything: there is no second relation to explain because the minimalist never demanded one.

So on this reading the identity theory is harmless. But it is also empty. It is a way of speaking that delivers no explanatory benefit beyond the schema. Calling thought-and-fact "identical" rather than "trivially related" is a relabelling, not a theory.

## The substantive reading

On the more committed reading — McDowell's — identity theory is a real metaphysical claim: thoughts and facts are made of the same conceptual stuff. The world is conceptually structured ("the world is everything that is the case", as McDowell quotes from the *Tractatus*); thoughts are conceptual; a true thought just *is* a configuration of the world.

This reading has force. It gives a clean answer to questions about the relation between mind and world: the relation is identity, so there is no gap to bridge. McDowell uses this to dissolve scepticism and to vindicate empirical content.

But the reading buys clean answers at metaphysical cost:

1. **It conceptualises the world.** If a fact is the very same thing as the corresponding true thought, then facts have to share whatever structure thoughts have — concept-application, logical articulation, intentional content. This is a strong idealist commitment dressed in moderate clothing. The world becomes thought-shaped because that's what facts are.

2. **It worldifies thoughts (Russellian path).** The alternative — say facts are non-conceptual and thoughts share that non-conceptual character — yields Russellian propositions: structured worldly entities containing actual objects and properties as constituents. This faces all the standard Russellian problems (e.g. how can a thought literally contain Mont Blanc?).

3. **Either way, the unity-of-the-proposition question rebounds.** What makes a fact a fact, rather than a list of constituents? What makes a thought a thought, rather than a sequence of concepts? Identity theory inherits both unity problems and has to answer them simultaneously, since the entity is supposed to be one thing under two descriptions.

These are not knock-down objections, but they show that the identity theory is not a free lunch. It buys its dissolution of the truth-relation by paying with substantive metaphysics that incurs comparable difficulties elsewhere.

## Is it a notational variant of correspondence?

Largely yes, with a twist. The differences from correspondence are:

- **Correspondence:** thought *T* and fact *F* are distinct, and stand in some relation R such that R(T, F) iff T is true.
- **Identity theory:** thought *T*, when true, *is* fact *F*; they are not two things but one.

The identity theory eliminates the relation by eliminating the duality. But the cost is that the duality has to be eliminated *somewhere*, and the natural moves all reproduce correspondence's problems in a new place:

- If thought and fact have the same structure (so identity makes sense), then we owe a theory of how that shared structure works — which is essentially correspondence's compositional axiom problem under a different name.
- If they have different structure, identity is incoherent — they can't literally be one entity if they are differently constituted.

So identity theory is a notational variant of correspondence in the sense that whatever explanatory work correspondence had to do, identity theory has to do too — it just does it under a different label. The "no relation to explain" claim is illusory: the relation is replaced by an identity, but identities between things-of-different-categories are no easier to make sense of than relations between them.

## Honest objections to my rejection

1. **"You're being unfair to McDowell — he isn't claiming a free lunch."** True; McDowell's view is embedded in a broader project (responding to the dualism of scheme and content, etc.) that gives the identity theory motivation beyond the theory of truth narrowly construed. My rejection is local: as a *theory of truth*, identity adds nothing. As part of McDowell's broader epistemology, it has more to recommend it — but those merits don't transfer to truth-theory.

2. **"Frege's identity reading is well-grounded textually."** Granted, but I'm assessing the theory, not its provenance. If Frege held it, that's an additional historical question; it does not bear on whether the theory is defensible.

3. **"Trivializing correspondence into identity *is* the deflationist's victory — why not claim it?"** Because the deflationist doesn't need to. The schema does everything the trivializing identity theory does, without the implication that we have a metaphysical thesis about thought-fact identity. Talking of "identity" suggests we are saying something about the world's metaphysics; the deflationist's view is that no such metaphysical thesis is part of the theory of truth at all.

## Considered view

Reject identity theory of truth.

- In the trivializing form, it's a notational variant of correspondence and collapses into the schema; harmless but empty.
- In the substantive form, it forces a metaphysical choice (conceptualise the world, or worldify thoughts) that the theory of truth shouldn't be in the business of forcing.
- It is not a genuine third option between correspondence and deflationism; it is either the limit case of correspondence or a separate metaphysical claim wearing a truth-theoretic costume.

The slogan: if you want what the identity theorist wants without paying their price, take deflationism. If you want a metaphysics of thought and world, work it out as a metaphysics, not as a theory of truth.
