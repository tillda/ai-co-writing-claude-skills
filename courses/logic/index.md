# Logic (PY1070)

Sainsbury's *Logic* subject guide is the spine of the course; it doubles as the main reading and supplies its own bibliography. Modules below mirror the guide's chapter breakdown, with module 0 added to capture introductory material on validity, monotonicity, and the place of logic in rationality. Where exam questions cluster around an external author (Frege on truth, Tarski's T-schema, Russell on descriptions, Kripke on essentialism, Quine on the analytic/synthetic distinction, Lewis on possible worlds, Edgington on conditionals, Carroll on the justification of deduction), follow Sainsbury's references into those sources.

**Topic emphasis:** ★★★ exam-heavy / ★★ recurrent / ★ peripheral. Stars reflect frequency in past papers (2020-2024) and the supplementary question bank, not philosophical importance — they are a rough guide for time allocation in revision, not for the depth of any individual essay.

## 0. Introduction

What logic is, what it is not, and why it matters. The deductive/inductive contrast, monotonicity vs. non-monotonicity, and the role of logic in rationality. Use this module rarely as a primary scope (most exams ask about specific topics), but pull from it when an essay needs a generic framing of validity or the question 'does logic have a subject matter?'.

**Topics (Sainsbury Introduction §§ 1-3):**
- ★★ Validity — deductive validity vs. inductive strength; what it is for an argument to be valid
- ★★ Monotonicity vs. non-monotonicity — why deductive consequence is preserved under added premises while inductive support is not
- ★★ Transitivity of entailment — and reasons to doubt it
- ★ The importance of logic in rationality and information extraction

**Sources:**
- Sainsbury *Logic* Introduction (sections 1-3)

## 1. What is Logic?

Formal vs. philosophical logic, the language of classical logic (connectives, quantifiers, formulas), the model/proof distinction, and the question of what counts as a logical constant. Use for prompts asking 'what is a logical constant?', 'does logic have a subject matter?', or 'how do we determine the correct logical constants?'.

**Topics (Sainsbury ch. 1, §§ 1.1-1.5):**
- ★★★ Logical constants — what makes an expression a logical constant, topic-neutrality, criteria for getting the list right (2024)
- ★★★ The subject matter of logic — does logic have one, and if so what (2020, 2022)
- ★★ Formal vs. philosophical logic — distinguishing the two enterprises and their respective tasks
- ★★ Models and proofs — the semantic and syntactic conceptions of consequence
- ★★ The language of classical logic — connectives, quantifiers, formulas (background; rarely essay-driving on its own)
- ★ Logic as *a priori* — the Wittgenstein framing (2023)
- ★ Challenges to classical logic — non-classical alternatives at a high level

**Sources:**
- Sainsbury *Logic* ch. 1 (sections 1.1-1.5)
- Batchelor 'Topic-Neutrality', *Mind* 120 (2011)

## 2. Truth

The Liar paradox, Tarski's T-schema, deflationism (disquotationalism, minimalism), correspondence, pragmatism/coherence/identity theories, and bivalence vs. excluded middle. Use for any prompt mentioning 'true', the property of truth, the redundancy theory, the Liar, or whether truth requires a substantive theory.

**Topics (Sainsbury ch. 2, §§ 2.1-2.6):**
- ★★★ Deflationism — disquotationalism, minimalism, the redundancy theory; whether 'true' picks out a substantive property; the role of the predicate if it does not (the most-asked theme: 2022 + many supplementary)
- ★★★ Correspondence — what 'corresponds to a fact' could mean; whether a satisfactory account is available (2020, supplementary recurrence)
- ★★★ The Liar paradox — 'this sentence is false', diagnoses, paracomplete and paraconsistent responses (2023, 2024)
- ★★ Tarski's T-schema — the semantic conception of truth, object language vs. metalanguage, whether the schema exhausts the concept (2021)
- ★★ Bivalence vs. excluded middle — the distinction, and why it matters (cross-references Modules 7 and 9)
- ★ Pragmatism, coherence, and identity theories — typically argued through *en route* to a deflationist or correspondence conclusion
- ★ Frege on truth as the goal of logic — historical framing (2021)

**Sources:**
- Sainsbury *Logic* ch. 2
- Beall & Glanzberg 'Liar Paradox' (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy)
- Glanzberg 'Truth' (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy)
- Frege 'The Thought' (on truth as the goal of logic)
- Dummett 'Truth' (1959)
- Huemer *Knowledge, Reality, and Value* §§5.2.1-5.2.2 (the law of non-contradiction and the law of excluded middle, derived from the meaning of negation; closing caveat on scope-restriction to proposition-expressing sentences)
- Huemer *Knowledge, Reality, and Value* §§5.5.1-5.5.2 (correspondence and the equivalence-schema rejection of pragmatism, coherence, verificationism)

## 3. Modality

Necessity and possibility, modal operators (boxes and diamonds), possible worlds, the *de re/de dicto* distinction, and Lewis's counterpart theory. Use for prompts on possible worlds, whether they exist, modal idioms, the necessity of identity, and the relation between modality and apriority (cross-reference Essentialism module).

**Topics (Sainsbury ch. 3, §§ 3.1-3.2):**
- ★★★ Possible worlds — their existence, what they help us express, whether they explain or presuppose modality (the dominant exam topic in this module: 2020, 2021, 2022, 2024 + many supplementary)
- ★★ The *de re* / *de dicto* distinction — its formulation, and its bearing on the necessity of identity (2021)
- ★★ Necessity of identity — that all true identity statements are necessarily true (cross-reference Module 8)
- ★★ Counterpart theory (Lewis) — its motivation and its costs
- ★★ Modal idioms in English — how to give the logical form of 'must', 'can', 'necessarily'
- ★ Boxes and diamonds — formal modal logic, frame conditions; usually background

**Sources:**
- Sainsbury *Logic* ch. 3
- Garson 'Modal Logic' (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy)
- Lewis *On the Plurality of Worlds* (counterpart theory)

## 4. Conditionals

The English 'if' vs. the material conditional, conditionals and probabilities (the Adams/Lewis 'bombshell'), and subjunctive/counterfactual conditionals. Use for prompts on whether indicative conditionals have truth-conditions, the meaning of 'if...then...', or how to distinguish true from false counterfactuals.

**Topics (Sainsbury ch. 4, §§ 4.1-4.3):**
- ★★★ The material conditional vs. ordinary 'if' — whether '→' captures the natural-language conditional, and whether divergences are merely pragmatic (2020, 2021, 2022)
- ★★★ Truth-conditions for indicative conditionals — whether they have any, and if so what (2024)
- ★★ Conditionals and probabilities — Adams/Lewis on the link between conditional probability and assertibility, the 'bombshell' that no truth-conditional account can deliver it (2023)
- ★★ Subjunctive and counterfactual conditionals — possible-worlds semantics, distinguishing true from false counterfactuals
- ★★ Indicative vs. counterfactual treatment — whether a single semantic theory covers both
- ★ The pragmatics of conditional assertion — Gricean explanations of paradoxes

**Sources:**
- Sainsbury *Logic* ch. 4
- Edgington 'On conditionals', *Mind* 104 (1995)
- Edgington 'Conditionals' (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy)
- Adams 'Subjunctive and indicative conditionals'

## 5. Reference – Names and Descriptions

Russell's theory of descriptions, the Millian/descriptive debate over proper names, existence and ontology, the *de re/de dicto* distinction in belief contexts. Use for prompts on definite descriptions ('the present King of France', 'the man drinking martini'), whether names abbreviate descriptions, how to handle empty names ('Vulcan', 'Pegasus', 'Sherlock Holmes'), or Frege's puzzle.

**Topics (Sainsbury ch. 5, §§ 5.1-5.4):**
- ★★★ Russell's theory of definite descriptions — the analysis, scope ambiguities, and standard objections (recurrent across years and supplementary)
- ★★★ Millian vs. descriptive theories of proper names — what a name contributes to truth-conditions, whether there is more to meaning than the bearer (2020, 2021, 2022)
- ★★★ Empty names and non-existent objects — 'Vulcan', 'Pegasus', 'Zeus', 'Sherlock Holmes', 'the present King of France', 'the planet between Mercury and the Sun' (2023, 2024 + many supplementary)
- ★★ Frege's puzzle — sense vs. reference, identity statements, informativeness
- ★★ Donnellan's distinction — referential vs. attributive use of definite descriptions
- ★★ Existence and ontology — whether 'many things don't exist' can be coherently expressed; non-existent objects as a logical commitment
- ★ The *de re* / *de dicto* distinction in belief contexts — bridges to Module 6
- ★ Rigid designation — the proper-name behaviour across possible worlds (cross-reference Module 8)

**Sources:**
- Sainsbury *Logic* ch. 5
- Frege 'On Sense and Meaning'
- Russell 'On Denoting'
- Donnellan 'Reference and Definite Descriptions'
- Kripke *Naming and Necessity* (selections)

## 6. Non-extensional Language

The limits of extensionality, truth-functionality, intensional transitive verbs ('fears', 'admires', 'thinks about'), substitutivity failure in propositional attitude reports, and empty terms. Use for prompts on whether attitude reports require an intensional language, the Cicero/Tully cases, 'fearing the ex-gangster who is the friendly old man', and whether one can admire Sherlock Holmes given he doesn't exist.

**Topics (Sainsbury ch. 6):**
- ★★★ Propositional attitude reports — substitutivity failure, whether they require an intensional language (2020, 2022 + supplementary)
- ★★ Intensional transitive verbs — 'fears', 'admires', 'thinks about'; non-extensionality in their direct objects (2024 ex-gangster)
- ★★ Empty terms in attitude contexts — admiring Sherlock Holmes; believing about non-existents (2021)
- ★★ Coextensive vs. cointensive expressions — substitutivity in different contexts (2023)
- ★★ Frege-style cases — 'George Eliot' / 'Mary Anne Evans' (supplementary)
- ★ The limits of extensionality and truth-functionality — diagnostic of where classical machinery breaks

**Sources:**
- Sainsbury *Logic* ch. 6
- Forbes 'Intensional Transitive Verbs' (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy)

## 7. Vagueness

The Sorites paradox, borderline cases, many-valued and supervaluational responses, and the threat to excluded middle. Use for prompts on the heap, whether vague predicates pose a serious logical problem, supervaluationism's tenability, and the law of excluded middle under vagueness.

**Topics (Sainsbury ch. 7):**
- ★★★ Excluded middle under vagueness — whether vague predicates pose a serious threat to a universal law of logic (2022, 2024 + supplementary)
- ★★ The Sorites paradox — formulation, what to give up, the epistemicist option (2023)
- ★★ Supervaluationism — its motivation and its tenability as a response (2020)
- ★★ The 'best account of vagueness' as a general question (2021)
- ★ Many-valued logics — degree-theoretic responses (Edgington)
- ★ Borderline cases — what makes a predicate vague in the first place; vagueness vs. ambiguity vs. generality

**Sources:**
- Sainsbury *Logic* ch. 7
- Edgington 'Vagueness by degrees'
- Keefe & Smith *Vagueness: A Reader*

## 8. Essentialism

The relations among necessity, apriority, and analyticity; Kripke's counterexamples to their coincidence (the necessary aposteriori, the contingent apriori); Quine's attack on essential properties and on the analytic/synthetic distinction. Use for prompts on whether the distinctions are independent, whether quantified modal logic commits us to Aristotelian essentialism, and whether contingent truths can be known apriori.

**Topics (Sainsbury ch. 8, §§ 8.1-8.3):**
- ★★★ Necessity vs. apriority (and analyticity) — whether the distinctions are independent (2023, 2024)
- ★★★ Aristotelian essentialism — Quine's attack, Geach's counter, whether quantified modal logic commits us to it (2020, 2022)
- ★★ Kripke's counterexamples — the necessary aposteriori, the contingent apriori
- ★★ Necessity of identity — 'everything is necessarily itself; so all true identity statements are necessarily true' (supplementary; cross-reference Module 3)
- ★★ Quine on the analytic/synthetic distinction — 'Two Dogmas' and the rejection of analyticity as a workable category
- ★★ Contingent *a priori* — whether contingent truths can be known *a priori* (supplementary)
- ★ The concept of analyticity — what would count as a good explanation of it
- ★ Rigid designation in modal contexts — the present Prime Minister case (supplementary)

**Sources:**
- Sainsbury *Logic* ch. 8
- Kripke *Naming and Necessity*
- Quine 'Two Dogmas of Empiricism'
- Quine 'Reference and Modality'
- Grice & Strawson 'In Defense of a Dogma'

## 9. The Justification of Deduction

The challenge of justifying basic logical laws, the threat of circularity in any such justification, Lewis Carroll's 'What the Tortoise Said to Achilles', Dummett's project, and reflective equilibrium. Use for any prompt on justifying excluded middle, modus ponens, or deduction in general; on whether circularity is vicious; on Carroll's regress.

**Topics (Sainsbury ch. 9):**
- ★★★ Circularity of any justification of deduction — whether it is vicious, whether 'rule-circular' arguments are acceptable (2020, 2022, 2024 + supplementary)
- ★★★ Justifying specific logical laws — most often excluded middle (2021 + supplementary)
- ★★ Carroll's 'What the Tortoise Said to Achilles' — modus ponens, the regress of premises and rules (2023 — the '4 o'clock' case)
- ★★ Reflective equilibrium — Goodman's response, mutual adjustment of rules and judgements
- ★★ Dummett's project — anti-realism and the demand for a justification of deductive practice
- ★ Excluded middle as a 'universal truth' or 'law of logic' — whether the very framing is appropriate

**Sources:**
- Sainsbury *Logic* ch. 9
- Carroll 'What the Tortoise Said to Achilles'
- Dummett 'The Justification of Deduction'
- Haack 'Dummett's Justification of Deduction'
- Goodman *Fact, Fiction and Forecast* (on reflective equilibrium)
