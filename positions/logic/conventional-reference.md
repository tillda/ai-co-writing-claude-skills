# Kripkean Rigid Designation (with Use-Theory for Borderline Cases)

Kripke is right. **If an object exists, a proper name of it is a rigid designator** — it picks out the same bearer in every counterfactual circumstance where the bearer exists. Identity statements between two co-referring proper names are therefore necessary, even where they are knowable only *a posteriori*: where two names both rigidly designate the same individual, the identity is true in every world where the individual exists, even though discovering the identity took empirical work. This is the headline of the position: accept Kripke's framework as the correct account of paradigmatic naming.

The three classical arguments for rigidity carry the case against descriptivism:

- **Modal.** "Aristotle could have been a fisherman" is intelligible. If "Aristotle" *meant* the teacher of Alexander, that sentence would be incoherent. So the name does not mean the description; it tracks the man.
- **Epistemic.** "Gödel could have stolen the proof from Schmidt." Even if every belief I associate with "Gödel" turns out to be true of Schmidt, I am still talking about Gödel — because the name's hook into the world was set by an initial baptism propagated through use, not by my descriptive beliefs.
- **Semantic.** "Feynman" refers, even when the speaker can offer no description that uniquely picks him out.

A name is fixed to its bearer by an initial baptism (typically ostensive, occasionally by reference-fixing description) and then propagates through a causal-historical chain of use. That chain is what carries the rigid hook across counterfactual circumstances — not any descriptive content the speaker happens to associate. This rules out descriptivism, the cluster theory, Frege-Russell description theories, and Searle's variant; they get the modal data wrong.

What follows from accepting this much:

- Identity of name with name is **necessary** at the reference layer when both names rigidly designate the same individual.
- There are genuine **necessary *a posteriori*** truths about identity.
- Descriptivism in any of its forms is the wrong theory of *paradigmatic* naming.

## Borderline cases

Like any theory of an ordinary practice, a theory of naming has clean central cases and ragged edges. Nearly all categories have borderline cases; many perfectly good concepts have borderline cases (see `wittgensteinian-conceptualism.md`). The philosophically interesting examples in the literature on naming — Vulcan, Santa Claus, Hesperus and Phosphorus, water = H₂O, Aristotle's parents — are not refutations of Kripke. They are **borderline cases of an otherwise good theory**, and they are best handled by allowing use-theory of naming to rescue the cases that Kripke's machinery alone cannot reach.

The rescue runs along the following four fronts.

**1. Empty names — Vulcan, Santa Claus, phlogiston, ether, the present king of France.** No bearer was ever picked out, so there is nothing for the name to rigidly designate. Kripke's framework requires a successful baptism, and these cases lack one. Speakers nevertheless have rich cognitive content associated with the term — gathered from theoretical roles, descriptions, stories. That content is acquired and transmitted by use. Sentences like "Vulcan is closer to the Sun than Mercury" are then **meaning-deficient** (the conventions try to single out a proposition about a particular individual, but no such individual exists for them to lock onto): we understand the words, but no determinate proposition is expressed, so the sentence is neither true nor false. Use-theory takes over where rigid designation has nothing to grip.

**2. Hesperus and Phosphorus, on the light-mode reading.** The orthodox Kripkean reading takes both names to rigidly designate Venus, making the identity necessary *a posteriori*. There is a competing reading on which the names were actually introduced to track *the bright thing in the evening sky* and *the bright thing in the morning sky* — i.e. light-phenomena, not the planet itself. On that reading, what speakers' practice actually locked onto were the two light-modes, and these are not the same thing: there is a possible world where the morning-light and the evening-light are not produced by the same body. So "Hesperus = Phosphorus" comes out **contingent**. This is a use-tracking reading enabled by treating the case as a borderline one — the apparent bearer (Venus) is not what the practice picked out; the bearers were the phenomena. The orthodox reading is also defensible. The dispute is not over whether unambiguous proper names rigidly designate; it is over **what the practice actually picked out**, which is precisely where use-theory has bite.

**3. Natural-kind terms — water, gold, tiger, lemon.** Kripke and Putnam extend the rigidity framework to natural kinds, making "water = H₂O" necessary *a posteriori*. This extension is genuinely debatable in a way the proper-name case is not. "Water" was acquired by repeated interaction with the watery stuff — rivers, lakes, rain — without any baptism on a hidden chemical structure. Putnam's Twin Earth elicits the intuition "Twin Earth water is not water", but that intuition is itself a piece of use-tracking, not evidence of a rigid hook into chemistry; once told the chemistry, speakers extend the role with a chemical condition. So "water = H₂O" arguably comes out **contingent *a posteriori*** once the use-acquired role is doing the work — though either reading can be defended. Natural-kind necessities are open territory.

**4. Origin essentialism.** Even granting that "Aristotle" rigidly designates the man across worlds, it is a further metaphysical claim that *those particular parents* are essential to him. Rigid designation tells us how the name *tracks*; it does not tell us what is *essential* to the bearer. The argument from rigidity to necessity-of-origin needs an extra premise about what constitutes the individual — a premise rigid designation does not supply. Speakers track Aristotle as a person; the parental origins are background, not constitutive. Necessity of origin does not follow.

## The general lesson

A theory of naming is itself a concept describing a fuzzy ordinary practice. We should expect it to fit central cases cleanly and run into edges. Kripke's theory tracks the central cases — proper names with existing bearers, intact baptism, intact causal chain — better than any rival, and on those cases it is substantially correct. **Kripke is saved for many cases.** Use-theory of naming tracks the edges — empty names, names whose practice locked onto something other than the apparent bearer, natural-kind terms acquired without baptism on a hidden essence. The two are not competitors at the same level; they are partners with different jurisdictions. Holding out for a single naming theory that captures every case in one mechanism is the Lockean error transposed to philosophy of language.

**Usage:** **Headline Kripke.** When the prompt asks about rigid designation, Kripke's framework, the necessity of identity, descriptivism vs. causal-historical theory, or any "is this necessary or contingent?" question about names, present the modal, epistemic, and semantic arguments seriously and accept rigid designation for paradigmatic proper names with existing bearers. The essay should reflect that **Kripke is correct for the central cases** and that descriptivism gets the modal data wrong. Use-theory of naming enters as the **borderline-case rescue** — never as an outright rival. Deploy it in one or more of these four fronts as the prompt invites: empty names (Vulcan, Santa Claus, phlogiston) where no bearer means no rigid hook; Hesperus / Phosphorus on the light-mode reading where the practice arguably tracked phenomena rather than the planet, making the identity contingent; natural-kind terms where the rigidity extension to "water = H₂O" is debatable and either reading can be defended; origin essentialism, which simply does not follow from rigid designation. When the prompt is squarely on Frege's puzzle, substitutivity, or attitude reports, defer to `names-conventional-content.md` for the cognitive-content side; when on modality more broadly, defer to `primitive-modality.md` and invoke this position only briefly. Always engage Kripke's actual arguments before introducing borderline-case framing — the examiner expects the canonical position to be taken seriously, and on the proper-name reference layer it is substantially correct.
