# What the Second Meditation contributes to mind–body distinctness

**Question:** Descartes insisted that he did not argue that the mind is distinct from the body until the Sixth Meditation. What, then, does the Second Meditation contribute to his argument for the claim that mind and body are distinct substances?
**Answer:** It establishes an *epistemic* asymmetry — thinking is the property the meditator cannot doubt of himself, while corporeality is something he can doubt — and identifies a class of mental properties that fall under his self-conception independently of any bodily content; this asymmetry becomes a premise the Sixth Meditation needs but cannot itself supply.

Descartes's insistence that the real distinction between mind and body is not argued until the Sixth Meditation is dialectically important. It saves him from the charge — pressed by Arnauld and Hobbes — that he tried to derive a metaphysical conclusion from the cogito alone. But the disclaimer also raises the question the prompt asks: if the Second Meditation does not argue for the distinction, what does it contribute? In my view, it contributes two things, both of which are needed by the Sixth Meditation argument and neither of which the Sixth Meditation could establish on its own. First, it sets up the epistemic asymmetry between mental and corporeal properties under the doubt. Second, it characterises the meditator's mental life in a way that excludes corporeal content. Both are inputs the later argument depends on.

To see the contribution clearly we need to recall what the Sixth Meditation argument actually requires. The Sixth Meditation argues something like this: I have a clear and distinct idea of myself as a thinking, non-extended thing; I have a clear and distinct idea of body as an extended, non-thinking thing; if I can clearly and distinctly conceive of two things as separate, God can make them separate; therefore mind and body are really distinct substances. This argument needs at least two pieces of material before it can run. It needs the meditator's clear and distinct idea of himself as a thinking thing, and it needs the meditator's clear and distinct idea of body as an extended thing. The ideas have to be *available* to the meditator before the Sixth Meditation can use them. The Second Meditation is where these ideas are produced.

Take the meditator's idea of himself first. The Second Meditation argues, after the cogito, that nutrition and movement and sense-perception in the full sense depend on the body, and therefore fall under the doubt; thinking, by contrast, does not. The meditator finds that what is inseparable from him, under the doubt, is thinking — doubting, understanding, affirming, denying, willing, imagining, having sensory experiences in the restricted sense. The list of properties that survive the doubt is therefore a list of mental properties. This list, and the fact that nothing corporeal appears in it under the doubt, is the input the Sixth Meditation needs. The clear and distinct idea of the meditator as a thinking thing is the result of this exercise.

This is the epistemic asymmetry I mentioned. Under the doubt, the meditator can know his thinking but cannot know his bodily nature. The asymmetry is not yet a metaphysical claim; it is a claim about what the meditator can know about himself given the supposition of total deception. But the asymmetry has the right structural shape for the Sixth Meditation argument to exploit. If thinking can be conceived without any reference to bodily properties — as the Second Meditation shows, since the meditator can fully characterise his thinking life under the supposition that he has no body — then the concept of thinking and the concept of corporeality come apart at least conceptually. Whether they come apart in reality is what the Sixth Meditation will later argue, on the basis of the clear-and-distinct-perception criterion validated in the Third.

The second contribution is the characterisation of mental life that the Second Meditation provides. The list of mental properties — doubting, understanding, willing, imagining, sensing in the restricted sense — defines, for Descartes, what thinking is. It is essential to the Sixth Meditation argument that this characterisation does not include any corporeal content. If thinking, properly described, were essentially tied to the body (as Aristotle thought, for instance, regarding the lower psychic functions), then the Sixth Meditation argument would not get off the ground; one could not clearly and distinctly conceive of a thinking thing that lacks bodily properties. The Second Meditation pre-empts this by carefully showing that all the mental properties on the list can be characterised without reference to the body. The meditator can imagine, will, doubt, understand, have seemings, all under the supposition that he is bodiless. Whether this characterisation is correct is itself disputable. But within Descartes's framework, the characterisation produced in the Second Meditation is what makes the Sixth Meditation move possible.

There is a third, smaller contribution that is worth noting. The Second Meditation also distinguishes sensing-as-experience from sensing-as-perception. Descartes emphasises that even if I am asleep, I certainly *seem* to see, hear, and feel heat. The seeming is purely mental. The full sensing — really seeing, really hearing — requires a body. This distinction, drawn explicitly in the Second Meditation, will be used in the Sixth to argue that the mental side of sensation can survive without the body, and so the body is not essential to the meditator's nature. Without the Second Meditation's distinction, the Sixth would have to make this move on the fly.

What the Second Meditation does *not* contribute is the metaphysical conclusion. Descartes is correct that the Sixth Meditation does the metaphysical work; the Second only sets up the materials. The materials are: a self-conception in terms of mental properties only, an epistemic asymmetry between mental and corporeal access, and a distinction between mental seemings and full sensory perception. These are the inputs. The Sixth Meditation then combines them with the clear-and-distinct-perception criterion (validated in the Third) to argue that the mental and corporeal are really distinct.

Whether the Sixth Meditation succeeds is a separate question. Hobbes pressed the worry that the cogito does not rule out a corporeal thinker, and Descartes's reliance on clear and distinct perception to bridge the conceptual to the metaphysical depends on the Third Meditation's proof of God's reliability. Both moves are open to objection. But the Second Meditation's role in the larger argument can be assessed independently. Its role is preparatory and conditional. *If* the Sixth Meditation argument works, it does so because the Second has done its preparatory job. If the Second had failed to provide a self-conception in purely mental terms, no later argument could have salvaged the distinctness claim.

So the Second Meditation's contribution is twofold and entirely structural. It produces the meditator's self-conception as a thinking, non-extended thing, with a list of properties that under the doubt are characterised without bodily reference. And it sets up the asymmetry between mental and corporeal that the later argument relies on. Descartes was right to deny that the Second Meditation argues for the distinctness. But he should also have acknowledged that without the Second Meditation, the Sixth Meditation argument would have nothing to work on.

A natural follow-up question — though one the prompt does not strictly require — is whether the contribution itself is on solid ground. The list of mental properties produced under the doubt looks honest, but it depends on Descartes's framing of the doubt and on his decision to subtract anything bodily from the meditator's self-conception. A different framing — one on which the meditator's self-presentation is treated as evidence about how a thinking creature appears to itself rather than as evidence about what kind of substance it is — would yield the same list without the dualist conclusion. The Second Meditation's contribution to mind-body distinctness is therefore real on Descartes's terms but optional on others. A physicalist can accept everything the Second Meditation establishes and reject what the Sixth Meditation tries to build on it, since the conceptual asymmetry between mental and corporeal properties does not yet show that the corresponding things are different substances.
