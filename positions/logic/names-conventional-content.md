# Names and Conventional Content (Use-Theory for the Borderline Cases)

Kripke's rigid-designator theory is correct for the central cases of naming (see `conventional-reference.md`): a proper name of an existing bearer rigidly designates that bearer in every counterfactual circumstance where the bearer exists, and identity statements between two such co-referring names are necessary. The use-theoretic account of conventional content set out below is **not a rival** to that view at the reference layer. It is the **rescue for the borderline cases** — the cases where Kripke's machinery alone leaves something puzzling unexplained. The puzzles of naming that fascinate philosophers (Frege's puzzle, substitutivity failure in attitude reports, empty names) are precisely such borderline cases of an otherwise good theory.

## The use-theory in brief

Language is a system of conventions — instructions we learn through use — that tell us which proposition a sentence is supposed to express. Meaning does not live at the level of the bare references of the parts. A sentence has a determinate meaning when the conventions, together with speakers' intentions, express a proposition; it is meaning-deficient when they over-constrain or under-constrain.

Applied to proper names: a name contributes its **conventional role** — the public practice through which it was introduced and sustained — alongside its bearer. A speaker who has acquired "Hesperus" has acquired the role *the bright thing in the evening sky*; that is what she grasps and what figures in the proposition expressed by sentences containing the name. Two co-referential names can carry different roles, having been introduced through different practices, so the propositions assigned to sentences containing them can come apart even when reference matches.

This is **not descriptivism**: the role is not a description the speaker has in mind, but a public convention attaching the name to its bearer through use. It is not pure Millianism either: bare reference does not fix what proposition a sentence expresses. The thesis is about **conventional content**, and is layered on top of the Kripkean reference relation, not in competition with it.

## Borderline cases the use-theory handles

**Frege's puzzle.** "Hesperus = Phosphorus" is informative; "Hesperus = Hesperus" is trivial. At the reference layer, on the orthodox Kripkean reading, the two names co-refer to Venus and the identity is necessary. Informativeness then has to live somewhere — and it lives at the content layer: the two names express different roles (evening-sky bright thing / morning-sky bright thing), so the propositions carried by the two sentences are different. Kripkean rigidity at the reference layer is preserved; the puzzle dissolves at the content layer. (A more radical reading, sketched in `conventional-reference.md`, takes the names to track *light-modes* rather than the planet, which would make the identity contingent. That reading turns Frege's puzzle into a borderline case at the reference layer too. Either treatment is available; both are use-theoretic rescues.)

**Substitutivity failure in attitude reports.** "Lois believes Superman flies" can be true while "Lois believes Clark Kent flies" is false, even though "Superman" and "Clark Kent" co-refer. The cognitive content differs because the conventional roles differ — Lois has acquired the names through different practices and treats them as picking out different things. Substitution salva veritate fails not because Kripkean rigidity fails, but because attitude reports are sensitive to **content**, and content is role-based.

**Empty names — Vulcan, the present king of France, the round square.** No bearer exists, so there is nothing for the name to rigidly designate. Kripke's machinery requires a successful baptism; these cases lack one. The conventions try to single out a proposition about a particular individual, but no such individual exists for them to lock onto. Sentences like "Vulcan is closer to the Sun than Mercury" and "the present king of France is bald" are **meaning-deficient**: we understand the words and the structure, but no determinate proposition is expressed, so the sentences are neither true nor false. This is not the same as meaningless — speakers can debate "what Vulcan was supposed to be like" because the role is real even though the bearer is not. Use-theory has the resources to mark this difference; rigid-designation theory by itself does not.

**Fictional names — Pegasus, Sherlock Holmes.** Possibly a different case from empty names: the convention can attach to a determinate entity in legend or fiction, so a proposition does get expressed within the fiction. Use-theory has the resources to handle this; it falls naturally out of the role-account.

## Why this is a rescue, not a replacement

Kripke's framework gets the modal data right for paradigmatic proper names. Use-theory does not undo that. What use-theory adds is an account of the **content** speakers actually grasp and transmit, which is where the borderline cases of naming generate their puzzles. Where Kripkean rigid designation has no purchase — empty names, content sensitivity in attitude contexts — use-theory takes over. Where rigid designation does have purchase — counterfactual identity, modal arguments — the role-account simply describes what the practice picked out, and rigid designation describes how it is then tracked across worlds. The two layers do different work and they cooperate.

The general lesson tracks the broader pattern from `wittgensteinian-conceptualism.md`: theories of naming are themselves concepts, and good concepts have fuzzy edges. The central cases of naming are Kripkean. The edges — empty bearers, content sensitivity, possibly Hesperus / Phosphorus on the light-mode reading — want use-theory.

**Usage:** Use-theory of names is the **borderline-case rescue**, not a rival to Kripke. When the prompt is squarely on Frege's puzzle, attitude reports, or empty names, this position can be the conclusion — but always present **Kripkean rigid designation first** as the correct theory of paradigmatic naming, then introduce conventional content specifically as the account of cognitive content that handles the cases where rigid designation alone leaves a gap. When the prompt is on rigid designation, descriptivism, natural-kind terms, or origin essentialism, defer to `conventional-reference.md` and invoke this position only as the content-layer companion. Cover the canonical analytic answers (Mill, Frege, descriptivism, direct reference) seriously before introducing the use-theoretic move; the examiner expects the canonical theories to be taken seriously.
