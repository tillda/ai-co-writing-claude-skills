# 14 Expressive Language


Some language is expressive; either by the way or primarily, it expresses feelings and/or attitudes. There are purely expressive utterances of the sort we saw in Chapter 6, but there are also expressive particles such as "goddam" that occur as parts of grammatical sentences. Moreover there are whole sentences that may or may not be used literally but that sometimes noncancelably express attitudes; the chief examples of those figure in irony, including and especially in sarcasm, and in pejorative utterances.

It is clear that these phenomena are neither illocutionary nor merely cases of conversational implicature. There are two general approaches to verbal irony, an "echoic" theory and a pretense theory. They share their main tenets, and it becomes even harder to distinguish them when we realize that an ironic utterance need be neither literally echoic nor literally a pretense. Irony requires tacit allusion to some pre-existing belief or attitude, and it expresses a dissociative belief or attitude toward the pre-existing one, but on top of that there is only a loose collection of typical features.

Sarcasm is a much more specific form of verbal irony. Elizabeth Camp argues that it requires a "meaning inversion," though in a very broad sense of "meaning" that includes illocutionary force and mere implicatures.

Pejorative language comes in several types. Racial and ethnic slurs provide the richest examples. According to the "received view," a slur is a denoting expression that conventionally implicates a negative belief or attitude in one or another sense of that technical term. But the received view faces several strong objections.

As you will have noticed, the entire tradition in Anglo-American philosophy of language is geared toward fact-stating and the communication of true or false beliefs. Use theorists and Austin had that very trenchant complaint to make against Verification and Truth-Condition Theories, and it could be applied even against Grice's psychological-communicative view. Recall objection 1 against verificationism (Chapter 8) and especially objection 1 against Davidson (Chapter 9). We have seen that illocutionary forces vary widely and there are many different kinds of speech act. What is more: Many perfectly normal utterances do not have any illocutionary force and are not propositional at all; recall the simple Wittgensteinian examples I used in introducing Use Theories.


In considering the objection against Davidson, I noted that according to some theorists there are even perfectly grammatical declarative sentences that are not in the true-false, fact-stating business. And even if moral judgments in particular are true or false and not "only expressive, evincings or ventings semantically like groans, grunts of protest, cheers, and the like," it is uncontroversial that some utterances are evincings, ventings, groans, grunts of protest, cheers, and the like. Still more importantly, parts and aspects of otherwise grammatical sentences have expressive features that must be explained by a theory of meaning.

## The Expressive per se

Let us start with ventings and cheers. Never mind grunts and groans, because typically they are not words at all; but lots of other ventings are articulate: Even "Ouch," as opposed to a yelp or mere cry, is a word of English, listed in dictionaries as an exclamation or interjection. "Ha, ha," as opposed to an actual laugh, is a verbal expression, often used ironically (I have seen "Ha bloody ha" in a British novel). "Damn!" "Hooray!" ("yahoo," "woo-hoo"). "Uh-oh." "Yuck." "Good gracious." "Boo-hoo." "Shame!" "Oh, dear." "Thank you." "Oops." "Amen." About time we returned to these, Wittgenstein would say.

One can try to understand exclamations and interjections as elliptical. "Thank you" surely does abbreviate the pure performative "I [hereby] thank you." And as noted in Chapter 12, Lewis (1970: 57-8) proposed to analyze "Hurrah for Porky" as "I cheer Porky"; but when I just shout "Hooray!" it does not seem I have made a performative utterance having propositional structure determined by hidden syntactic construction. "Damn!" is a better candidate: A linguist might show that there is a tacit direct object—we do nearly equivalently say "Damn it"—and possibly that there is a syntactically "higher" subject, "God" (though the latter idea is refuted syntactically by Quang (1971)). The utterance could further be a hortatory subjunctive, "Let/may God damn [whatever]." But that is not the normal use; sincere utterers of "Damn" need not believe in God, nor urge that He actually condemn a particular object to Hell.

There is no reason to insist that all the examples be treated in the same way: Perhaps "Ouch" and "Gracious" are just exclamations, and even a truth-condition theorist can admit them as meaningful by courtesy. Perhaps "Hooray" or "Damn" do actually have underlying propositional structure, though that would still have to be shown by positive argument. And perhaps some of the cases just do counterexample verificationist and truth-conditional theories taken as explaining all meaning.

But some expressions of this sort are subsentential. "Damn" can function as an adjective contributing syntactically to a containing sentence: "That damn cat has pooped on your pillow"; "He hasn't done a damn thing." In such sentences, "damn" is not merely an interjection; “My son has married—damn!—a new-age guru” is fine, but “My son has married damn a new-age guru” is just ungrammatical. Likewise for “goddam,” and as before, “That goddam cat has pooped ...” does not (even metaphorically) mean “That cat has been condemned to Hell by God and has pooped ...” “Goddam” is expressive only, despite its quite determinate syntactic role.


Following David Kaplan,¹ I shall call such linguistic items—that is, those that are words of particular languages and have syntactic properties but only express attitudes or other mental states and do not seem to contribute content to propositional meaning—“expressives.” They form a surprisingly wide and diverse category. We shall return to them below, but first I shall take up an expressive style of speech that is not covered either by speech-act theory or by purely Gricean conversational pragmatics.

## Irony and Sarcasm

Grice contended that irony and sarcasm are simply cases of conversational implicature. That claim is embarrassed by several facts about sarcasm, but let us first consider verbal irony more generally. (I assume that irony is the wider category of which sarcasm is a special case; but irony is diverse, and we should not assume that irony has a single correct analysis.) The obvious example of irony that is not sarcasm is ironic understatement:

**`(1)`** Karbunkle’s not the smartest kid on the block.

Two other types of nonsarcastic irony:

**`(2)`** [’T]is not so deep as a well, nor so wide as a church-door; but ’tis enough, ‘twill serve... [Mercutio, having received his death wound, Romeo and Juliet, III. i. 96.²]

**`(3)`** He’s a genius, you know/after all. [Where the speaker is not implying that the man is stupid. The idea is only that the man overrates his intelligence.]

Some other examples of irony:

**`(4)`** Where are the Thought Police when you need them? [Said when someone has expressed a fairly outrageous political view.]

**`(5)`** Hegelians, who love a synthesis, will probably conclude that he [the present King of France] wears a wig. [Russell in “On Denoting,” previously quoted in note 6, Chapter 2.³]

**`(6)`** Lady, do you think if you sit there long enough it’ll turn some color other than red, yellow or green? [Traffic cop to inattentive driver halted at a stop light.]

(7) Don’t go even a mile over the speed limit, now. [Said to a neurotically cautious driver; cf. Wilson (2006).]

**`(8)`** A revolution is a dinner party after all! [Playing off Mao's famous saying, "A revolution is not a dinner party."]

**`(9)`** Nice try, pal. [Said to someone who has been, e.g., defending a thesis that is really indefensible.]

**`(10)`** What does our commander do? Shoots himself in the arse—doubtless in an attempt to blow his brains out. [Brigadier Shelton on General Sir William Elphinstone, just before the British withdrawal from Kabul in 1842. Shelton added, perhaps unnecessarily, "He can't have missed by much."]

**`(11)`** Today was a very cold and bitter day, as cold and bitter as a cup of hot chocolate; if the cup of hot chocolate had vinegar added to it and were placed in a refrigerator for several hours. [Lemony Snicket: The Unauthorized Autobiography.]

It would be hard to reconstruct each of these as inviting Gricean inferences: "He has said that p; ..." For one thing, in cases like *`(5)`* and *`(11)`*, there is no obvious implicatum. The ironic aspect of the utterance seems only to express an attitude, not to convey more information. Moreover, the attitude expressed is a normative attitude or at least references a social/cultural norm.

There is a very general idea that irony "draw[s] attention to some discrepancy between a description of the world that the speaker is apparently putting forward and the way (she wants to suggest) things actually were" (Wilson 2006: 1722; sarcasm would be a particularly direct, nearly limiting case). But of course there are many ways of doing that other than verbal irony. Two more specific accounts have emerged.

Sperber and Wilson (1981) made use of a concept we have not yet encountered: that of "echoic" usage. Sometimes in speech we echo someone else's utterance. A: "First I went to the bicycle shop"—B: "The bicycle shop, got it, where next?" Such echoing normally indicates that the hearer has noted the speaker's utterance and is at least briefly thinking about it: "The bicycle shop. Do they sell helmets there?" And sometimes we do the echoing in a critical or mocking tone: "You went to the bicycle shop; huh/a likely story/you'd like me to believe that/and I'm Rex, the Wonder Horse," or just "You went to the bicycle shop" uttered with a frankly disbelieving expression on our face. Sperber and Wilson suggested that verbal irony is an echoic "allusion," the echoing of a (usually imaginary) thought or utterance tacitly attributed to someone else, where its point is to express a dissociative attitude and/or to exhibit the falsity, visibility, inadequacy, or other infelicitousness of the thought or utterance echoed. In ironic understatement such as *`(1)`*, the speaker is echoing someone who does not appreciate how stupid Karbunkle really is. *`(2)`* is a bitter response to Romeo's "Courage, man; the hurt cannot be much." *`(3)`* echoes the subject's self-assessment. In *`(4)`*, the speaker is echoing a loyal citizen of a totalitarian state, the discrepancy being that s/he is not and could never be one, implying hyperbolically that the opinion in question is extreme enough to warrant suppression by force.


In passing and quietly, Grice had acknowledged that irony and sarcasm do not fit his original model. In particular, for those cases it is inaccurate to begin the interpretive reasoning with "He has said that $p$"; the speaker has only "made as if" to say that $p$ and only "purports to be putting forward" that proposition (1975: 34). Inspired by this, Clark and Gerrig (1984), Recanati (2004), and Currie (2006) have offered pretense accounts of irony, according to which the speaker's whole utterance is not the speech act it purports to be; the speaker is only pretending to assert or ask etc. what her/his sentence does. (Recall the pretense views of Walton (1990) and Crimmins (1998), discussed in Chapter 4.) The speaker is mocking or otherwise dissociating from one who might use the same sentence with the force it superficially looks to have. 

Thus, an utterer of (1) is making-believe s/he does not appreciate how stupid Karbunkle really is, 
Mercutio is pretending to agree with Romeo's callous throwaway, 
an utterer of (3) is pretending to share the subject's self-assessment, 
the utterer of (4) is pretending to be a loyal citizen of a totalitarian state reacting to a forbidden opinion, and so forth.

It has been wondered by how much the echoic view and the pretense view differ. They share their most important tenets: (i) Grice's original model was wrong, because an ironic utterance is not a genuine assertion, question, or the like. (ii) Grice's model was wrong, because irony involves the expression of an attitude, not merely the conveying of extra information. (iii) The attitude is dissociative.

Moreover, we cannot understand "pretense" in a very strong literal sense. An utterer of (1) is not really pretending in Austin's full-bore way, nor in uttering *`(5)`* was Russell out-and-out pretending to do anything or to be any kind of person. Rather, we must understand it as what Crimmins called "shallow" pretense, merely "talking as if," and that sharpens the question of how the pretense view differs from the echoic view. A further problem is that both echoing and pretending indisputably do sometimes figure in irony; some ironic utterances are full-bore pretending, with deliberate vocal and facial imitation. Does it matter whether Russell, in uttering *`(5)`*, was scripting the comic role of a Hegelian or merely echoing a line satirically attributed to Hegelians?

But the paradigms do differ. Grice (1978) actually had made a nice little point in favor of pretense: An ironical utterance cannot be prefaced by "To speak ironically" (in the way that a metaphorical one may be by "To speak metaphorically"), because "while one wants the pretense to be recognized as such, to announce it as a pretense would spoil the effect" (p. 54); but there is nothing wrong with "To echo [utterance U], ..."

Moreover, the pretense theorist may complain that echoic "allusion" to a merely possible utterance is precisely not echoing; it is at best "as if" echoing, i.e., guess what, pretense.

Yet Wilson and Sperber argue that the echoic paradigm is more fundamental, for pretense alone does not suffice. I can pretend to assert what is an absurdity without achieving irony, if the absurdity comes out of nowhere:

**`(12)`** The first penguin to achieve a standing broad jump of 3 meters will be named Karbunkle.

Even if I uttered *`(12)`* in a conspicuously ironic, “dripping” tone, the response would be just a puzzled “What?”, precisely because no one had said or was even imagined to say anything at all about penguins or track and field.⁶ (Though an utterance can be out of the blue and still ironical, if it is recognizably attributable to a particular person, group of people, or culture, or even to people in general.) So pretense theorists have had to add the allusive or attributive feature ad hoc.

Wilson (2006) further argues that talk of pretense is appropriate only when there is some element of mimicry or simulation of the hypothetical speaker’s delivery. Where only the abstract propositional content is being held up for scrutiny and there is no hint of imitation, as in *`(8)`* or *`(9)`*, there is no pretense. But this seems too strong, since “shallow” pretense, mere “talking as if,” does not require mimicry.

A better point would be that in some of the examples, such as *`(9)`* and *`(11)`*, there is genuine commitment to, if not outright assertion of, the truth of all or part of what is uttered. That is not mere making as if to assert.

Indeed, the ironic aspect of an utterance sometimes targets only a small part of what is being genuinely said:

**`(13)`** As I reached the bank at closing time, the bank clerk helpfully shut the door in my face. [Wilson 2006: 1736]

This would be a perfectly genuine assertion, not a pretend one, though it may be responded that the speaker is pretending at least to have found the clerk’s action helpful rather than the reverse.

Where are we at this point? Actual echoing is not required for irony, and, it seems, neither is pretense in any strong sense. At best, there must be echoic “allusion,” which is not echoing and is pretense only in a very weak sense. And, contrary to pure theories of either type, an ironic utterance may constitute a genuine speech act, indeed may have the force characteristic of the sentence taken literally. So we have one necessary feature, normative expressiveness, perhaps a second one, echoic allusion, and a few other typical features, but no clear criterion. Indeed, I say only “perhaps” the echoic requirement, because in *`(9)`*, *`(10)`*, and *`(11)`*, it is nontrivial and a bit strained to identify an echoic referent.

But as I noted at the outset, irony is a broad and somewhat diverse category, very likely not a natural kind, and it may admit of no adequate general treatment. It may still be that sarcasm, a special case of irony, can be more crisply characterized.

### Sarcasm

The paradigm case of sarcasm is a speaker's uttering a declarative, meaning the opposite (not just the negation) of what the sentence means, in an ironic tone, thereby expressing a normative attitude.

**`(14)`** You're a fine friend.

Of course, questions and imperatives and exclamations can be sarcastic, and the ironic tone is not required. Nor is there a sharp line between actual sarcasm and irony more broadly; *`(4)`*, *`(5)`*, *`(6)`*, and *`(8)`*, for example, might be considered borderline cases.

But one thing that is distinctive of sarcasm is that there are syntactic marks of it (and that is fatal to the conversational implicature analysis). Consider:

**`(15)`** A fine friend you are.

*`(15)`* differs from (14) only in word order, but cannot be heard as sincerely nonsarcastic. And sarcastically used sentences can contain negative polarity items such as “ever” and “any,” which normally occur only in syntactically negative sentences, even when the sentences used are not negative:

**`(16)`** Sure, you ever paid your debts on time.

**`(17)`** Oh, right, you were going to give any money to Veterans for Bertrand Russell.

**`(18)`** Thanks for inviting me to the meeting or even letting me know there was going to be one. [From a bitter note once received by a department chair from a colleague.]

*`(16)`*-*`(18)`* can be heard only as sarcasm; the message is noncancelable. And Camp (2012) calls our attention to the sarcasm indicator “like”:

**`(19)`** Like that’s a good idea. [p. 14]⁷

Camp goes on to propose that sarcasm always involves “meaning inversion”—as when the utterer of *`(14)`* means the opposite of what s/he says, but also in a much broader sense of “meaning” which includes implicatures and illocutionary force. More specifically:

> **`[5]`**arcastic utterances presuppose a normative scale; they pretend to undertake (or at least, evoke) one commitment with respect to this scale; and they thereby communicate some sort of inversion of this pretended commitment. The sarcastic pretense can take four different “scopes.” 
>
> Propositional sarcasm [as in *`(14)`*] targets and inverts a proposition that would have been associated with a sincere assertion of the uttered sentence; 
>
> lexical sarcasm [as in *`(13)`*] targets just a single expression or phrase within the uttered sentence; 
>
> “Like”-prefixed sarcasm [as in *`(19)`*] targets the focal content of an embedded declarative sentence; 
>
> and illocutionary sarcasm encompasses the entire speech act that would have been undertaken by a sincere utterance.
>
> >
>
> **(pp. 20-1, italics original)**

An example of the last would be

(20) Could I entice you to eat another small slice? [To someone who has already scarfed most of the pizza (Kumon-Nakamura, Glucksberg, and Brown 1995: 4).]

(Let us pass over Camp's appeal to pretense, and let that part stand or fall with whatever we may decide on the echo/pretense issue for irony generally.)

To give an example of how the model works for something other than the paradigm case, here is one in which the sarcasm targets, not the proposition expressed by the sentence uttered, but only one of its implicatures:

(21) A: I'm sorry Aunt Louisa is such a bother.

B: Oh, she never stays for more than a month at a time, and she always confines her three cats to the upper two floors of our house. (p. 22)

Here B makes a genuine assertion and speaks truly, but “evokes a scale of ease and burdensomeness of guests and pretends to implicate that Aunt Louisa’s described actions place her at the easy end of the scale, but thereby implicates the contrary proposition: that her visits are quite burdensome” (ibid.).

And here is an illocutionary example.

(22) Thanks for holding the door. [To someone who has let the door slam in the speaker’s face.]

Here the normative scale is that of politeness, on which holding the door ranks high, and by pretending to thank, the speaker has drawn attention to how low the addressee’s action ranks on that scale, how undeserving of thanks it was.

As of this writing, Camp’s analysis is the most thorough and sophisticated I know. And certainly her general idea of inversion is a good thing to mean by “sarcasm.” I leave its further assessment to you.

## Pejorative Language

Pejoratives come in several different kinds. (This list will be far from exhaustive.) (i) Vague adjectives like “goddam,” and ones like “rotten,” “stinking,” and “lousy,” which are more specifically negative but only slightly so. It seems adequate to classify them as expressives in Kaplan’s sense. (ii) Fairly vague words for people we dislike or disapprove of: “jerk,” “bitch,” “bastard,” “a-hole,” “creep,” “s-head.” Those are expressive, but they are not expressives, for each has some referential content, applying only to persons (“I was seated next to a creep” entails “I was seated next to someone"), and some have at least further connotations: "bastard," for example, implies being unscrupulous. (iii) Quite specific expressions that add normative assessment to otherwise perfectly descriptive concepts: "He played at being department chair"; "President McKinley was assassinated by Czolgosz" (only important people can be assassinated) and for that matter "McKinley was murdered by Czolgosz" (it is sometimes said that "murder" simply means "wrongful killing," but that is not obvious). (iv) More specific pejorative terms for people of certain dispreferred types. "Moron," "idiot," and "dolt" have stupidity as part of their meanings; "nutcase," "lunatic," and "psycho" rate their referents' mental health; "slut," "sleazeball," and "slime" refer to people of variously bad character. (What is it about words beginning with "sl-?") (iv) Impolite, often offensive terms for people of certain religions, ideologies or political stripes. (v) Derogatory expressions for sexual orientation and/or preferences. (vi) And of course, racial and ethnic slurs.


Let us consider slurs in particular, for they exhibit a rich and problematic set of features, and a good theory of them will probably apply back to pejoratives of simpler types.

"Kike," "chink," "kraut," "frog," "limey," "canuck," "honky," the unutterable "n-r," and others without end all over the globe. They denote types of people, where the types are ethnicity, race, or nationality, but they also express negative attitudes toward and/or unflattering beliefs about people of that type as such. They are also offensive, some more so than others.

What I just said is already a bit controversial. It has been denied that slurs do denote,⁸ and it is generally agreed that such words can be used without expressing anything negative. So let us speak of the "received view,"⁹ and consider several objections.

But first, how should we understand the negativity? Does someone who uses "frog," as in "My new assigned roommate is a frog," merely express negative feelings toward French people, exactly as if she were to say "My roommate is French" in a disappointed tone and make a face, or does she convey a belief however vague, as in "My roommate is French [implied: and so does not deserve complete respect]"? On the latter interpretation, the slur would be generating a conventional implicature on at least a weak interpretation of that ambiguous term. Let us not try to decide that issue, except to note that slurs are conventional words, not merely ordinary words accompanied by sneers or slight spitting sounds.

On to the objections. Let us start with the claim that slur words can sometimes be used without derogatory implications.

### Objection 1

If true, that claim would seem to refute the conventional implicature model, since conventional implicatures are noncancelable. There are cases of three sorts. But the first is easily set aside: the use of slurs in mock insults. Friends can rail at each other using terms that are normally very insulting, without giving offense. “That’s just like you, you evil s—t” (said with a smile). But mock insults are mock *insults*; they work precisely because the words used are derogatory.


Second, Hom (2008) and Anderson and Lepore (2013) call our attention to “appropriation” or reclamation projects, in which uses of a slur such as “n—r,” “dyke,” or “queer,” at least by members of the relevant oppressed groups in particular contexts, can be rendered unpejorative and even positive. Sometime during the 1960s, African-Americans very self-consciously reclaimed “black,” which had been a slur, under the slogan “Black is beautiful,” and I believe “black” is even now no slur though “African-American” may be preferred.

### Reply

But to appropriate or reclaim a slur in that way is to change its meaning, as if we were to decide to use “but” in such a way as to imply no contrast. If a hard-core truth-condition theorist should insist that conventional implicatures are not part of meaning and “but” is precisely synonymous with “and,” we can put the point by saying that a reclaimed slur is a *former* slur, derogatory no longer, at least in the new dialect of the oppressed group.

However, third, Hom (2008), Hornsby (2001), and Richard (2008) have adduced more troublesome examples.

**`(23)`** He is *not* a n—r. [Meant to chide a racist speaker who has used the word.] [Hornsby 2001: 129]

**`(24)`** He said that I was a n—r. [Spoken by an African-American.] [Richard 2008: 13] (Or better, “He called me a n—r.”)

**`(25)`** Institutions that treat Chinese people as chinks are racist. [Hom 2008: 429]

Notice that even if we understand these uses of slurs as really only mentioning and commenting on the slur words rather than as pejorative (and obviously the respective speakers are not intending them as derogatory), merely voicing such words is usually taken to be offensive whether or not the speaker has a slurring intention. Anderson and Lepore take this to show that slurs are taboo words, and not just implicature generators. It remains to be explained why *`(23)`*–*`(25)`* do *not* carry the offensive implicatures, if indeed they do not.

### Objection 2

Croom (2011) argues that since according to the received view “the terms *African American* and *n—r* have the same literal meaning, ... [it] seem[s] committed to the view that racist claims such as ‘African Americans are n—s’ literally express analytic truths that are knowable a priori” (p. 352). We might add that T-sentences encounter a similar problem: “‘Xiang is a chink’ is true iff Xiang is a chink” will roll off Davidson’s assembly line just as will “‘Xiang is Chinese’ is true iff Xiang is Chinese,” and together they entail (worse) “‘Xiang is Chinese’ is true iff Xiang is a chink.” Proponents of the received view must swallow these consequences and defend them by pointing out that strict “literal” truth is not what matters in regard to offensiveness. Or, at this point one might abandon the received view in favor of the Conjunction Theory, according to which the pejorative content is actually, semantically a conjunct in the proposition expressed; but that view is effectively criticized by Saka (2007: 123ff).


### Objection 3

The received view implies that for every slur there is a neutral counterpart, a term that refers to the same class or type of people but is not derogatory or offensive. We do have “African-American,” “Chinese,” “French,” and others. But DiFranco (2015) calls our attention to *compositional slurs* and argues that they do not always have neutral counterparts. For example, “slant-eye” literally means a person having eyes with epicanthic folds, or at least eyes that slant, but the slurring use of that term does not literally entail concepts of those kinds. Ashwell (2016) argues that for some slurs, such as “slut,” there are no *non-normative* counterparts.

### Objection 4

Williamson (2009), himself a proponent of the received view, anticipates a very pertinent objection: that since implicature of any sort is a matter of what is communicated by a public speech act in a social context, an account featuring it will not carry over to silent, private thought; yet we can and do think pejoratively.

### Reply

Williamson maintains that pejorativeness is still essentially communicative, and that there are communicative phenomena in thought, “in silent communication with oneself” (2009: p. 155); “one manipulates the rhetorical effect of one’s own thoughts on oneself.” I cannot tell whether Williamson thinks there is a deeper level of thought that is flatly literal and merely propositional. But notice that any expressive, positive or negative, pejorative or not, can occur in thought. (A silent “Hooray!”) This is a genuine issue that deserves at least a long separate discussion by philosophers of mind. It will not get that discussion here, though it will recur in the next chapter.

### Objection 5

As I have noted, slurs vary in their degrees of negativity, or at least in their degrees of offensiveness. To contemporary Americans, “n–r” is probably the worst of all, while “frog” though impolite is not so bad, “limey” has lost what sting it may have had in the nineteenth century, and it would be hard to find an American who minds being called a yank. The received view does nothing to explain such differences, which are consequential to say the least.

### Reply

Indeed they are consequential, socially so; I expect there are even legal differences between “n–r” and “limey.” But those differences are not themselves linguistic even though they are differences between words. What explain them are, rather, general social and especially historical facts (Saka 2007: 148). Popa-Wyatt and Wyatt (2017) offer some detailed and plausible explanations of just that sort, based on the relations between discourse roles and power imbalances.

### Rejoinder

That reply insists on a narrow sense of “linguistic,” and a use theorist would and should reject it. There is not so sharp a difference between meaning and use broadly speaking.

## Summary

- Some language serves to express feelings and/or attitudes.

- Apart from mere exclamations, there are two main categories of expressive language: verbal irony, and pejoratives.

- There are two general accounts of verbal irony, the “echoic” theory and a pretense theory.

- Each of those theories is onto something, but each as it stands is too strong.

- Sarcasm is a much more specific form of verbal irony. Elizabeth Camp argues that it requires a “meaning inversion,” though in a very broad sense of “meaning” that includes illocutionary force and mere implicatures.

- According to the “received view” of slurs, a slur is a denoting expression that conventionally implicates a negative belief or attitude in one or another sense of that technical term.

- But the received view faces several strong objections.
