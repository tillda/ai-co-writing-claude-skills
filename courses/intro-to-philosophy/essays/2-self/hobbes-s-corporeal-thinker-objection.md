# Hobbes's corporeal-thinker objection In connection with the Second Meditation, Hobbes said that it was possible that something that thinks should be something corporeal. Do Descartes' arguments succeed in ruling this possibility out?

In my view Descartes's arguments do not succeed in ruling out Hobbes's possibility. Hobbes's point is that nothing in the cogito or the surrounding reasoning of the Second Meditation forces us to conclude that the thinker is non-corporeal. The cogito establishes that something thinks; it leaves entirely open what kind of thing that something is. Descartes's attempts to close the gap rest on an inference from "I do not know that I am corporeal" to "I am not corporeal", and that inference is illegitimate. Once the inference is exposed, the corporeal-thinker possibility is not ruled out — and contemporary evidence in fact strongly supports it.

Hobbes's objection is structurally simple. The cogito gives us: thinking is going on; therefore something exists that is doing the thinking. This is fine. Descartes then identifies this something with himself, and, after considering what he can affirm about himself under the doubt, concludes that he is "in the strict sense, only a thing that thinks" — a wholly mental, non-corporeal substance. Hobbes objects that this last step is not earned. Granted that something thinks, and that we can call this something "I", nothing in the argument shows that this thinking thing is not a body of some kind. The argument requires us to add the further claim that thinking is incompatible with being corporeal, and the cogito does not give us that claim.

To see the force of the objection, consider what Descartes actually concludes from. He has set aside everything he could doubt. The body, on the malicious-demon hypothesis, can be doubted; therefore he sets aside the assumption that he has a body. Thinking he cannot doubt; so thinking remains. He then concludes that he is, in the strict sense, only a thinking thing. But Hobbes can object: the fact that you set the body aside under the doubt does not show that the body is not there. It only shows that, under the constraints of the doubt, you cannot affirm it. If a thinking thing turned out to be a body, that body would still be there even though you have not yet been entitled to assert it. The doubt rules out *affirming* corporeality at this stage; it does not rule out corporeality *itself*.

Descartes has a reply, which appears in the Sixth Meditation but is foreshadowed in the Second. He argues that what he clearly and distinctly perceives can be made to exist by God in just the way he conceives it. He clearly and distinctly perceives himself as a thinking thing, without conceiving any extension as part of his nature. Therefore God could create him as a thinking thing without extension. Therefore he is a thing whose nature does not include extension — that is, a non-corporeal thing. So the corporeal-thinker possibility is, after all, ruled out.

This reply does not work. The trouble is in the move from "I conceive myself without conceiving extension" to "I am a thing whose nature does not include extension". Conceiving myself without conceiving extension shows only that my idea of myself does not contain the idea of extension. It does not show that *I* do not contain extension. To assume otherwise is to assume that my idea of myself is *complete* — that it captures every essential feature of what I am. But the very point at issue is whether the idea is complete. If I am in fact a corporeal thinker, then the idea I have of myself in the Second Meditation, formed under the deliberate exclusion of bodily features, is incomplete. Descartes cannot use the incompleteness-stripped idea to prove that the bodily features do not belong, because the question is precisely whether they belong.

This is just Arnauld's objection in another form. Arnauld pointed out that one might clearly and distinctly perceive that a triangle has three angles and yet not perceive that it has any other geometrical properties — for instance, that the square on the hypotenuse equals the sum of the squares on the other two sides, in the case of right-angled triangles. The triangle has the further property whether or not it appears in the clear-and-distinct perception. Similarly, the thinker may have the property of being extended whether or not it appears in the clear-and-distinct perception of himself as a thinker. So the inference from "not in my idea" to "not in me" fails, and Hobbes's possibility is not closed off.

There is a second strand in Descartes's defence: the appeal to the qualitative difference between thought and extension. Thought, Descartes says, is essentially private, intentional, indivisible; extension is essentially public, geometrical, divisible. How could a single thing have both kinds of property? The reply is that this is a question, not an argument. It is a real and difficult question how thinking and extension are related in one entity. But asking the question does not establish that no answer is possible. The history of physics is full of cases where two seemingly incompatible properties turn out to belong to one thing — light is wave-like and particle-like, electrons are localisable and non-localisable depending on the description. The mere fact that thinking and extension look qualitatively different does not establish that they cannot belong to a single corporeal thinker. It just means we need an account of how they do.

In favour of Hobbes, the contemporary evidence is overwhelming. Damage to the brain produces specific deficits in thinking, memory, and emotion. Drugs alter conscious experience in predictable ways. Brain activity correlates tightly with mental activity. None of this is decisive on its own — the dualist can always say the brain is a transmitter or instrument of the immaterial mind — but the explanatory burden has shifted heavily. The simplest hypothesis is that the brain just *is* the thinker, and the activities we call thinking are activities of the brain. This is precisely the corporeal-thinker possibility Hobbes opened up.

I prefer a version of physicalism on which the mental is a process the physical is engaging in, rather than a separate substance. Thinking is what brains do, in the way fire is what fuel does under combustion. This honours the reality of the mental — there is something it is like to think, and we are not just describing a physical mechanism — without positing an extra immaterial substance. The corporeal thinker is not just possible; it is the most plausible thing for the thinker to be.

In conclusion, Descartes's arguments fail to rule out Hobbes's possibility. The move from "I do not affirm of myself that I am corporeal under doubt" to "I am not corporeal" is not valid; the appeal to clear and distinct perception begs the question of whether the perception is complete. Hobbes was right that the cogito leaves the corporeal thinker entirely open, and the right response to Descartes is to take that possibility seriously.
