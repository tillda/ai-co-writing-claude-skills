# Introduction to Philosophy (PY1020)

Single-textbook course built around *Reading Philosophy* (Guttenplan, Hornsby, Janaway, Schwenkler; Blackwell, 2021). Each module corresponds to one seminar and one or two chapters of that book. Examination questions are tightly tied to the textbook's selections, so essays should default to the textbook authors and only reach beyond when the prompt explicitly asks for it.

## 1. Doubt & The Self

Cartesian skepticism, the method of doubt, the cogito, and Moore-style common-sense replies. Use whenever the essay touches Descartes, the *Meditations*, the existence of the self, or the problem of distinguishing dreaming from waking. Discussions of the cogito's circularity, what 'I' refers to in 'I think therefore I am', and the relation between doubt and self-knowledge belong here.

**Sources:**
- Reading Philosophy ch. 1 (Descartes' First Meditation)
- Reading Philosophy ch. 2 (Descartes' Second Meditation; cogito; the self)

## 2. Tragedy

Hume on the paradox of tragedy and the conversion of the painful into the pleasurable; Feagin's contemporary reply. Use for any prompt about why we enjoy tragic art, the moral or epistemic value of tragedy, or the 'subordination of passions'.

**Sources:**
- Reading Philosophy ch. 3 (Hume *Of Tragedy*; Feagin response)

## 3. Dilemma

Moral dilemmas as genuine conflicts of obligation. Lemmon's framework, Foot's revisitation, Nussbaum's tragic question. Use for any prompt about whether moral dilemmas can be resolved, whether one is bound to act wrongly, or whether dilemmas reveal a defect in moral theory.

**Sources:**
- Reading Philosophy ch. 4 (Lemmon 'Moral Dilemmas'; Foot and Nussbaum in commentary)

## 4. Friendship

Aristotle's account of *philia* in *Nicomachean Ethics* book 8 alongside Sarah Stroud on epistemic partiality in friendship. Use for prompts about whether friendship requires partiality (epistemic or moral), the relation between justice and friendship, or whether friends are obliged to bias their beliefs.

**Sources:**
- Reading Philosophy ch. 5 (Aristotle NE bk. 8; Stroud on epistemic partiality)

## 5. Equality

Bernard Williams' 'The Idea of Equality' against Robert Nozick's entitlement theory of justice. Use for prompts about distributive justice, whether wealth-based distribution of medical care or legal protection is irrational, or libertarianism and its critics.

**Sources:**
- Reading Philosophy ch. 6 (Williams *The Idea of Equality*; Nozick *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* selections)

## 6. Identity

Locke on personal identity through consciousness/memory, and Bernard Williams on 'The Self and the Future'. Use for prompts about what makes a person at one time the same as a person at another, body-swap and brain-transplant cases, and whether psychological or bodily continuity is fundamental.

**Sources:**
- Reading Philosophy ch. 7 (Locke *Essay* II.xxvii; Williams *The Self and the Future*)

## 7. Freedom

Schopenhauer on the grounds of the will and Susan Wolf on real moral freedom. Use for prompts about free will, determinism, moral responsibility, or whether genuine freedom requires the ability to do otherwise.

**Sources:**
- Reading Philosophy ch. 8 (Schopenhauer *On the Freedom of the Will*; Wolf *Sanity and the Metaphysics of Responsibility*)

## 8. Consciousness

Nagel's 'What is it like to be a bat?' against Churchland's reductive materialism. Use for prompts about the subjective character of experience, qualia, the explanatory gap, or whether consciousness can be physically explained.

**Sources:**
- Reading Philosophy ch. 9 (Nagel *What is it like to be a bat?*; Churchland reply)

## 9. Causality

Hume on causation as constant conjunction and Anscombe's case for causation as observable. Use for prompts on necessary connection, the regularity theory of causation, or whether causation is a relation we perceive or merely infer.

**Sources:**
- Reading Philosophy ch. 10 (Hume *Treatise* on causation; Anscombe *Causality and Determination*)

## 10. Qualities

The primary/secondary quality distinction from Locke, with Boyle's corpuscularianism in the background and Berkeley's idealist objections. Use for prompts about whether colour, sound, or taste are 'in' the object, whether the distinction collapses under Berkeley's pressure, or how qualities relate to scientific description.

**Sources:**
- Reading Philosophy ch. 11 (Locke *Essay* II.viii; Berkeley *Three Dialogues* selections; Boyle in commentary)
