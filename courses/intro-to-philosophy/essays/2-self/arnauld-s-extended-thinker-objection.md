# Arnauld's extended-thinker objection 'Although I clearly and distinctly know my nature to be something that thinks, may I not perhaps be wrong in thinking that nothing else belongs to my nature apart from the fact that I am a thinking thing? Perhaps the fact that I am an extended thing may also belong to my nature' (Arnauld). Does Descartes have a satisfactory response to Arnauld's objection?

In my view Descartes does not have a satisfactory response to Arnauld's objection. Arnauld puts his finger on the central weakness of the Cartesian argument for mind–body distinctness. From the fact that I clearly and distinctly perceive myself as a thinking thing, it does not follow that thinking is the *only* thing essential to me. I might also be essentially extended, and my clear and distinct perception, formed under conditions designed to exclude awareness of body, would not show this. Descartes's reply — that I clearly and distinctly perceive myself as a *complete* thinking thing — only restates the conclusion he is trying to defend. So the objection stands, and the inference from clear and distinct perception to real distinctness fails.

The objection is worth stating in full. Descartes's argument for mind–body distinctness depends on a key inference. He clearly and distinctly perceives himself as a thinker, without perceiving extension as belonging to him. He concludes that he is a thing whose nature does not include extension — that is, a non-corporeal thinker really distinct from any body. Arnauld's worry is about the gap between "I do not perceive extension as belonging to me" and "extension does not belong to me". The first is a fact about the content of my perception; the second is a fact about my nature. They are not the same, and the move from one to the other requires that my perception capture every essential feature of what I am. But how do I know my perception is so complete?

Arnauld presses the point with an analogy from geometry. Take a right-angled triangle. Someone might clearly and distinctly perceive it as a three-sided figure with one right angle, and form no perception of any further property. Yet the triangle has the further essential property that the square on the hypotenuse equals the sum of the squares on the other two sides. This property belongs to its nature whether or not the perceiver notices it. The perceiver's clear and distinct grasp of the triangle as right-angled does not exclude the further property; it just leaves it out. By parity, my clear and distinct perception of myself as a thinker does not exclude the further property of being extended; it just leaves it out. The Cartesian inference treats absence-from-perception as evidence of absence-from-nature, and that is exactly what cannot be assumed.

Descartes's main reply, in the Fourth Replies, runs as follows. The triangle case is not a good analogy. There the perceiver has only an *inadequate* grasp of the triangle. By contrast, when I perceive myself as a thinker, I perceive myself as a *complete* thing — a thing capable of existing on its own, with no further essential properties needed to make sense of what I am. A thinker does not need extension to be a thinker, in the way the triangle does need its further property to be the triangle it is. So my perception is not merely incomplete in the way Arnauld imagines; it is a perception of a complete substance. And from a clear and distinct perception of a complete substance, the inference to real distinctness is licensed.

This reply does not succeed. The trouble is that it relies on a premise that Arnauld is precisely contesting — namely, that the perception is of a complete thing. How does Descartes know that his perception of himself as a thinker is a perception of a complete substance, rather than a partial perception of something more? Asserting that the perception is "complete" is not the same as showing that it is. The completeness claim is just the distinctness claim under another name, smuggled in to justify the inference.

To see this, consider what would have to be true for Descartes's reply to work. He would need some independent way of telling, from inside the act of clear and distinct perception, that nothing further essential to him is being left out. But there is no such test. The perception itself only tells us what it represents, not what it leaves out. To assume otherwise — to assume that whatever I do not perceive in a clear and distinct conception of myself is therefore not essential to me — is to assume the very omniscience about one's own nature that Descartes is supposed to be earning. The reply turns out to be circular.

A second strand of the reply is the appeal to God. Descartes argues that whatever he can clearly and distinctly conceive, God could create in just that way. So if he can conceive himself as a thinker without extension, God could create him as a thinker without extension, and therefore extension is not essential to his nature. But this argument has the same defect. The premise that "God could create him as I conceive him" requires that my conception captures everything essential. If my conception is incomplete — if I am, in fact, essentially extended but my conception leaves this out — then "God could create me as I conceive me" is false: God could not create the thing I am while leaving out one of its essential features. So once again the argument depends on the completeness of the perception, and that is exactly what Arnauld is questioning.

The objection is also strengthened by what we now know about thinking. There is overwhelming empirical evidence that thinking depends on the brain in highly specific ways. Damage to particular regions reliably affects particular cognitive functions; chemical changes alter mood and reasoning; activity in particular neural systems correlates with particular conscious experiences. None of this is a knock-down argument against substance dualism — the dualist can always reinterpret correlations — but it provides exactly the kind of independent reason to think that extension is, after all, part of the thinker's nature. Arnauld's objection is not merely a logical possibility; it is the most plausible reading of what we now know.

The better view, I think, is that the thinker is a particular brain, and thinking is what that brain is doing. Extension belongs essentially to the thinker because the thinker is a body. Mental properties are processes the body engages in — patterns in its activity, in the way fire is a pattern in the activity of fuel under combustion. This view honours the reality of mental life without positing a separate immaterial substance. Arnauld was right to suspect that extension belongs to the thinker; the right development of his suspicion is full physicalism.

In conclusion, Descartes does not have a satisfactory response to Arnauld. His attempt to distinguish the thinker case from the triangle case rests on an unsupported claim that the perception of the thinker is a perception of a complete substance. That claim is exactly what is at issue. The appeal to God's creative power inherits the same defect. Arnauld was right: from the fact that I do not include extension in my clear and distinct perception of myself, it does not follow that extension is not part of my nature. And, as the empirical evidence now strongly suggests, extension is in fact part of the thinker's nature. Descartes's argument for distinctness fails at exactly the point Arnauld identified.
