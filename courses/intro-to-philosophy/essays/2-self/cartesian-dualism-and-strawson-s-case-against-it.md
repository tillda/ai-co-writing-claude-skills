# Cartesian dualism and Strawson's case against it Is dualism compatible with ordinary thought and talk about persons?

In my view, dualism is not compatible with our ordinary thought and talk about persons, and Strawson's case against the Cartesian succeeds. The reason is straightforward. We talk about a single thing — a person — and we ascribe to it both physical predicates ("she is six feet tall") and mental ones ("she is depressed"). The Cartesian wants to split this single subject into two substances joined by a relation. But once the split is made, the unity cannot be put back together. Strawson is right that the concept of a person is more basic than the concepts of mind and body, and that the Cartesian's project of building "person" out of "mind" plus "body" cannot be carried through. The essay also addresses the further question of whether Cartesians are committed to a dualist reduction of the person, and whether such a reduction is possible.

Cartesian dualism is the view, defended by Descartes in his Second Meditation, that a human being is composed of two substances of wholly different kinds: an immaterial thinking thing and a material extended thing. The mind has thoughts, doubts, and sensations; the body has shape and location. They are joined causally — Descartes infamously suggested through the pineal gland — but each is, in itself, what it is independently of the other. On this picture, the soul could in principle exist without the body. Mental properties belong to the soul alone; physical properties to the body alone. A person is a composite of these two, with the "real" self typically identified with the soul.

Strawson argues that this is the wrong shape of theory, and that the right shape begins from the concept of a *person*. A person, in Strawson's sense, is a single subject to which we ascribe both physical predicates ("she weighs sixty kilos") and what Strawson calls predicates of consciousness ("she is in pain", "she believes it is raining"). Notice that we use the same "she" in both ascriptions. The same thing is heavy and is in pain. This is the core datum of ordinary talk about persons, and any account of what a person is must explain it. Strawson's claim is that the concept of a person is primitive — it cannot be analysed as the concept of a body plus the concept of a mind, because that analysis cannot generate the unity of the subject. We do not arrive at "person" by first identifying a body, then identifying a mind, and then conjoining them. We start with a person.

This brings us to the reduction-analysis argument. The Cartesian, Strawson holds, is committed to thinking that the concept of a person can be reduced to or analysed in terms of two more basic concepts: a body on one side and a mind on the other. After all, if a person *is* a composite of two substances, then talk of persons must in principle be re-expressible in talk of the two components. But the reduction cannot be carried through. The Cartesian's "mind" is supposed to be individuated independently of any body — it is a pure thinking thing. Yet we have no criteria for identifying or counting such pure thinking things. We have no way to say what makes the mind I refer to here the same mind, or a different mind, from one mentioned in some other context. Without such criteria the Cartesian's mind is not a genuine particular at all, and the analysis cannot get off the ground.

Now consider ordinary talk. We say without effort: "She felt the cold and shivered", "He remembered the conversation and frowned". These run mental and physical predicates together as belonging to one subject. The Cartesian must paraphrase them, splitting each into two: a soul that felt the cold and willed a shiver, and a body that received a stimulus and moved in response. But the original sentence does not feel like two sentences glued together. It treats one person as the agent of both the feeling and the shivering. The Cartesian reconstruction leaves us with what Ryle calls "two collateral histories" — one mental, one bodily — and no good account of how the same "she" picks out something in each. Our ordinary talk simply does not work that way.

This is the difference Strawson is pointing at when he says the disagreement between the Cartesian and his opponent is a difference of view about the relation between the concept of a person and the concept of a person's mind. For the Cartesian, the concept of a mind is logically prior: minds are the basic mental things, and persons are derivative composites. For Strawson, the concept of a person is logically prior: persons are basic, and a person's mind is just a way of talking about that person's mental aspect. The Cartesian thinks "person" can be built up out of "mind" and "body". Strawson thinks the building goes the other way.

The better positive view is that the mental is a *process* the physical engages in, not a separate substance. Consciousness stands to the brain roughly as fire stands to fuel: real, but not a further item. We need not posit a soul to recognise that what brains do is not exhausted by physics, just as we need not posit a fire-substance to recognise that combustion is not exhausted by talk of carbon. This dissolves the Cartesian's interaction problem and respects the unity of the person that ordinary talk presupposes.

In conclusion, dualism cannot be made compatible with ordinary talk about persons. The Cartesian is committed to a reduction of the person to two substances, but the reduction cannot be performed: there are no criteria for individuating the pure mental substance the Cartesian needs. Ordinary talk treats one person as the bearer of mental and physical predicates alike, and Strawson is right that this requires the concept of a person to be primitive. The right response is not to repair Cartesianism but to abandon it.
