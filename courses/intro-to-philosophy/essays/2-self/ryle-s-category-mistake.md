# Ryle's category mistake What are Ryle's grounds for claiming that Cartesian dualism commits a 'category mistake'? Are they good grounds?

In my view Ryle's grounds are largely good. The diagnosis correctly identifies the structural error in Cartesianism: treating the mind as a further item, on the same logical level as the body, when it should be understood as the way the body is operating. Where Ryle goes too far is in his positive proposal — a strict logical behaviourism that reduces all mental talk to talk of dispositions to behave. But the negative argument, that Cartesianism rests on a category mistake, succeeds and is the right starting point for any serious account of the mind.

A category mistake, on Ryle's account, is the error of treating something as belonging to one logical category when it actually belongs to another. He gives several examples to make the structure clear. A foreigner is shown around Oxford — the colleges, the libraries, the playing fields, the museums — and at the end asks where the University is. The visitor has assumed that the University is one more building of the same kind, alongside the colleges and libraries. But it isn't. The University is just the way those buildings are organised and run together. There is no extra item to find.

A second example: a child watches a parade of battalions, batteries, and squadrons, then asks when the division will appear. Again, the division is not a further unit alongside the battalions; it just is the battalions and squadrons in formation. A third: a foreigner watching cricket asks who is responsible for team-spirit, having seen the bowler, the batsmen, and the fielders. Team-spirit is not a further player. It is the keenness with which the players carry out their tasks. In each case the mistake is to expect an extra entity where the activity of the entities already in view is what one is asking about.

Ryle's claim is that Cartesianism makes exactly this kind of mistake about the mind. When we say someone is intelligent, or that they are thinking, or that they are angry, the Cartesian assumes that we must be referring to events in some inner mental theatre, a "ghost in the machine" that runs alongside the body and causes its movements. But, Ryle argues, this is to misread the logic of mental-conduct talk. To call someone intelligent is not to report a hidden inner episode; it is to characterise the way they perform certain tasks. To say that someone is thinking through a problem is, in part, to say something about how they are behaving — what they are working on, what they would do if asked, what they would say. Cartesianism takes mental concepts and treats them as if they referred to extra items, parallel to bodies but immaterial. That is the category mistake.

Are these good grounds? In my view, yes — though with a qualification.

Consider the structure of the Cartesian inference. Cartesianism begins with the unproblematic observation that what we describe as intelligent behaviour cannot be redescribed in purely physical or mechanical terms. From this, the Cartesian concludes that there must therefore exist something non-physical alongside the body to be the cause of intelligent behaviour. This inference is exactly parallel to concluding that, since the University is not exhausted by the description of any one college, there must be a further building called "the University" hidden somewhere. The conclusion does not follow. It is open to us — and Ryle thinks it is correct — to say that mental-conduct talk operates at a different logical level from talk of physical episodes, without therefore having to posit a second realm of episodes. What looks like a need for a second substance is really only a need for a second *vocabulary*.

Ryle reinforces the point with what we may call the absurdity argument. If Cartesianism were correct, the only access we would have to other people's minds would be through inference from their behaviour, since the inner episodes are by hypothesis private. But, Ryle observes, our actual practice presupposes that we *do* know other people's minds reasonably well — we describe them as intelligent or stupid, prudent or foolish, kind or cruel, and we routinely correct ourselves when we get such descriptions wrong. If Cartesianism were correct, this entire practice would be suspect; we could never have learned to make such judgements at all. Yet we plainly have learned. So whatever account is correct, it cannot be one on which mental life is locked away in a private chamber to which only the subject has access. This is a strong argument. It does not just complain about the Cartesian picture; it shows that the picture is incompatible with our actual ability to use mental-conduct concepts.

One natural objection is that Ryle's argument shifts the question rather than answering it. Granted that Cartesianism is the wrong picture, what *is* the mind? If it is not an inner ghostly substance, what is it? Ryle's own answer — that mental terms are dispositional, picking out tendencies to behave in certain ways — has well-known problems. It struggles to accommodate the felt quality of conscious experience: there is something it is like to be in pain, and this seems to be more than a disposition to wince. So a critic can grant the negative case against Cartesianism and still insist that Ryle has not given us a satisfactory positive theory.

This is a fair worry, but it does not undermine the negative case. Even if Ryle's behaviourism is the wrong replacement, the diagnosis of Cartesianism as a category mistake stands on its own. The right replacement, in my view, is to treat the mental as a process that the physical engages in — the brain's activity, organised in a particular way — rather than as a separate substance running parallel to the body. On this view, the mind is to the brain roughly as the University is to the colleges: real, but not a further item. Ryle's negative point is preserved without his positive theory's costs.

In conclusion, Ryle's grounds are good. Cartesianism does treat the mind as if it belonged in the same logical category as the body — a further item alongside it — and this is a mistake of exactly the kind he describes. His behavioural-dispositional alternative is not the only or best replacement, but the diagnosis is correct, and any acceptable theory of mind must respect it.
