# The Humanity Formula Captures What's Right about Agent-Centred Constraints

Kant's second formulation of the Categorical Imperative — act so that you treat humanity, whether in your own person or in that of another, always as an end and never merely as a means — is the most defensible Kantian formulation. It does explanatory work the universal law formula struggles to do, it grounds the language of agent-centred constraints and rights, and it explains the most discussed contrast in modern moral philosophy (Trolley vs. Footbridge) cleanly. The position credits the formula with this work, while noting honestly that it is not a complete moral theory and that some cases lie outside its reach.

The argument:

- **What the formula captures.** People are not mere tools. To make another person part of your plan of action without regard to their choices and goals is a distinctive moral wrong. Note the precision of the wording: not "treat as a means" (everyone uses everyone else as a means in trivial ways — the customer at the till is using the cashier as a means to the transaction) but "treat *merely* as a means". The defining feature is the absence of regard for the other person's autonomy, characteristically signalled by the absence of consent. When consent is given (the lender consents to the unrepaid loan), the formula is satisfied even though the other person remains part of your plan.

- **The Trolley/Footbridge contrast.** In the standard Trolley case, diverting the runaway trolley away from five and onto one does not use the one person as a means to saving the five. The plan succeeds whether or not the one person is on the side track — if he were absent, the diversion still saves the five in the same way. He dies as a foreseen but not used-as-means consequence. In the Footbridge case, by contrast, pushing the heavy person off the bridge to stop the trolley uses his body as the means of stopping the trolley — the plan only succeeds because his body is there to be used. The formula explains, on principled grounds, why the verdicts differ in two cases that consequentialism collapses into one.

- **The Doctrine of Double Effect formalises the same intuition.** Intentional harm, where the harm is the end aimed at or the means to that end, is harder to justify than foreseen but unintended harm. The wartime distinction between deliberate civilian targeting (a war crime) and collateral damage from striking a legitimate military target (subject to a proportionality test but not absolutely prohibited) is the standard application. The doctrine has the same shape as the humanity formula: what matters morally is not just outcomes but the structure of the agent's intention.

- **The formula grounds rights.** A right is a constraint on what others may do to a person without that person's consent — paradigmatically, not to interfere (negative rights) or to provide some good (positive rights). The connection to the humanity formula is direct: respecting a person as an end requires respecting the constraints that protect their standing as a chooser, which is what rights articulate. Rights are also agent-centred: respecting them is a constraint on what *I* may do, not a target to be maximised across the population. (You cannot violate one right to prevent two future violations by others — that would be to use the first person as a means to a future-harm-minimisation plan.)

- **Honest qualification: not everything fits.** The formula does not handle every counter-example to consequentialism. The Electrical Accident case (a man caught in TV-station equipment will suffer for an hour unless the World Cup broadcast is interrupted) cannot be analysed in means/end terms — the trapped man is not being used as a means to anyone's entertainment; the entertainers are simply unwilling to bear a moderate cost to relieve his pain. So the formula does much, but not all, of the deontological work. A complete view needs additional principles (a duty of easy rescue, or a fairness constraint on imposed costs).

- **Trolley as a remaining puzzle for the rights-version.** When rights are invoked alongside the humanity formula (you may not violate the one's right to life), the position predicts that trolley diversion is impermissible. A non-trivial number of people share that intuition. But the more common verdict is that diversion is permitted, which suggests that the rights extension of the formula needs further refinement — perhaps via the means/end distinction itself (the one's death is foreseen, not intended-as-means), perhaps via thresholds. This is unfinished business for the position, not a refutation of it.

- **Why I reject the rivals.** Strict consequentialism collapses Trolley and Footbridge into a single case and produces the wrong verdict on Footbridge. The strict universal-law formulation handles the cases it handles by self-undermining-of-maxim, which is a less natural fit for the Trolley/Footbridge contrast than the means/end analysis. The humanity formulation, taken as the central moral principle and supplemented where needed, does the most work with the fewest counter-intuitive consequences.

**Usage:** Additive to a Kant essay on the second formulation, the end-in-itself, or how much of moral conduct it captures. Engage Kant's text first; the Trolley/Footbridge contrast and the Doctrine of Double Effect are the modern way of testing how far the formulation reaches. The position credits the formulation with substantial explanatory power while acknowledging that it does not exhaust morality.
