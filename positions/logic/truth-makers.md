# Truth-Makers (Modest Anti-Maximalism)

The truth-maker theorist asks: in virtue of *what entity* in the world is each truth true? Truth-maker maximalism adds that every truth has such an entity, and that the entity's existence necessitates the truth.

I am not a maximalist. The maximalist demand is not forced by the concept of truth, and its standard test cases — negative existentials especially — look like puzzles only once the demand has been accepted. I am not, however, dogmatically anti-truth-maker: in ordinary positive cases, identifying the relevant feature of reality is harmless and often illuminating. The issue is whether the demand for a specific truth-maker is binding on *every* truth.

## What I accept

- Every true claim is true *in virtue of* how the world is. The equivalence schema already captures this: `<p>` is true iff p.
- For ordinary positive claims, locating a "truth-maker" is often good metaphysics, and truth-maker talk can usefully frame realism / anti-realism debates in particular domains.
- Compositional and structural relations between the truth of a whole and the semantic features of its parts may well hold, and can be added as legitimate extensions of the basic theory.

## What I reject

- **Maximalism.** The thesis that every truth must be necessitated by some specific entity treats a metaphysical programme as if it were a constraint on the theory of truth. The schema does not entail it; the correspondence intuition does not either.
- **The "problem" of negative existentials.** "There are no unicorns" is true iff there are no unicorns. That is the whole story the *theory of truth* owes. Whether to posit absences, totality facts, or other exotic entities is internal to a maximalist programme I do not accept; it is not a question forced on us by truth-talk itself.

## Two converging routes

There are two natural ways to reach this anti-maximalist conclusion. They agree on the verdict but rest on different foundations, and it is worth keeping them apart.

**Route 1: semantic complementation.** The meaning of "not" is the complement: *not p* obtains in exactly the cases where *p* fails to obtain. There is nothing further to ask about what makes a negative claim true beyond what makes the corresponding positive claim fail to obtain. The truth of "there are no unicorns" is just the absence of any unicorn-truth-maker; no further entity is needed. As a bonus, the laws of non-contradiction and excluded middle fall out as immediate consequences of what "not" means.

**Route 2: deflationary disquotation.** The theory of truth is exhausted by the equivalence schema. The truth-maker theorist's "in virtue of what entity is ~A true?" is a metaphysical question, not a question for the theory of truth. What makes ~A true is just: ~A. Anything beyond that belongs to a separate ontological theory, not to a theory of truth.

**Where they overlap.** Both routes refuse to translate "in virtue of what is ~A true?" into "what entity grounds ~A?". Both treat the demand for a positive grounder as misplaced. Both deliver an unembarrassed handling of negative existentials.

**Where they differ.** Route 1 rests on a substantive semantic claim about negation — that negation is set-theoretic complement of truth-conditions. Route 2 refuses such a claim: defining "not" via truth and falsity would be circular against the deflationary project, so on Route 2 the meaning of "not" is fixed by its inferential role and acceptance conditions instead. The two foundations are not consistent if pressed: Route 1 takes the semantics of negation as primitive; Route 2 takes its inferential use as primitive and forbids the semantic story.

I do not commit between them. Route 1 gives a more committal semantic picture I can lean on if pressed about *how* negation works. Route 2 gives a cleaner refusal of the maximalist demand, at the cost of taking on the rest of the deflationary programme. Each is independently defensible; either gets us where we want to go.

## Honest objections

1. **"You are dodging the question."** The truth-maker theorist will say I have refused to engage with the demand rather than answered it. Fair — but the demand needs independent motivation that I have not been given. Refusing an unmotivated demand is not the same as failing to meet it.
2. **Harder cases than negative existentials.** Modal truths ("it could have rained"), general truths ("all electrons are negatively charged"), and counterfactuals are less obvious. The semantic-complement story does less work here; the disquotational story shifts the burden to a metaphysics I have not provided. If anything would push me toward a more moderate truth-maker position, it is these cases. I leave this open.
3. **Schema-silence about ontology.** "`<p>` is true iff p" is silent on what makes p the case. A complete metaphysics has to address that. Granted — but the addressing is not the job of the theory of truth. Conflating the two levels is the maximalist's error, not a deficit of the position.

## Considered view

Reject truth-maker maximalism. Accept truth-maker talk where it is useful and not forced. Decline, in advance, to commit between the semantic-complement route and the disquotational route — both reach the same anti-maximalist conclusion, and which is the better fit depends on how the prompt presses.

The slogan: anti-maximalist, not anti-truth-maker. Non-committal between two routes that converge on the same verdict. The harder cases (modal, general, counterfactual truths) are a real soft spot, openly conceded.
