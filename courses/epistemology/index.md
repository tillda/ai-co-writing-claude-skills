# Epistemology (PY1025)

Huemer's *Understanding Knowledge* is the canonical text for this course — its chapter-by-chapter expositions form the backbone of each module's `**Sources:**` list. Longworth's subject guide and the wider literature (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entries, primary papers, monographs) appear under `**Readings:**` as deepening additions, each typically elaborating in detail an argument that Huemer presents in shorter form. The modules below split the content into ten distinct exam topics rather than by Longworth's three big chapters, so a prompt about reliabilism doesn't drag in unrelated discussion of memory, and a prompt about contextualism doesn't drag in foundationalism. Where *Understanding Knowledge* does not cover a module's topic (the value of knowledge; introspection and self-knowledge), `**Sources:**` is left empty and the essay falls back to the canonical analytic position from general knowledge.

**Books usage:**
- *Understanding Knowledge* (Huemer) — canonical for this course; lead with it on every prompt and prefer it over Readings where they overlap.

## 1. The Tripartite Analysis and Gettier Cases

The JTB account of knowledge, Gettier's 1963 counterexamples, and post-Gettier repairs that add or replace the justification condition (causal theories, defeasibility, no-false-lemmas, sensitivity). Use for any prompt asking whether knowledge is justified true belief, how to respond to apparent counterexamples to JTB, or whether the project of analysing knowledge can succeed.

**Sources:**
- Huemer *Understanding Knowledge* ch. 2 §§2.4–2.5 (Gettier's refutation; the post-Gettier repairs: no-false-lemmas, reliabilism, proper function, sensitivity, safety, relevant alternatives, defeasibility)

**Readings:**
- Longworth guide §1.1
- Gettier 'Is Justified True Belief Knowledge?'
- Russell *Problems of Philosophy* ch. 13
- Goldman 'A Causal Theory of Knowing'
- Zagzebski 'The Inescapability of Gettier Problems'
- Steup 'The Analysis of Knowledge' (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy)

## 2. Truth-Tracking, Belief, and Knowledge as Primitive

Nozick's truth-tracking analysis (counterfactual sensitivity), Radford on knowledge without belief (the unconfident examinee), and the Williamson/Craig view that knowledge resists analysis and should be treated as primitive. Use for prompts on Nozick's account, whether knowledge requires belief, whether knowledge is a mental state, or whether the project of conceptual analysis is the wrong tool for epistemology.

**Sources:**
- Huemer *Understanding Knowledge* §2.5.4 (Nozick's truth-tracking / sensitivity analysis)

**Readings:**
- Longworth guide §§1.2.1-1.2.2
- Nozick *Philosophical Explanations* ch. 3
- Radford 'Knowledge—by Examples'
- Williamson 'Is Knowing a State of Mind?'
- Williamson *Knowledge and its Limits* chs. 1-3
- Craig *Knowledge and the State of Nature*

## 3. The Value of Knowledge

Why knowledge matters more than mere true belief — Plato's *Meno* puzzle, the swamping problem, and contemporary virtue-epistemological replies. Use for prompts on why knowledge is more valuable than true belief or on Plato's 'tied down' metaphor.

**Readings:**
- Longworth guide §1.2.3
- Pritchard 'The Value of Knowledge' (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy)
- Sosa *A Virtue Epistemology* chs. 4-5
- Kvanvig *The Value of Knowledge and the Pursuit of Understanding*

## 4. Justification: Internalism vs Externalism

The structure of justification (foundational vs coherentist — covered in its own module) and the nature of justifiers: do subjects have to be aware of what justifies their beliefs? Reliabilism as the central externalist option; access internalism as its counterpart; McDowell/Williamson's hybrid view that combines elements of both. Use for prompts on reliabilism, whether justification requires reflective access, whether reliable mechanisms suffice, and the rationality/reliability distinction.

**Sources:**
- Huemer *Understanding Knowledge* §3.3 (internalism vs externalism) and §2.5.2 (reliabilism)

**Readings:**
- Longworth guide §1.3
- Goldman 'What is Justified Belief'
- Goldman 'Reliabilism' (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy)
- BonJour 'Externalist Theories of Empirical Knowledge'
- Pappas 'Internalist vs Externalist Theories' (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy)
- McDowell 'Knowledge and the Internal'
- Bonjour & Sosa *Epistemic Justification*

## 5. Perception

The nature of perceptual experience (naïve realism, sense-datum theory, intentional/representational theory, disjunctivism) and how perception can yield knowledge. The argument from illusion, the common-element thesis, hallucination as a challenge to direct realism. Use for prompts on whether veridical perception and hallucination share an experiential element, whether the argument from illusion succeeds, or whether disjunctivism preserves naïve realism.

**Sources:**
- Huemer *Understanding Knowledge* ch. 9 §§9.2–9.4 (direct realism, disjunctivism; argument from illusion, hallucination, time-gap, objections to indirect realism)

**Readings:**
- Longworth guide §§2.1.1-2.1.2
- Crane 'The Problem of Perception' (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy)
- BonJour 'Epistemological Problems of Perception' (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy)
- Soteriou 'The Disjunctive Theory of Perception' (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy)
- Martin 'The Limits of Self-Awareness'
- McDowell 'Criteria, Defeasibility and Knowledge'
- Snowdon 'Perception, Vision and Causation'
- Austin *Sense and Sensibilia*

## 6. Testimony and Memory

How knowledge is transmitted and retained. The reductionist/anti-reductionist debate over testimony (do we need positive reasons to trust testifiers, or are we entitled by default?); the analogous question for memory; the claim that memory and testimony are merely preservative vs. potentially generative. Use for prompts on whether testimony or memory can yield justified belief without positive reasons, on the gullibility worry, and on the epistemic status of remembered beliefs.

**Sources:**
- Huemer *Understanding Knowledge* ch. 11 §§11.1–11.2 (memory: forgotten evidence; inferential, foundational, preservation, dualistic theories)
- Huemer *Understanding Knowledge* ch. 13 §§13.2–13.4 (testimony: inductive account, default of credulity, coherence)

**Readings:**
- Longworth guide §2.2
- Coady 'Testimony and Observation'
- Coady *Testimony: A Philosophical Study*
- Fricker 'Against Gullibility'
- Adler 'Epistemological Problems of Testimony' (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy)
- Lackey *Learning from Words*
- Burge 'Content Preservation'
- Huemer 'The Problem of Memory Knowledge'
- Senor 'Epistemological Problems of Memory' (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy)

## 7. Introspection and Self-Knowledge

Privileged access, first-person authority, transparency (infallibility and self-intimation), and the inner-perception model. Shoemaker's self-blindness argument against inner perception. Use for prompts on how we know our own minds, whether self-knowledge is based on inner perception, and the relation between introspection and ordinary perception.

**Readings:**
- Longworth guide §2.3
- Gertler 'Self-Knowledge' (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy)
- Shoemaker 'Self-Knowledge and "Inner Sense"'
- Shoemaker *The First-Person Perspective and Other Essays* (esp. essays 10-12)
- Armstrong *A Materialist Theory of the Mind* ch. 15
- Burge 'Individualism and Self-Knowledge'
- Moran *Authority and Estrangement*

## 8. Sceptical Hypothesis and the Closure Principle

The brain-in-a-vat / dreaming argument (premises (i)-(iii) and modus tollens to scepticism), the closure principle for known entailment, and Nozick/Dretske's denial of closure as a way to keep ordinary knowledge while admitting we don't know we're not BIVs. The zebra/mule case and other purported counterexamples to closure. Use for prompts on whether you can know you're not a brain in a vat or dreaming, whether knowledge is closed under known entailment, and whether truth-tracking refutes the sceptic.

**Sources:**
- Huemer *Understanding Knowledge* §§8.2.1–8.2.3 (dream argument, brain-in-a-vat, deceiving god)
- Huemer *Understanding Knowledge* §3.2 (closure principle), §3.2.4 (zebra and mule counterexample), §8.3.1 (relevant alternatives response)

**Readings:**
- Longworth guide §§3.1-3.2
- Stroud *The Significance of Philosophical Scepticism* ch. 1
- Putnam *Reason, Truth, and History* ch. 1
- Nozick *Philosophical Explanations* ch. 3 §2
- Dretske 'Epistemic Operators'
- Vogel 'Are there counter-examples to the closure principle?'
- Luper 'The Epistemic Closure Principle' (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy)

## 9. Other Replies to Scepticism: Moore, Putnam, Contextualism

Moore's 'here is a hand' refutation and the question-begging worry; Putnam's semantic externalist argument that we can't be radically deceived; DeRose/Lewis contextualism (knowledge ascriptions vary with the conversational standard). Use for prompts on whether Moore begs the question, whether semantic externalism dissolves scepticism, and whether contextualism gives a satisfying response. The 'KK' question and iterative knowledge also fit here.

**Sources:**
- Huemer *Understanding Knowledge* §4.4.3 (Moorean response to skepticism)
- Huemer *Understanding Knowledge* §§8.3.2–8.3.4 (contextualist response, semantic externalism, case against the BIV hypothesis)
- Huemer *Understanding Knowledge* §3.1 (contextualism) and §6.1 (KK thesis)

**Readings:**
- Longworth guide §3.3
- Moore 'Proof of an External World'
- Putnam *Reason, Truth and History* ch. 1
- Brueckner 'Semantic Answers to Skepticism'
- DeRose 'Solving the Skeptical Problem'
- Lewis 'Elusive Knowledge'
- Cohen 'Contextualist Solutions to Epistemological Problems'

## 10. Foundationalism, Coherentism, Infinitism, Agrippa's Trilemma

The structure of justification: are our beliefs justified by basic foundational beliefs, by holistic coherence, by infinite regress, or by none of the above? The fairy-tale objection to coherentism; the regress problem; Agrippa's trilemma as a sceptical engine. Use for prompts on whether justification must terminate in basic beliefs, whether coherence suffices, the circularity worry against coherentism, and the regress of reasons.

**Sources:**
- Huemer *Understanding Knowledge* ch. 4 §§4.2–4.5 (infinitism, coherentism, scepticism about justification, foundationalism)
- Huemer *Understanding Knowledge* ch. 5 (acquaintance theory, Sellarsian dilemma, phenomenal conservatism, foundherentism)

**Readings:**
- Longworth guide §3.4
- Dancy *An Introduction to Contemporary Epistemology* chs. 4-9
- Fumerton 'Foundationalist Theories of Epistemic Justification' (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy)
- Kvanvig 'Coherentist Theories of Epistemic Justification' (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy)
- BonJour 'Can Empirical Knowledge have a Foundation?'
- BonJour *The Structure of Empirical Knowledge*
- Klein 'Human Knowledge and the Infinite Regress of Reasons'
