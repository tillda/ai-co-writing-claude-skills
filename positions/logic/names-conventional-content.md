# Names and Conventional Content

Language is a system of conventions — instructions we learn through use — that tell us which proposition a given sentence is supposed to express. Meaning does not live at the level of the bare references of the parts. A sentence has a determinate meaning when the conventions, together with speakers' intentions, express a proposition; it is meaning-deficient when they over-constrain or under-constrain (this does not mean that the sentence is *completely* without meaning, just that the meaning is deficient).

Applied to proper names: a name contributes its conventional role, not merely its bearer. Two co-referential names can play different roles in the conventions — having been introduced and sustained in use through different practices — so the propositions assigned to sentences containing them can come apart even when reference matches.

This is a thesis about **conventional content**, not about the reference relation. It is compatible with rigid designation at the reference layer: a proper name with a successfully baptised bearer can rigidly track that bearer across counterfactual circumstances, while the content the speaker grasps remains the role. Frege's puzzle is then resolved at the content layer — different roles, different propositions, informative identity — without denying that "Hesperus" and "Phosphorus" co-refer rigidly to Venus. (See `conventional-reference.md` for the broader two-tier picture and the limits of Kripkean necessity beyond proper-name identity.)

This is not descriptivism: the conventional role is not a description the speaker has in mind, but a public convention attaching the name to its bearer. It is not pure Millianism either: bare reference does not fix what proposition a sentence expresses.

The same framework handles non-referring names and non-denoting definite descriptions. "Vulcan is closer to the Sun than Mercury" and "the present king of France is bald" are meaning-deficient: the conventions try to single out a proposition about a particular individual, but no such individual exists for them to lock onto. The sentences are not meaningless — we understand the words and the structure — but no determinate proposition is expressed, so they are neither true nor false. (Fictional names like "Pegasus" or "Sherlock Holmes" may be a different case: the convention can attach to a determinate entity in legend or fiction, so a proposition does get expressed.)

**Applicable to:** Frege's puzzle, sense and reference, Millian vs. descriptive theories of proper names, names and meaning, substitutivity failure, propositional attitude reports, *logic / 5. Reference – Names and Descriptions*, *logic / 6. Non-extensional Language*

**Usage:** This topic almost always requires covering the canonical analytic answers (Mill, Frege, descriptivism, direct reference). Present the conventions/instruction theory alongside them in the closing section as one candidate solution. Promote it to the essay's conclusion only if the canonical theories fail to give a clear positive answer; otherwise leave it as a peer alternative.
