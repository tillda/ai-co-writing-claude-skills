# 'The doubt based on dreaming calls as much into question as does the doubt based on a deceiving God. So Descartes has no need to include both in the First Meditation.' Discuss.

The claim is that dreaming and the deceiving God do the same sceptical work, and so the second is redundant. In my view this is false. The dream argument and the deceiving-God argument target different classes of belief. The dream undermines beliefs about the external world but leaves the truths of arithmetic and geometry untouched. The deceiving God reaches those very truths by corrupting the faculties of reasoning themselves. Without the second argument the doubt of the First Meditation would not be radical. Both are needed. Descartes had reason to include both.

To see the difference, we should be precise about what the dream argument actually does. It runs roughly as follows. (1) For any present perceptual experience, I cannot rule out that I am dreaming it. (2) Dreams are not a reliable guide to the external world. (3) So for any belief grounded in present perception — that I have hands, that I am here by the fire, that the paper is in front of me — I cannot confidently assert it. The argument does not say that perception is in principle unreliable. It says that, given the indistinguishability of waking from dreaming on the inside, I have no test by which I can confirm any particular sensory belief.

But notice what this argument leaves alone. Descartes himself notices it. Dreams, he thinks, are like paintings. Even the most fanciful painting combines real colours and shapes. So the very general categories — extension, number, shape — must apply even within the dream. From this he concludes that the formal disciplines, arithmetic and geometry, are not undermined by the dream argument. Two and three make five whether I am awake or asleep. A square has four sides in any world. These transparent truths survive.

This is the sense in which the dream argument is bounded. It is an argument against beliefs about the external world, not against the operation of the rational faculties themselves. In a dream my reasoning is intact, even if the contents I reason about are illusions. I might dream that I am sitting at a desk, but if in the dream I add two and three, I get five.

The deceiving-God argument is designed precisely to attack what the dream leaves alone. It supposes a being of unlimited power who could have made me such that I am deceived even when I count the sides of a square. The point is not that the proposition might be false — that is a metaphysical claim Descartes does not need. The point is epistemic: my judgement that the proposition is true could be the product of a corrupted faculty. The deceiver does not falsify arithmetic; he falsifies my access to arithmetic. He undermines my capacity to know transparent truths.

This is the work the dream argument cannot do. Within a dream, my faculties are not corrupted. The dream-sufferer reasons normally about a fictitious world. The victim of the deceiver, by contrast, reasons abnormally about anything at all — including the formal disciplines that the dream cannot touch. So the two arguments target different things. The dream argument cuts off beliefs about hands and fires. The deceiver cuts off beliefs about arithmetic, geometry, and any other domain where the rational faculties are essential to knowledge.

We can put the difference in another way. In the dream argument, what is suspended is the relation between my experience and the world: I cannot trust that the experience represents anything real. In the deceiver argument, what is suspended is the relation between my judgement and the truth: I cannot trust that the way things seem to me, even on reflection, lines up with how they actually are. The first is a doubt about input. The second is a doubt about processing.

We might be tempted to think the dream argument can be pushed harder. Why not say that I might be dreaming that I am adding two and three? In a confused dream I might feel certain of arithmetic propositions that are false. Some commentators argue that the dream argument, properly pressed, already reaches the formal disciplines, making the deceiver redundant. But I think this stretches the dream argument beyond its natural shape. A dream is, on the ordinary understanding, a misrepresentation of a world that the dreamer takes to be real. It is not, ordinarily, a corruption of the dreamer's reasoning. We can imagine a dream where I dream that I get a sum wrong, but in that case my dreamed self has made an error my waking self could correct. The dream does not generally undermine the operation of the faculty itself. To get to that, we need a stronger scenario — and that stronger scenario is the deceiver.

There is a further consideration. Descartes is not just arranging a logical hierarchy of doubts. He is also writing a meditation, a piece of intellectual discipline. Even if the dream argument could in principle be stretched to reach arithmetic, it does not do so vividly. The deceiver scenario gives the meditator a concrete picture of what radical doubt would feel like. A demon employing all his energies to mislead me makes the doubt about arithmetic graspable. The dream alone, even if logically capable of doing the work, does not focus the imagination in the same way. So even on the most charitable reading of the redundancy claim, the deceiver still earns its place in the meditation.

What about the converse direction? Could the deceiver replace the dream? Logically yes — a deceiver of unlimited power could produce all the effects the dream argument exploits, plus more. But there is a reason to keep the dream argument. It is a familiar, ordinary phenomenon. Nearly everyone has had a dream that felt indistinguishable from waking life. So the dream argument is the bridge. It uses an experience the reader already trusts to introduce the rationality of doubt. The deceiver, by contrast, is a piece of philosophical machinery. Without the dream as a stepping stone, the deceiver would seem too contrived to take seriously. Descartes uses the dream to soften the reader up for the more radical move.

So the claim that the two arguments do the same work is false in both directions. The dream argument cannot reach arithmetic and geometry without being stretched in ways that take it beyond its natural shape. The deceiver argument can reach those targets but, taken alone, would lack the ordinary phenomenon that makes the doubt accessible. Each does work the other cannot do. Descartes had reason to include both, and the structure of the First Meditation depends on having both available — the dream as a familiar door into doubt, the deceiver as the engine that makes the doubt radical.
