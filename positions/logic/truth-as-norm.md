# Truth as the Aim of Assertion and Belief (Deflationary Recovery)

The thought is familiar: to assert is to *present as true*; to believe is to *take as true*; truth is therefore the constitutive norm of assertion and the constitutive aim of belief. Williamson, Boghossian, and Wright, among others, build serious philosophical pictures on this thought, and at least one of them — Wright — argues that deflationism cannot do justice to it.

I am a deflationist (see *Truth Minimalism*). So the live question for me is whether the normative role truth plays in assertion and belief is recoverable from the equivalence schema plus independent theories of belief and assertion, or whether it forces a more substantial conception of truth. My view: it is recoverable. The norm is real but is not a primitive feature of truth. It flows from what assertion and belief *are*, with the schema doing the connecting work.

## What I accept

- Assertion is constitutively a presentation of how things are. To assert that p is to put p forward as the case.
- Belief is constitutively an aiming-at-the-world. To believe that p is to take p to be how things are.
- Truth is implicated in both: "presenting as true" and "taking as true" are not idle synonyms but accurate descriptions of the speech act and the mental state.
- There is a real normative pull: it is *bad* to assert what isn't true; it is *defective* to believe what isn't true. Calling these defects is not just a matter of taste.

These concessions matter — denying them would be a non-starter — and they are compatible with deflationism. The disagreement is not about whether truth has a normative role, but about whether the normative role is built into the *theory of truth* or contributed by the theory of belief and assertion.

## What I reject

- That truth's normative role is *primitive*, in a way that requires loading it into the meaning of "true".
- That deflationism cannot recover the role.
- That the "norm of belief" or "norm of assertion" is a substantive *additional* fact about truth, beyond the schema and the constitutive features of belief and assertion themselves.

## How the recovery works

The argument has three moves.

1. **The schema secures the equivalence.** If asserting p is "presenting p as true", then asserting p is committing to p's truth — and by the schema, committing to p's truth is just committing to p. "Presenting as true" reduces to "presenting as the case". No mystery about why this is the right description: the schema makes "p" and "<p> is true" interchangeable in the relevant respects.

2. **The norm flows from the act, not from the property.** What makes it bad to assert a falsehood? Not some primitive normative force in the truth predicate. The badness is constitutive of assertion: an assertion is *defective* if its content isn't the case, in the same way a promise is defective if not kept and a question defective if it presupposes falsehood. The norm belongs to the speech act. Truth enters because the schema lets us state the norm using "true" — "assert only what is true" — but it would equally be expressed as "assert only p when p" without semantic loss.

3. **Same story for belief.** Believing aims at how things are; "the norm of belief is truth" reduces, on the schema, to "believe p only if p". This is hardly a substantive thesis — it's just the constitutive aim of belief stated using the truth predicate. The deflationist does not have to add anything to truth to get this; it falls out.

So the apparent normativity attached to truth is a shadow cast by the constitutive aims of belief and assertion, plus the schema's licence to translate "<p>" and "p" into one another. No primitive normative tie is needed.

## What "to assert is to present as true" tells us about truth

Strictly: very little. It tells us a great deal about *assertion* — that assertion is a truth-tied speech act, distinct from supposition, conjecture, or fiction-making. But the part of the slogan that mentions truth is doing minimal work; it could equally be "to assert is to present as the case". The slogan is informative because of what it says about presentation, not because of what it says about truth.

This is the deflationary diagnosis: claims that look like they extract substantial truth-theoretic content from assertion-theory or belief-theory typically just exploit the schema to reformulate, without adding anything.

## Honest objections

1. **Wright's worry: deflationism can't capture *primitive* normativity.** Wright argues that truth has an irreducible normative pull — we are *correct* in asserting what is true — that the deflationist has to merely note rather than explain. Reply: the correctness condition isn't primitive; it is the constitutive correctness condition of assertion as a type of speech act. A deflationist who has any theory of speech acts at all has the resources to explain correctness without inflating truth. The challenge is real only if you assume truth must be the *source* of correctness; the deflationist denies that assumption.

2. **The "engagement" worry.** Critics say the deflationist account makes truth look optional or merely linguistic, undermining the seriousness with which inquirers pursue truth. Reply: inquirers pursue truth seriously because they want to *know how things are*. The seriousness is grounded in the value of understanding the world; the truth predicate is the indispensable *device* for stating the goal generally, not the source of its seriousness. We pursue truth because we want to know — not because "true" carries weight independent of "knowing how things are".

3. **The norm-of-belief argument from circularity.** Some argue that any reductive analysis of belief in terms of "aiming at truth" already presupposes a substantial conception of truth, so deflationism cannot give an account of belief without smuggling in non-deflationary truth. Reply: the deflationist needn't analyse belief in terms of truth at all. They can give a story about belief in terms of representation, behavioural disposition, or rational role, and use the schema to *describe* this story using truth-talk afterwards. The "aim at truth" formulation is convenient summary, not a definition.

4. **The difficulty of distinguishing constitutive from regulative norms.** The deflationary recovery treats assertion's truth-norm as constitutive (you cannot count as asserting if you aren't presenting as the case). But some norms might be regulative (rules of good practice rather than identity-conditions of the act). If the norm of assertion is regulative, the deflationist's recovery has to be more careful — the constitutive story doesn't generalise easily. I leave this as an open complication rather than a refutation.

## Considered view

Truth is genuinely the aim of assertion and belief — no deflationist worth taking seriously denies this. What deflationism denies is that truth's role here requires inflating the *theory of truth*. The schema, plus independent theories of what assertion and belief are, suffices.

The slogan "to assert is to present as true" tells us almost everything about assertion and almost nothing about truth: the truth predicate appears in the slogan as a reusable connective, not as the bearer of normative content. Truth does its work through the schema; assertion and belief do theirs through their own constitutive aims.

The right line is: yes, truth norms assertion and belief. No, this doesn't require a substantive theory of truth. Deflationism handles the role — at the cost, as ever, of relying on independent theories of assertion and belief that the deflationist treats as separate matters. That cost is honest and unavoidable.
