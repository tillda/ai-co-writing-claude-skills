# Philosophical Positions — Epistemology (PY1025)

These are my stated leanings on questions in this course. They follow Michael Huemer's *Understanding Knowledge* (UK) chapter by chapter. When the prompt is silent on direction, default to these positions as the **conclusion** of the essay. Use the theory-rejection structure honestly: present rival theories in their strongest form, raise the genuine objections to them, then arrive at the position below as the considered view. Do not strawman the rejected positions — the examiner can tell.

When the prompt directs a specific position, follow the prompt; these defaults apply only when the prompt is silent.

## Knowledge Resists Analysis (UK ch. 2)

JTB is refuted by Gettier; every patch (no-false-lemmas, reliabilism, proper function, sensitivity, safety, relevant alternatives, defeasibility) fails to a counterexample. No analysis works because "knowledge" is a Wittgensteinian / family-resemblance concept, not a Lockean one with necessary-and-sufficient conditions.

**Applicable to:** justified true belief, JTB, Gettier, Gettier cases, knowledge as JTB, sufficiency of the tripartite analysis, no-false-lemmas, defeasibility, conceptual analysis of knowledge, *epistemology / 1. The Tripartite Analysis and Gettier Cases*

## Against Knowledge as Primitive

The right diagnosis is Wittgensteinian, not Williamsonian. Knowledge resists analysis because of how concepts work in general (family resemblance), not because knowledge is a sui generis mental state with no decomposition. Reject Williamson's knowledge-first programme.

**Applicable to:** knowledge first, primitivism about knowledge, Williamson, knowledge as a mental state, Craig, *epistemology / 2. Truth-Tracking, Belief, and Knowledge as Primitive*

## Truth-Tracking Fails

Nozick's sensitivity condition is one more failed analysis. It violates closure (you'd know you have hands but not that you're not a BIV) and faces standard counterexamples. Reject sensitivity/tracking analyses.

**Applicable to:** Nozick, truth-tracking, counterfactual sensitivity, sensitivity condition, Dretske conclusive reasons, *epistemology / 2. Truth-Tracking, Belief, and Knowledge as Primitive*

## Knowledge Requires Belief

Radford's unconfident-examinee case does not refute the belief condition; the examinee does believe (perhaps non-confidently). Knowledge entails belief.

**Applicable to:** Radford, unconfident examinee, knowledge without belief, belief condition on knowledge, *epistemology / 2. Truth-Tracking, Belief, and Knowledge as Primitive*

## Closure Holds (UK ch. 3)

The closure principle (knowledge is closed under known entailment) is correct. Apparent counterexamples (Dretske's zebra/mule, Nozick) trade on ambiguity or are bullets we should refuse to bite. Denying closure is a worse cost than accepting that we know we are not BIVs.

**Applicable to:** closure, closure principle, knowledge closed under known entailment, Nozick on closure, Dretske on closure, zebra and mule, *epistemology / 8. Sceptical Hypothesis and the Closure Principle*

## Reject Contextualism

DeRose/Lewis contextualism is implausible: "know" does not behave like "here" or "tall". The motivating cases are better explained by shifts in psychological confidence and conversational salience, not in semantic content.

**Applicable to:** contextualism, knowledge ascriptions, conversational standards, DeRose, Lewis Elusive Knowledge, shifting standards, *epistemology / 9. Other Replies to Scepticism: Moore, Putnam, Contextualism*

## Internalism vs Externalism: Both, in Different Senses

Both contain something right. Distinguish *animal* knowledge (broadly externalist — reliable processes suffice) from *reflective* knowledge (internalist — requires reflectively accessible justifiers). Pure reliabilism fails the new evil demon and generality problems; pure access internalism is too demanding for ordinary perceptual knowledge.

**Applicable to:** internalism, externalism, access internalism, mentalism, reliabilism, reliable process, Goldman, new evil demon, generality problem, justifiers, awareness of justifiers, *epistemology / 4. Justification: Internalism vs Externalism*

## Foundationalism (UK ch. 4)

Foundationalism is correct. Coherentism fails the alternate-coherent-systems / fairy-tale objection and ultimately needs foundations anyway. Infinitism fails the finite-mind objection. Global scepticism is self-defeating. Some beliefs must be non-inferentially justified.

**Applicable to:** foundationalism, coherentism, infinitism, basic beliefs, structure of justification, regress of reasons, fairy-tale objection, Agrippa, Agrippa's trilemma, Pyrrhonian scepticism, regress argument for scepticism, *epistemology / 10. Foundationalism, Coherentism, Infinitism, Agrippa's Trilemma*

## Phenomenal Conservatism (UK ch. 5)

The ground of foundational justification is appearance: if it seems to S that p (and S has no defeater), then S has prima facie justification to believe p. PC handles perception, memory, intuition, and testimony uniformly. The acquaintance theory founders on the Sellarsian dilemma; qualified-appearance theories add nothing. PC is not self-defeating.

**Applicable to:** phenomenal conservatism, seemings, appearances, foundational justification, prima facie justification, acquaintance theory, Sellarsian dilemma, BonJour, Fumerton, Pryor dogmatism, easy knowledge, self-defeat objection to PC

## Direct Realism about Perception (UK chs. 8–9)

Direct (intentional / awareness-based) realism: in ordinary perception we are directly aware of mind-independent objects; the experience is the means of awareness, not an inner object of awareness. Reject sense-datum / indirect realism (it reifies mental intermediaries and creates the knowledge problem). Reject disjunctivism (it overreacts to the argument from hallucination and denies a real common phenomenal element).

**Applicable to:** naïve realism, direct realism, indirect realism, sense-data, sense-datum theory, intentional theory of perception, representationalism, disjunctivism, theory of perception, *epistemology / 5. Perception*

## Argument from Illusion / Hallucination Fails

Illusion shows only that things sometimes appear other than they are, not that we perceive mental intermediaries. Hallucination shows there is a common phenomenal element across veridical and non-veridical experience (against disjunctivism), but the common element is the *experience*, not a sense-datum we are aware of.

**Applicable to:** argument from illusion, argument from hallucination, common-element thesis, indistinguishability, perceptual experience and hallucination, *epistemology / 5. Perception*

## External-World Scepticism: Direct Realism + Bad-Theory Reply (UK ch. 8)

The BIV / dream / deceiver hypotheses are bad theories: ad hoc, unfalsifiable, and parasitic on the ordinary world for their content. Once direct realism is accepted, the sceptic's premises lose force. Moore is roughly right (we are more sure of "here is a hand" than of any sceptical premise); Putnam's semantic-externalist reply is suggestive but limited; contextualism is the wrong diagnosis. The right reply is anti-sceptical realism, not concession.

**Applicable to:** brain in a vat, BIV, dreaming argument, sceptical hypothesis, can I know I have hands, can I know I am not dreaming, response to scepticism, Moore proof of an external world, Putnam semantic externalism reply, *epistemology / 8. Sceptical Hypothesis and the Closure Principle*, *epistemology / 9. Other Replies to Scepticism: Moore, Putnam, Contextualism*

## Metaknowledge: No KK, Benign Circularity OK (UK ch. 6)

Reject the KK thesis (knowing does not require knowing that you know — the regress is vicious). Some epistemic circularity is benign: a foundational belief-forming method can vindicate itself without that being a defect. Track-record arguments, however, are bad circularity.

**Applicable to:** KK thesis, KK principle, iterative knowledge, epistemic circularity, track record arguments, meta-justification, justified theories of justification

## Memory: Dualistic (UK ch. 11)

A remembered belief is justified iff it was rationally acquired *and* is being rationally retained. Reject the pure preservation theory (it implausibly justifies beliefs after a Five-Minute-Hypothesis-style reset). Reject the pure foundational theory (it makes memory a generative source). Reject the pure inferential theory (it overintellectualises ordinary remembering).

**Applicable to:** memory, preservative memory, generative memory, Lackey memory generative, memory knowledge, do we need positive reasons to trust memory, forgotten evidence, *epistemology / 6. Testimony and Memory*

## Testimony: Default Credulity + Coherence (UK ch. 13)

Anti-reductionism is right: testimony gives prima facie justification by default (a special case of PC applied to assertion-as-true). Coady is broadly right against Hume's inductive account. Coherence with the testimonial network and background beliefs upgrades the default. Fricker-style reductionism is too demanding and empirically unrealistic.

**Applicable to:** testimony, reductionism about testimony, anti-reductionism, default trust, gullibility, Coady, Fricker, Burge content preservation, principle of charity, *epistemology / 6. Testimony and Memory*

## Rationalism about the A Priori (UK ch. 10)

Synthetic a priori knowledge exists (mathematics, logic, ethics, basic metaphysics). Empiricism is self-defeating (its own central claim is synthetic and not empirical). Logical positivism's verification criterion is incoherent. Quine's holism does not eliminate the analytic/synthetic distinction in any useful sense. Kant's transcendental idealism is implausible. The faculty is rational insight into universals and their relations.

**Applicable to:** a priori, synthetic a priori, rationalism, empiricism, logical positivism, verificationism, Quine two dogmas, analytic/synthetic distinction, Kant, transcendental idealism

## Induction: Multiply-Grounded, Not Sceptical (UK ch. 12)

Hume's scepticism is wrong. Induction is vindicated by a convergence of routes: the proportional syllogism plus the law of large numbers, objective Bayesianism (with a defensible Principle of Indifference), and inference to the best explanation. These are compatible and mutually supporting. Grue is solved: "grue" is genuinely less natural / less projectible than "green".

**Applicable to:** problem of induction, Hume on induction, proportional syllogism, objective Bayesianism, principle of indifference, inference to the best explanation, IBE, grue, Goodman new riddle of induction

## Scientific Realism (UK ch. 14)

Scientific realism is correct. Underdetermination does not refute it. Falsifiability is a genuine epistemic virtue (vindicated by Bayesian likelihoods). Simplicity / Occam's razor is a genuine epistemic virtue (best defended by the likelihood account, not by mere pragmatism). The pessimistic meta-induction overstates its case.

**Applicable to:** scientific realism, anti-realism, underdetermination, falsifiability, Popper, simplicity, Occam's razor, Bayesian confirmation, ravens paradox, pessimistic induction

## Ethical Intuitionism (UK ch. 15)

Moral statements express genuine propositions; reject expressivism and nihilism. Ethical intuitions are intellectual seemings about moral matters; under PC they confer prima facie justification. Evolutionary debunking arguments fail (we routinely correct evolved biases via reflection). Emotions can bias moral judgement but are not its source.

**Applicable to:** moral knowledge, ethical intuitionism, intuition, expressivism, error theory, moral nihilism, is/ought gap, open question argument, evolutionary debunking, moral realism

## Religious Knowledge: Evidentialism (UK ch. 16)

Evidentialism is correct: religious belief should be proportioned to evidence. Reject fideism. Religious testimony is weak (traditions conflict, no independently verifiable distinctive feats). Religious experience gives PC-style prima facie justification but is heavily defeated by cultural variability and conflict. Philosophical arguments (fine-tuning vs. problem of evil) are where the real action is.

**Applicable to:** religious knowledge, evidentialism, fideism, faith vs evidence, religious experience, sensus divinitatis, Plantinga reformed epistemology, fine-tuning, problem of evil

## Rational Irrationality (UK ch. 17)

Most persistent disagreement on politics, religion, and contested philosophy is not driven by difficulty or ignorance but by *non-epistemic belief preferences* — people find some beliefs more pleasant or socially useful and accept them at low individual epistemic cost. Becoming rational is a moral project, not just a cognitive one.

**Applicable to:** rational irrationality, motivated reasoning, doxastic preferences, automatic bias, becoming rational

## Expert Trust: Default Withhold on Disputed Questions (UK ch. 18)

Defer to experts on settled, narrow, well-replicated, politically inert findings. On controversial, recent, complex, politically aligned, or unreplicated claims, the right epistemic stance is suspension of judgement, not deference. The replication crisis is real and discounts confidence in much published science.

**Applicable to:** trust in experts, deference, scientific method, replication crisis, scepticism about experts

## Peer Disagreement: Total Evidence View (UK ch. 19)

Reject pure equal-weight (it self-defeats and ignores first-order evidence) and pure steadfastness (it licenses dogmatism). On the Total Evidence View, first-order evidence can downgrade an apparent peer's reliability — if your interlocutor asserts something prima facie absurd, that is itself evidence they are not in fact your peer on this question. Most philosophical disagreement reflects gestalt-perception differences rather than crisp factual disputes.

**Applicable to:** peer disagreement, equal weight view, steadfast view, right reasons view, total evidence view, conciliationism, gestalt perception in philosophy
