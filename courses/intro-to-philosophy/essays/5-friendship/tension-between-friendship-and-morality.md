Friendship asks us to do more for some people than for others. We pick up our friends from the airport, defend them when others gossip, lend them money we would not lend to strangers. Most influential moral theories, by contrast, demand impartiality: each person counts the same, and the right action is the one that an objective view of everyone's interests would recommend. So there is, at first sight, a tension. In my view the tension is real — the reconciliationist moves do not succeed — and the right response is to take friendship as a constraint on acceptable moral theory rather than to give up friendship in the name of impartiality.

Let me set out the conflict more carefully. Sarah Stroud puts the point clearly in her essay on partiality in friendship: friendship constitutively involves caring more about what happens to friends than about what happens to strangers, and being more motivated to advance their interests. We even seem to have special responsibilities to friends — duties of loyalty, of help, of trust — that we do not have to people in general. Impartialist theories, the most prominent of which is utilitarianism, hold that the right act maximises total welfare regardless of whose welfare it is. So if we can produce more good by spending an afternoon volunteering for a charity than by helping a friend move house, the impartialist verdict is that we ought to volunteer. But that verdict seems false. A friend who reasoned that way would be a defective friend.

There are three ways to react to this clash. The first reconciles partiality under impartiality. The second concedes the conflict and gives friendship priority. The third treats the conflict as tragic and incommensurable. I take the first to fail and the second to be roughly right.

The first response — call it the indirect impartialist strategy — argues that being partial to friends is itself recommended by impartial morality, because a world in which people have close friendships goes better than a world in which everyone is uniformly benevolent. We can imagine a sophisticated utilitarian who tells us: cultivate friendship, treat your friends specially, do not always run the calculation, because agents who internalise these dispositions produce more total good than agents who do not.

The strategy is clever, but it does not really solve the problem. The reason is that it gets the explanation of friendship's demands backwards. When I help my friend, I do it because she is my friend, not because the act maximises total welfare. If, in some particular case, the calculation said that I should let her down — perhaps because the resources spent on her could marginally help several strangers — the impartialist would have to say I should let her down. A good friend does not let her friend down on those grounds. So the indirect impartialist either gives the wrong verdict in cases or, to avoid those cases, relies on a rule of partiality that the impartialist machinery does not actually justify. Either way, friendship is not really being honoured; it is being instrumentalised.

The second response accepts the clash and says: so much the worse for impartiality. Partiality to friends is constitutive of friendship, friendship is constitutive of a good human life, and therefore any moral theory that condemns the partiality of friends is a theory we have decisive reason to reject. This view traces back at least to Aristotle, who treated philia as central to the good life and as a virtue, not as a deviation from impartial concern. For Aristotle the friend is loved for her own sake — not because doing so happens to maximise some impersonal value — and a life without such friendship is not a fully flourishing one. If that is right, then a moral theory that forbids loving anyone for their own sake is not a moral theory we should accept. It is the theory that has to give.

The third response is the tragic one: the demands of friendship and the demands of impartial morality are simply incommensurable, and there is no fact of the matter about what one ought to do overall. This view has the appeal of honesty, but it concedes too much. We do not, in ordinary practice, treat helping a friend at the cost of failing to maximise impersonal welfare as an unresolved tragic dilemma. We treat it as obviously the right thing to do. If our theory tells us otherwise, the trouble is with the theory.

One might object that the second response — friendship trumps impartiality — is just self-serving favouritism dressed up in philosophical language. After all, every cruel nepotist could say that he is just being a good friend. But this misses the point. The argument is not that any partiality whatsoever is justified by the value of friendship. It is that the kinds of partiality constitutive of friendship — caring more about a friend's wellbeing, being more motivated to help her, spending more time on her concerns — are not moral failings. Stealing a job for a friend, or harming a stranger to benefit a friend, are not merely partial acts; they are unjust acts. The objector conflates partiality with injustice, and these are different things. Aristotle himself emphasised that friendship and justice are connected: good friends do not cheat, do not harm third parties, and hold each other to ethical standards.

So here is the verdict. The tension between friendship's partiality and morality's impartiality is real. The reconciliationist tries to dissolve it but ends up either misdescribing what friends do or recommending behaviour that no good friend would accept. The tragic view concedes too much. The honest answer is that friendship is a fixed point — too central to a good life to be optional — and any moral theory must be tested against it. A theory that condemns ordinary friendship is, for that reason, suspect. We should keep our friends and revise the theory.
