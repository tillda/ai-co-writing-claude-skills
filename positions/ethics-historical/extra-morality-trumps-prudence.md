# Hume Cannot Explain Morality's Authority over Prudence and Desire

Common moral practice ranks morality above prudence above desire: when an appetite conflicts with prudence, prudence ought to win; when prudence conflicts with morality, morality ought to win. Hume's sentimentalism cannot recover this ranking. Sympathy, the supposed source of morality, is one emotion among many, and on Humean terms it has no claim to override hate or self-interest.

- **The structural account the rationalist can give.** Prudential judgment is not a desire competing with appetites; it is an evaluation that has already taken the appetites into account. Weighing appetite against prudence double-counts the appetite. The same relation holds between prudence and morality: a moral judgment that one ought to sacrifice some self-interest already incorporates the self-interest in its formation, so weighing self-interest against the moral verdict double-counts. The ordering is built into what kind of judgment each is.
- **Why Hume cannot give it.** On the Humean view, moral motivation is a particular subset of desires (those traceable to sympathy). The Humean has no principled reason to privilege sympathy-desires over hate-desires; second-order desires don't help, since someone who hates and desires to keep hating does not thereby acquire a moral motive. Morality on Humean terms is an arbitrarily privileged emotional set.
- **The bite for the Hume module.** This is what makes the sensible knave look more devastating than Hume admits. The knave's challenge "why be moral when I can profit by injustice?" gets no structural reply on Hume's grounds; Hume can only hope that the knave's sympathies happen to run the right way. The rationalist can say the knave's question is itself confused — moral verdicts already weigh the self-interest he is asking us to weigh again.
