# Phenomenal Conservatism (Foundationalism)

I accept **phenomenal conservatism** (PC) as the right epistemology: justification flows defeasibly from how things seem. If it seems to me that P, I am thereby (prima facie) justified in believing P, unless and until some other appearance gives me a specific reason to doubt it. This is a form of foundationalism — appearances are the foundational source of justification — but a generous one: sensory experiences, memory, introspection, and intellectual intuitions all count as "appearances" on equal footing.

The argumentative path:

- **Belief-formation is in fact appearance-tracking.** When you actually pursue truth (setting aside wishful thinking, leaps of faith, and other agreed-bad cases), your beliefs are based on what seems right to you. Even arguments work this way: the basic premises must seem correct, and each step must seem to follow, or the argument moves you not at all.
- **Self-defeat of the rivals.** Any theory that denies appearance-based justification is self-undermining: the theory itself is held because it seems right to its proponent. So if appearances don't justify, the theory itself isn't justified. The way to avoid self-defeat is to grant that some appearances confer justification — and there is no principled way to restrict which species count (sensory vs. intuitive vs. introspective). The unrestricted version is PC.
- **No prior verification required.** The skeptic demands that we first verify the reliability of any belief-forming faculty before trusting it. But that demand leads to either infinite regress or epistemic circularity (cf. KRV §7.2, §7.5.5). The right policy is the inverse: trust appearances unless and until defeated. This is what we actually do — and the alternative collapses into global skepticism, which is itself self-refuting.
- **Unified explanatory power.** PC accounts for external-world knowledge, mathematical knowledge, moral knowledge, memory, knowledge of the past — all from one principle. Most rival theories struggle to deliver even one or two of these without ad hoc additions; PC delivers them as a package.
- **Why this matters for the skepticism essays.** PC is the positive epistemology that *grounds* the Moorean shift. The reason "I have hands" carries more initial plausibility than the skeptic's premises (B) and (C) is that "I have hands" rests on a vivid sensory appearance plus an intuitive seeming, while (B) and (C) rest on more fragile theoretical intuitions about the structure of knowledge. Without PC (or something like it) the comparative-plausibility move looks ad hoc; with PC it falls out of the general epistemology.

Why I reject the rivals:

- **Strict Cartesian foundationalism** — restricts foundational beliefs to indubitable introspective and necessary truths. Too narrow: leaves perception, memory, and induction without foundational grounding, which then has to be reconstructed inferentially (and historically, hasn't been).
- **Coherentism** — circular at base. A coherent fairy-tale and an accurate worldview can be equally internally consistent; coherence alone can't anchor either to reality.
- **Plain reliabilism** (without PC) — runs into the skeptic's prior-verification regress: how do you know your faculty is reliable without first using a faculty?
- **Restricted PC** ("only intellectual intuitions count, not sensory appearances", as some skeptics say) — arbitrary. There is no principled reason to privilege one species of appearance over another, and the restriction is itself based on an appearance.

In detail: see Huemer *Knowledge, Reality, and Value* §7.6 — §7.6.1 [222-239] for the thesis, §7.6.2 [240-259] for the self-defeat argument, §7.6.3 [260-265] for why PC is a good theory; and §7.5 [122-219] for the surrounding case for foundationalism (especially §7.5.1 the foundationalist view, §7.5.4 the two-kinds-of-reasons distinction).

**Usage:** Supporting epistemology, not the headline thesis. Almost no PY1020 prompt is *about* foundationalism or phenomenal conservatism directly; this position is here to be drawn on in the body of essays whose main subject is something else (typically First-Meditation skepticism, Moore's reply, or the cogito) — to explain *why* the appeal to seemings/initial plausibility is principled rather than ad hoc, *why* "I have hands" can count as foundationally justified, *why* we need not first verify perception before trusting it. Pair with the Moorean-response position. Do not make PC the conclusion of an essay unless the prompt explicitly asks about foundationalism, the structure of justification, or the source of foundational warrant. When the prompt is about Descartes or Moore, the conclusion remains the Moorean-response position; PC is the epistemology underneath it.
