# The Analytic/Synthetic Distinction

The distinction is real. An analytic statement is one that can be transformed into a logical truth by substituting synonymous expressions — "all bachelors are unmarried" goes to "all unmarried men are unmarried" by definition. A synthetic statement is one whose truth is not so fixed. This is a clear and useful distinction, and Quine's attempt in *Two Dogmas of Empiricism* to dissolve it does not succeed.

That said, Quine packaged two theses together and only one of them needs rejecting. The semantic thesis — that there is no coherent notion of analyticity because there is no coherent notion of synonymy or meaning — is wrong. The epistemic thesis — confirmation holism, that empirical hypotheses face the tribunal of experience only in bundles with auxiliary assumptions — is, on its own, plausible and probably correct in philosophy of science. The mistake is treating one as evidence for the other.

The argumentative path:

- **The distinction is teachable and reliable.** Anyone who has been shown a few examples ("all bachelors are unmarried", "all octagons have eight sides", "all sisters are siblings") can extend the list to fresh cases nobody coached them on. Agreement is near-universal among speakers competent in the relevant words. A distinction that ordinary practice tracks this reliably is not nonsense, whatever conceptual difficulties surround its formal definition.
- **Quine's attack on "synonymy" overreaches.** Once "analytic" is explained in terms of synonymy, and synonymy in terms of meaning, Quine claims not to understand "meaning". But by that standard no concept survives — every explanation eventually bottoms out in ordinary words. "Synonymous" is itself an ordinary English word used in its ordinary sense; refusing to grasp it is a rhetorical move, not an argument (Grice & Strawson, "In Defense of a Dogma").
- **Hard cases don't dissolve the distinction.** Quine's "all green things are extended" is meant to embarrass the dichotomy, but borderline cases are normal for almost any concept (the existence of reddish-orange things doesn't show that nothing is red). And the case isn't even hard once you notice that "green" has only an ostensive definition — there is no synonym to substitute, so the sentence is straightforwardly synthetic.
- **Radical empiricism is self-defeating.** Quine's view rejects analytic *a priori* knowledge and treats every claim as empirical. But the claim "all knowledge is empirical" is itself synthetic (it's not true by definition) and is not supported by any observation — so by its own lights it cannot be known. Empiricism in this strong form refutes itself.
- **Confirmation holism, however, survives.** The Duhem-Quine point — that a hypothesis is never tested alone, only together with its auxiliary assumptions, instruments, background theory — is a real insight about how empirical science works. When a prediction fails, the failure does not pinpoint which member of the bundle was wrong. This is a thesis about the structure of *evidence and testing*, and it stands independently of any view about meaning.
- **Confirmation holism does not entail meaning holism.** Even if every empirical claim is confirmed only as part of a web, analytic statements are not confirmed at all — they are constitutive of the meanings that make confirmation possible in the first place. The two theses live on different levels: one is about how we test claims, the other about how words have content. Quine conflated them; they should be separated.

So: keep the distinction. Concede the philosophy-of-science point about holistic testing. Refuse the slide from "isolated claims are hard to test" to "there is no such thing as truth-in-virtue-of-meaning".

Essentialism*

**Usage:** Default conclusion when the prompt asks whether the analytic/synthetic distinction is defensible, whether Quine's *Two Dogmas* succeeds, or whether all knowledge is empirical. When the prompt is about essentialism or the necessary *a posteriori* more broadly, this position is background only — invoke it briefly to legitimise talk of analyticity, then move on. The confirmation-holism concession should appear in any full treatment of *Two Dogmas*; skip it only when the prompt narrowly asks about the *semantic* arguments.
