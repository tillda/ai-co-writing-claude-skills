# Cartesian dualism and Strawson's case against it

**Question:** Is dualism compatible with ordinary thought and talk about persons?
**Answer:** No: ordinary person-talk treats a person as a single subject of both physical and psychological predicates, which the dualist cannot reconstruct from a mind plus a body without losing what makes person-ascription work.

**Question:** What is Cartesian dualism? Is Strawson's case against it successful?
**Answer:** Cartesian dualism is the doctrine that mind and body are distinct substances of fundamentally different kinds, joined by causal interaction; Strawson's case is successful at its target — the dualist analysis of persons — even if it does not by itself settle every question in the philosophy of mind.

**Question:** 'The difference between the Cartesian and his opponent is a difference of view about the relation between the concept of a person on the one hand and the concept of a person's mind on the other' (Strawson). Discuss.
**Answer:** The Cartesian treats "person's mind" as the prior concept and tries to build "person" out of mind plus body, while Strawson treats "person" as the prior concept and treats "person's mind" as derivative; the second view is right because the first cannot accommodate how we actually individuate persons.

**Question:** Explain and assess Strawson's reasons for thinking (i) a Cartesian is committed to thinking that a dualist reduction or analysis of the idea of a person is possible and (ii) that such a reduction or analysis is not possible.
**Answer:** (i) The Cartesian is committed to the reduction because a dualism of substances forces the person to be either one of the two or a conjunction of them; (ii) the reduction fails because minds, taken as independent substances, cannot be individuated without already presupposing the persons whose minds they are.

Cartesian dualism is the view that a human being is, fundamentally, a composition of two distinct substances — an immaterial mind and a material body — that causally interact. The position has its source in Descartes's *Meditations*, especially the Second, where the meditator finds that thought is the only property he cannot suppose himself to lack, and concludes that he is, in the strict sense, only a thing that thinks. Subsequent passages develop this into a metaphysical thesis: mind and body are different sorts of substance, with thought as the essence of mind and extension as the essence of body, and a person is the union of the two. Strawson's case against this doctrine is one of the most influential twentieth-century replies. In my view, the case succeeds at its specific target — the dualist analysis of personhood — and supports the broader rejection of substance dualism in favour of some form of physicalism, though it does not by itself argue for physicalism.

The first specific asks whether dualism is compatible with ordinary thought and talk about persons. To answer this we have to look at what such talk actually does. We say things like "she is six feet tall and depressed", "he is a fast runner and a kind man", "you are tired". These predications attach physical predicates and psychological predicates to one and the same subject. The subject in question is the person. There is no implication, in the use of these predicates, that "she" splits into a body which is six feet tall and a separate mind which is depressed. The grammar of person-ascription resists that split. Ordinary thought and talk treat a person as a single subject of both kinds of predicate.

Cartesian dualism cannot accommodate this without distortion. If a person is a composite of two substances, then strictly speaking the body is six feet tall and the mind is depressed, and the predication of both to the person is at best a loose way of speaking about a conjunction. The Cartesian has to read "she is six feet tall and depressed" as shorthand for "her body is six feet tall and her mind is depressed". This loses something, namely the fact that the unified subject of the predications is metaphysically primitive, not the result of conjoining substances. Ordinary talk takes the person as basic and the body and mind as aspects of the person; the Cartesian inverts this and takes mind and body as basic.

This brings us to Strawson's central argument, which is best understood as a claim about which concept is prior. The Cartesian, Strawson says, is committed to a *reduction* or *analysis* of the concept of a person — the person is to be understood as mind plus body, and so the concept of a person is built up from concepts of two more basic things. Strawson's response is that no such reduction is available. Consider what it would take. To analyse "person" as "mind + body" we need to be able to identify minds independently of persons and bodies independently of persons, and then build the person concept by composition. Identifying bodies independently is straightforward. Identifying minds independently is not.

The question Strawson presses is how we are supposed to individuate minds. To talk coherently about minds at all we must know the difference between one mind and two. But minds, as the Cartesian conceives them — immaterial, non-spatial substances — have no obvious means of individuation. They are not at any place; they have no spatial relations to one another. We cannot point to one and then to another. The only way we have of distinguishing one mind from another is via the persons whose minds they are: this person's thoughts, that person's thoughts. So mind-individuation presupposes person-individuation. If that is so, the concept of a person is more basic than the concept of a mind, not the other way round. The Cartesian's intended reduction reverses the order of priority and so fails.

This handles the second specific. Strawson's case is successful against the dualist analysis of personhood. He shows that the Cartesian needs the reduction to make sense of how mind and body combine into a person, but the reduction is not available because the materials it would require — independently individuated minds — cannot be supplied.

The third specific is the Strawson quote. The disagreement, on Strawson's framing, is about the relation between two concepts, not directly about substances. The Cartesian thinks the concept of a person is derivative of the concept of a mind plus the concept of a body. Strawson thinks the concept of a person is primitive — neither derived from nor reducible to those of mind and body. On Strawson's view, "her mind" makes sense only because we already have the person; "his body" makes sense in a different way, since bodies can be picked out by their physical properties, but even there the body of a person is not just any body, it is the body of *that* person. So the disagreement is about which concept does the explanatory work. Strawson is right that the person concept does the work, because the alternative requires individuating minds in a way that cannot be done.

The fourth specific asks for the assessment in pieces. (i) Why is the Cartesian committed to a reduction of the person? Because if mind and body are two distinct substances, the person must either be identified with one of them or with a composite of both. Identifying the person with the mind alone leaves out the body in a way that is hard to reconcile with our talk; identifying the person with the body leaves out the mind in the same way. The remaining option is the composite, and this is the dualist reduction. The Cartesian framework forces the composite reading. (ii) Why does the reduction fail? Because the composite "mind + body" requires its components to be available for combination, and minds, on the Cartesian conception, are not so available. Their individuation depends on the persons they belong to, so they cannot be the building blocks from which persons are constructed. The reduction is circular.

A natural objection is that even if the Strawsonian point about person-individuation is correct, the Cartesian could retreat to a less ambitious form of dualism — say, property dualism — that does not require the person to be a composite of substances. This is fair, and shows that Strawson's case is targeted at *substance* dualism specifically. It does not by itself rule out every dualism. But that is not a flaw of the argument; it is a feature of its scope. Where it operates, it operates well.

The argument also fits with other anti-dualist considerations that mutually reinforce it. The Cartesian's interaction problem — how an immaterial substance can causally affect a material one — has no satisfactory answer. Ryle's category-mistake critique shows that treating "the mind" as an inner item alongside the body misconstrues the kind of thing the mind is. And the better positive view, in my opinion, is one on which consciousness is genuinely non-physical *as a process* but not as a *substance*: it is what the brain is doing, in the same way fire is what burning fuel is doing, or life is what organised biological matter is doing. We do not posit a non-physical fire-substance; we should not posit a non-physical mind-substance. Strawson's argument, by undermining the dualist analysis of persons, clears the ground for this kind of physicalist account.

So the answer to all four specifics points the same way. Dualism is not compatible with ordinary thought and talk about persons. Strawson's case against it is successful within its scope. The disagreement Strawson identifies is genuine and the Cartesian loses it. The reduction the Cartesian needs cannot be carried out. The right view treats persons as primitive and treats mental and physical predicates as predicates of persons rather than as inhabitants of two separate worlds joined together.
