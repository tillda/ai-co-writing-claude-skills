# 'I shall suppose that... some malicious demon of the utmost power and cunning has employed all his energies in order to deceive me.' What leads Descartes to make this supposition?

Descartes introduces the malicious demon for two related reasons. First, his earlier sceptical arguments — the senses argument and the dreaming argument — leave certain beliefs untouched, in particular the truths of arithmetic and geometry. He needs a stronger scenario to extend doubt to those. Second, the demon plays a psychological role as well as a logical one: it gives the meditator a vivid counterweight to the habitual confidence in ordinary belief that, Descartes admits, keeps creeping back. In my view the first reason is the more important one philosophically, but the second is essential to the project of the meditation as a whole.

To see why the demon is needed, we should look at where the previous arguments stop. The senses argument doubts beliefs based on unreliable perception, but only the kind of perception that is known to deceive. It does not undermine the simple, direct perceptual beliefs we form in good light, close to the object. The dreaming argument extends doubt much further: from any present sensory experience, I cannot rule out that I am merely dreaming it, so my perceptual beliefs about the external world are doubtful. But Descartes himself thinks the dream argument has a limit. Even within a dream, certain very general categories — extension, shape, number — must apply. Dreams are like paintings, which combine real colours and shapes even when the figures depicted are imaginary. So the dream argument leaves arithmetic and geometry standing. Two and three make five whether I am awake or asleep. A square has four sides in any world. These look immune to the dream.

Descartes notices that something more is needed if the doubt is to be radical. He first considers an all-powerful God who could have made him so that he goes wrong every time he adds or counts. The natural objection is that God is good and would not deceive us. Descartes replies that God allows us to be deceived occasionally, and so universal deception cannot be flatly inconsistent with his goodness either. The dilemma is then: if there is an all-powerful God, he could have made me a constant deceiver; if there is no such God, my faculties owe themselves to chance or fate, and a less perfect cause makes constant error more, not less, likely. Either horn supports the same conclusion. My faculties might be radically defective, and the truths of arithmetic might fail to be what they seem.

The demon scenario is the dramatic expression of this conclusion. Suppose, Descartes asks, that some powerful and cunning being has employed all his energies to deceive me. Then I have no hands, no body, no world; the whole panorama of experience is the demon's invention; even my judgements that two and three make five are products of a faculty he has corrupted. Notice what the demon adds beyond the dream: the dream changes the world but leaves my reasoning intact, while the demon corrupts the reasoning itself. That is what extends doubt to mathematics. The dream argument leaves me a clear-headed reasoner with bad sensory inputs. The demon argument denies me even a clear head.

This is the philosophical reason for the supposition. There is also a more practical reason. Descartes is doing something difficult: he is trying not just to acknowledge intellectually that all his beliefs are doubtful, but to live, for the duration of the meditation, as if they were. The trouble is that habitual belief reasserts itself. Even after he has admitted that everything is in doubt, his old opinions keep coming back and capturing his assent. He compares himself to a prisoner who is dreaming of freedom and prefers not to wake up. To resist this pull he needs a vivid mental image to hold in mind — something that makes the doubt feel real. The demon is that image. By picturing the deceiver as actively working to mislead him, Descartes gives himself a counterweight to the gravity of common-sense belief.

Some commentators read the demon as if it were a separate sceptical argument, but it is really a vivid restatement of the deceiving-God argument. The deceiving-God argument says: my faculties might be such that I am deceived even about transparent truths. The demon says the same thing in concrete form. The advantage of the concrete form is psychological. It is hard to keep in view that 'my faculties might be defective in some unknown way.' It is much easier to keep in view a single cunning enemy bent on my deception. The demon is a thought-experiment that recruits the imagination to support an austere conclusion that the intellect alone struggles to maintain.

A natural objection is that the demon is not really a fresh sceptical scenario but only a vivid replacement for the deceiving-God idea. Descartes himself half-acknowledges this. He says he will suppose the deceiver is not God but rather some malicious demon. Why the substitution? One reason is that Descartes does not want his doubts to depend on the supposition that God is actively bad. His aim is to find what survives radical doubt, and the meditation can proceed even if we set aside any view about God's character. Replacing God with a separate demon decouples the sceptical hypothesis from theology and lets the doubt stand on its own.

So what leads Descartes to make the supposition is, first, that the previous arguments fail to reach far enough — arithmetic and geometry survive both the senses and the dream — and, second, that the doubt has to be made vivid enough to resist the pull of habit. The demon is the supposition that fills both gaps. It corrupts even the rational faculties that the dream had left alone, and it gives the meditator a concrete, narratively gripping enemy to set against the habitual return of common-sense belief.

The supposition has been criticised. One might argue that the demon is incoherent — that no being can corrupt logic or arithmetic without those faculties remaining minimally functional. Or one might argue that the Cartesian standard of certainty is too high, and that a bare logical possibility of deception does not give a real reason for doubt. These are genuine criticisms of the use Descartes makes of the demon, but they do not affect the question of why he introduces it. He introduces it because the project of doubting everything cannot be carried through on the strength of the senses and dreams alone, and because a purely intellectual concession to doubt is not enough to overcome the habits of ordinary belief.

The demon, then, is not an idle flourish. It is the engine of radical doubt and the mental discipline that lets that doubt be sustained.
