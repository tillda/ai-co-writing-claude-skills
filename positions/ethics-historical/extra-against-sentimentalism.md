# Sentimentalism Cannot Be the Foundation of Morality

Hume's claim that moral discrimination is a matter of sentiment, not reason, is the historical ancestor of contemporary subjectivism. The descendants — speaker subjectivism, cultural relativism, ideal-observer theory — fail in ways that retro-fit onto Hume's view. The first-order moral psychology of *Treatise* III and *Enquiry* II is not the harmless residue Hume's defenders treat it as.

- **Disagreement collapses.** If "X is wrong" reduces to "I (or my culture) disapprove of X", then when one speaker says "X is wrong" and another "X is not wrong" they make consistent claims and cannot really be disagreeing. They plainly are. Cultural relativism faces the same problem at one remove and adds a fresh one: the reformer (Wilberforce, King) becomes by definition wrong about her own society's morality, since the prevailing customs settle what is right.
- **The ideal observer dodges by collapsing.** If "ideal sensitivity" is allowed to include moral sensitivity, the theory presupposes the very evaluative facts it was supposed to explain — non-reductive, harmless to intuitionism. If it is restricted to non-moral sensitivities, it is wide open whether the ideal observer's approvals track what is actually good, and the theory loses its claim to be about morality at all.
- **Hume inherits the structural problem.** Sentimentalism owes us an account of why a particular subset of psychological responses (sympathy-based) rather than another (hate-based, fear-based) determines the moral facts. Hume has no answer that is not effectively ad hoc.

**Usage:** Use for Hume essays on the sentimentalist account of moral judgment; deployable also when the prompt asks about convergence in moral judgments (sentimentalism predicts convergence only where sympathies happen to align, which is too thin).

**Applicable to:** Hume's moral sentimentalism, moral judgments do not arise from reason, the relation between virtue and pleasure, convergence in moral judgments, subjectivism, cultural relativism, ideal observer theory, *ethics-historical / 3. Hume*
