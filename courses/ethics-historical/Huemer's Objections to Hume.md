# Huemer's Objections to Hume

A synthesis drawn from chapters 4, 7, and 9 of Michael Huemer's *Ethical Intuitionism*. The focus is the family of Humean doctrines that Huemer treats as the central obstacle to moral realism: the Humean Conception of Reasons, Hume's denial that actions can be rational or irrational, the natural-sympathy account of morality, and the broader anti-realist programme that Humean assumptions sustain. Hume's Law (the is-ought gap) is set aside, since on that one issue Huemer agrees with Hume rather than objecting.

## 1. The target: the Humean Conception of Reasons

Huemer's primary target, set out in chapter 7.1, is a tripartite package widely accepted in modern philosophy and economics:

1. The Humean Conception of Reasons. All reasons for action derive from pre-existing desires. A motivating reason to do A exists if and only if one has some standing desire that A would help satisfy; a normative reason to do A exists if and only if one has some standing desire that A could be reasonably expected to satisfy.
2. The Belief/Desire Gap. Beliefs alone never entail desires; one can believe any objective fact while lacking any specific desire.
3. The Magnetism of Values. Moral attitudes necessarily provide some motivational pull, and moral facts (if any exist) provide normative reasons whether or not the agent currently desires anything relevant.

These three premises generate two anti-realist arguments. The first concludes that moral attitudes cannot be beliefs about objective facts (since moral attitudes magnetize, beliefs about objective facts do not). The second concludes that there are no objective moral facts at all (since moral facts would have to entail desires, which no objective fact can do). Both arguments rest on the Humean Conception. Huemer's strategy is to grant the Belief/Desire Gap and grant a suitably qualified Magnetism, but to reject the Humean Conception itself. If reasons are not all desire-derived, the anti-realist argument collapses regardless of whether Magnetism holds.

## 2. The Humean Conception is not introspectively obvious

Most defenders of the Humean Conception treat it as obvious. Huemer's first move (section 7.4.1) is to undermine this appeal to obviousness rather than to attack the principle frontally.

His method is to point out that very general intuitions are often illusions of completeness. Consider the principle that two solid objects cannot occupy the same space at the same time. It seems unimpeachable until someone shows you Lumpy the lump of clay and Venus the statue made from it: the two are distinct (Lumpy existed first, has different persistence conditions), both are solid, and they share a region of space. The intuition was firm, widely shared, and wrong. It seemed obvious only because we were running through cases like hands-and-tables and not running through cases like statues-and-clay.

Huemer claims the Humean Conception is obvious in just the same restricted way. When you run through ordinary beliefs, the belief that the house is on fire, the belief that 13 is prime, the belief that weasels are mammals, it does feel implausible that any of these could motivate action without a desire to back them up. The reason is intelligible: such beliefs do not pick out any particular action as the one to take. Telling a man at the train station that the train for New Brunswick is leaving does not motivate him in any direction unless he already has some attitude toward going to New Brunswick.

But evaluative beliefs are not like this. They do pick out a course of action. The belief that promise-keeping is good bears a special relation to promise-keeping that no descriptive belief bears to any specific act. Once we run through evaluative beliefs alongside descriptive ones, the principle that beliefs cannot be inherently motivating loses its appearance of obviousness. The intuition that originally sustained the Humean Conception was an artefact of looking only at non-evaluative beliefs.

The next move (section 7.3) is the rationalist taxonomy of motivation: appetites, emotions, prudence, and impartial reasons. Appetites and emotions are correctly classed as desires; the Humean is right about them. Prudential and impartial motives are not. Consider the student who says she does not want to study, but knows she needs to. Consider the judge who admits he wants the bribe more than he wants the criminal punished, but recognises that taking the bribe would be wrong. The natural reading of these dialogues is that the agent has appetitive or emotional desire pulling one way and a non-desire-based motivation, prudential or moral, pulling the other.

The Humean has two replies, and Huemer thinks both fail. The first reply is to insist that the student "really" wants to study and the judge "really" wants to refuse the bribe. But this just reads desire off behaviour, contradicts what the agents say about themselves, and is unsupported by introspection. The second reply is to broaden "desire" to mean any motivation whatsoever. This trivialises the Humean Conception into the tautology that motives for action require motives for action. On this broadened reading, beliefs can themselves be desires, the Belief/Desire Gap collapses, and the entire anti-realist argument loses its premise.

The structure of this critique is important: Huemer is not denying the existence of desires or their motivational role. He is denying that all motivation is desire-based, and he is showing that the Humean is forced to choose between an introspectively false claim and a trivial one.

## 3. Hume's own argument against rationalism is invalid

Hume's own positive argument for the Humean Conception, given in the *Treatise*, runs like this: the function of reason is to determine what is true and false; actions cannot be true or false; therefore reason cannot evaluate actions; therefore actions cannot be rational or irrational. Huemer (section 7.4.1) treats this as a bad argument and produces a clean parallel.

Reason can only judge truth and falsity. Numbers cannot be true or false. Therefore reason cannot make judgments about numbers, so reason cannot tell us that seven is prime. The conclusion is plainly false; reason routinely tells us things about numbers. The natural Humean reply is that although numbers themselves are not truth-bearers, propositions about them are, and that is what reason judges. But once that reply is granted, exactly the same reply is available to the rationalist: actions are not truth-bearers, but propositions about actions are, including the proposition that an action is right; and that is why reason can evaluate actions.

This is the simplest and probably the most decisive of Huemer's objections. The argument Hume gives in his own voice for the Humean Conception is invalid by the same standards Hume would have to apply to other domains where reason demonstrably operates. So even if the Humean Conception turns out to be defensible on other grounds, it cannot be defended by Hume's official argument.

## 4. Smith's direction-of-fit argument equivocates

Michael Smith offers a more sophisticated argument for the same conclusion: beliefs have mind-to-world direction of fit (they aim to match reality), desires have world-to-mind direction of fit (they aim to make reality match them), having a goal is being in a state with world-to-mind direction of fit, therefore beliefs cannot be motivating reasons. Huemer's reply (section 7.4.2) targets the second premise.

The phrase "world-to-mind direction of fit" is metaphorical. Cashed out literally, it might mean either of two things:
- (2C) Having a goal is being in a state such that, when one notices the *content* of that state is unrealised, one tries to change the world to bring about that content.
- (2G) Having a goal is being in a state such that, when one notices the *goal represented by* that state is unrealised, one tries to change the world to bring about that goal.

For the Humean these collapse, since on the Humean view the content of a desire just is the goal it represents (when I desire to take over the world, the content "that I take over the world" is identical to the goal of taking over the world). For the rationalist they come apart. On the rationalist view, my having the goal of being happy might consist in my believing that my happiness is good. The content of that belief is "my happiness is good"; the goal it represents is my happiness.

Read as 2C, premise 2 of Smith's argument is false on rationalism: discovering that happiness is not good would not lead me to try to change the facts of ethics. Read as 2G, premise 2 is acceptable, but premise 3 must then be reinterpreted to track the goal rather than the content, and on that reinterpretation premise 3 is false: if I notice that the goal represented by my "my happiness is good" belief is unrealised, I will of course try to make myself happy. Either reading invalidates the argument. The only way to make both premises come out true simultaneously is to assume, with the Humean, that content and represented goal are the same thing, which is precisely what is at issue. Smith's argument therefore begs the question against the rationalist.

## 5. The Neo-Humean rescue attempts fail systematically

Hume himself drew the most extreme conclusions: actions cannot be reasonable or unreasonable, and it is not contrary to reason to prefer the destruction of the world to the scratching of one's finger. Modern Humeans recognise this is absurd, and they propose constraints on rational action that are supposed to fall out of the Humean Conception while distinguishing rational from irrational behaviour. Huemer (section 7.5) considers five and argues that none survive scrutiny.

### The Foresight Constraint

The proposal is that rational action must take account of one's future desires, not just one's current ones. The student who parties tonight is irrational because she will want to have studied tomorrow. Huemer's objection is that on a strictly Humean conception, only present desires can motivate or justify present actions. A future desire I do not yet have can no more move me now than a past desire I no longer have. Future desires can become relevant only via a present second-order desire to have one's future desires satisfied, but the Humean has no principled reason to demand such a second-order desire, just as he has no principled reason to demand that I want anyone else's desires satisfied. Furthermore, the contrast cases pull apart sharply: Odysseus is rational to bind himself against his future Sirens-desire; the partying student is irrational to ignore her future exam-desire. The Humean has no resources to discriminate, because in both cases the agent has a present preference contrary to the future desire. The discriminating factor is evaluative: Odysseus's future desire would be a desire for the bad, the student's future desire would be a desire for the good.

### The Imagination Constraint

The proposal is that rational action must take account of desires one would form on vivid and accurate imagining. Again the contrast cases pull apart. The patient at the hospital is rational to refuse to vividly imagine the surgery and proceed with the operation. The donor considering Oxfam is irrational to refuse to vividly imagine African poverty and spend the money on a stereo. On the Humean view both should reason in the same direction: avoid the vivid imagining whose induced desire conflicts with present preference. Common sense distinguishes the cases by appealing to evaluative facts: the surgical-flight desire would be misguided, the Oxfam desire would track something genuinely bad in the world. Without such facts, the Humean cannot motivate the constraint selectively, and motivating it universally yields the absurd verdict that the Oxfam refuser is rational.

### The Consistency and Coherence Constraints

The proposal is that one must have a logically consistent and well-systematised set of desires. Huemer's reply trades on Smith's own direction-of-fit point against him. Inconsistent beliefs are defective because they cannot be true and beliefs aim at truth. Inconsistent desires cannot all be satisfied, but desires do not aim at being satisfied in the way beliefs aim at being true; they aim at making reality match them. The unsatisfiability of a desire does not mark it as defective. If I want there to be a God and someone proves there is none, my belief is shown wrong but my wish is not. Furthermore, if "hard to satisfy" were a Humean ground for revising desire, we would have reason to abandon the desire that world hunger end (it is, after all, hard to satisfy), which is plainly not what the constraint should yield. Coherence fares no better: the reason coherence is a virtue in beliefs is that more coherent belief sets are more likely to be by-and-large true. Desires have no parallel aim, so the parallel argument cannot be run. Smith, the Humean defender of the coherence constraint, is the very philosopher whose direction-of-fit point sinks it.

### The Deliberation Constraint

The last and most tenacious Neo-Humean tenet is that one is rationally required to deliberate about how to satisfy one's desires and to act on the deliberation's results. Huemer's argument here (section 7.5.4) is a regress. Suppose you choose career A after five minutes of thought, and a longer deliberation would have favoured career B. Either you had no control over the brevity of your deliberation, in which case you cannot be rationally criticised for it (one cannot be unreasonable for failing to do the impossible); or you had control over it, in which case the question simply transfers: was your decision to deliberate only briefly itself rational? On the Humean account, your shorter deliberation was rational given the beliefs and desires you had at the time about how long to deliberate. So the criticism shifts to those beliefs, which raises the same question, and so on without end. On Humean grounds, every act of choice is rational given the agent's mental states at the moment of choice; criticism only ever bites by relocating to a prior mental state, generating an infinite regress that cannot terminate. Therefore no Humean criticism of any action can succeed.

The structural moral of section 7.5 is that each Neo-Humean constraint either fails to follow from Humean premises (Foresight, Imagination), turns out to mis-describe the nature of desire (Consistency, Coherence), or generates a regress that swallows the whole conception (Deliberation). The only way to draw the discriminations we actually want to draw is to admit evaluative facts. That admission is precisely what intuitionism asks for.

## 6. Hume cannot account for the authority of morality

Section 7.6 adds a constructive dimension. Even if Humeanism could draw some rational/irrational distinction, it could not explain why morality has the kind of authority we ordinarily take it to have, namely, that morality trumps prudence, which in turn trumps desire.

Huemer's rationalist explanation is structural. Prudential judgment is not a desire competing with appetites and emotions; it is an intellectual evaluation that already takes those desires into account. Weighing one's appetite for ice cream against one's prudential judgment that one should eat the salad is double-counting, since the prudential judgment was formed by considering the appetite already. The same relation holds between prudence and morality: a moral judgment that one should sacrifice some self-interest already incorporates self-interest in its formation; weighing self-interest against the moral judgment double-counts.

The Humean has nothing to put in place of this. On the Humean view, all motivations are desires of various strengths, and morality is at best a particular subset of desires, traceable, on Hume's own account, to natural sympathy. But sympathy is one emotion among others. Hate, envy, and lust are equally emotions, and the Humean has no principled reason to privilege the desires sympathy generates over those generated by hostility. Moral theorising on the Humean view amounts to the systematic study of how to satisfy a particular emotional tendency, and there is no Humean argument that this tendency deserves special weight when it conflicts with other tendencies. The Humean attempt to ground morality in the conjunction of all desires fails for the same reason: we do not in fact regard hate-desires and love-desires as on a par when assessing moral reasons. The Humean attempt to ground morality in second-order desires fails too: someone who hates women and wants to continue hating them does not thereby acquire a moral motive. Humeanism makes morality an arbitrarily privileged subset of psychology.

## 7. Hume cannot accommodate weakness of will

Section 7.6.3 turns to weakness of will, which Huemer treats as decisive against the Humean Conception. Weak-willed action has a stable phenomenology: the agent judges A best, feels tempted by B, finds A difficult and B easy, experiences guilt on doing B, and is judged by us to be irrational. Crucially, the agent acts voluntarily; nothing about weakness of will is consistent with muscle spasm, addiction in the strong sense, or alien personality.

For the Humean, weakness of will is not just hard to explain; it is unintelligible. On the Humean view, when motives conflict, the stronger desire wins. If B answers to the stronger desire, choosing B is not weakness, it is rationality. If A answers to the stronger desire and the agent somehow chooses B, that is not weakness either; it is unintelligible. The asymmetries of difficulty, guilt, and irrationality cannot be reconstructed from differences of desire strength alone. The Humean reply that the conflicting desires happen to be exactly equal fails: the probability of two distinct continuous-strength desires being exactly equal is zero, and the equal-strength story does not explain the asymmetries anyway.

The rationalist conception, by contrast, posits desires and normative judgments as motivations of different kinds, not commensurable along a single dimension of strength. The agent's normative judgment that A is best does not enter the contest as just-another-desire-with-some-strength; it operates as a different kind of consideration altogether. That is why one course of action is "harder" while the other is "easier", why guilt attaches to one outcome and not the other, and why rational criticism gets a foothold. Huemer is careful to note that this does not yet explain how free will works; it only shows that the rationalist has the conceptual room to accommodate weakness of will, while the Humean does not.

## 8. Humean anti-realism corrodes moral belief and motivation

Section 7.6.4 and section 9.5 advance a further, more practical objection. If the Humean Conception is wedded to anti-realism (as in Mackie, Ruse, and most contemporary metaethics), the package corrodes the very institution of morality. This is not offered as evidence that anti-realism is false. It is offered as evidence that, if anti-realism is false, accepting it is harmful.

The argument works in three steps. First, Huemer cites anti-realists themselves admitting that ordinary moral discourse is committed to objectivity (Mackie) and that morality is socially functional only insofar as it appears to agents as externally binding (Ruse). On these admissions, the anti-realist programme attacks beliefs and attitudes that are essential to morality's functioning. Second, the practical argument: if the only reason to be moral is desire-satisfaction, and one's moral concern is, on honest introspection, no stronger than one's desire for a stereo or a meal out, then the recognition of "moral obligation" loses traction. The Singer-on-Oxfam example is illustrative: an agent who is told that giving away thousands of pounds is "morally required" but who also accepts that morality is just an arbitrarily privileged emotional set will rationally conclude that he need not give. Third, the cheating-husband dialogue makes the same point at the personal level: someone who has internalised that marital fidelity is an arbitrary convention, that guilt is irrational residue, and that emotional preference is the only ground of action, has no stable resource against acting on whichever desire happens to be strongest.

Huemer is honest about the dialectical status of this argument: it is not a refutation of Humeanism, only a reason to care whether Humeanism is true. But the structure of the case is important. The Humean's reply, that we can train people to have stronger sympathetic emotions, concedes the point. On the Humean view, moral reform is sentimental conditioning, not reasoned correction. There is no resource, on a Humean view, to address an agent who says he simply does not have the relevant feelings.

## 9. The cultural diagnosis: why Humeanism became orthodoxy

Chapter 9, section 9.4, offers a sociological diagnosis that complements the philosophical one. The question is why the Humean Conception and its anti-realist consequences came to seem obvious to twentieth-century philosophy when intuitionism, having been respectable in the early twentieth century, fell out of favour by mid-century.

Huemer's claim is that intuitionism was not refuted; it was bypassed. The objections that did the work, infallibility, disagreement, weirdness, all rest on epistemological double standards that no one would apply to perception, memory, or scientific reasoning. The weirdness objection is particularly thin, depending on a notion of "weird" that is either subjective (and so non-probative), counter-intuitive (which intuitionism is not, given the realism of ordinary moral discourse), or poorly understood (which fails to distinguish intuitionism from numbers, consciousness, propositions, and other things we accept). The pattern fits rationalisation rather than refutation.

The deeper drivers, on Huemer's account, are three cultural attitudes: cynicism, which makes any account of human moral motivation as something other than disguised self-interest implausible; political correctness, which makes any moral realism that judges some practices worse than others uncomfortable; and scientism, which makes any non-scientific source of knowledge automatically suspect. Each operates as a bias rather than an explicit premise: it shapes which positions feel plausible and which arguments feel "interesting", rather than figuring as a stated reason. Most academic philosophers did not arrive at anti-realism by reasoning from cynicism, political correctness, or scientism; they inherited it from teachers and colleagues who were already working in a milieu shaped by those attitudes.

The diagnostic argument is not evidence for intuitionism. It is meant to defuse the rhetorical force of the consensus against intuitionism. Once one sees that the consensus is sustained by cultural mood rather than philosophical argument, the burden of proof shifts back where Huemer thinks it belongs: onto whoever wishes to deny that pain is bad, that murder is wrong, or that moral judgments are evaluations of facts.

## 10. Where the case is strongest, where it is most contestable

Putting the chapters together, Huemer's objections to Hume cluster into three groups, of unequal strength.

The strongest objections are the ones that work by direct philosophical argument rather than appeal to introspection. The parallel-argument move against Hume's "reason judges only truth and falsity" (section 7.4.1) is decisive: if the move from "actions are not truth-bearers" to "reason cannot judge actions" worked, the same move would prove that reason cannot judge that seven is prime. The disambiguation move against Smith's direction-of-fit argument (section 7.4.2) is also tight: it shows the argument trades on an equivocation that is invisible only on Humean assumptions, and so begs the question. The regress argument against the Deliberation Constraint (section 7.5.4) is structurally elegant; it shows that no Humean criticism of an action can ever terminate without invoking something outside the Humean framework.

The middle-strength objections are the cumulative case across the Neo-Humean constraints (section 7.5), which depends on accepting Huemer's evaluative readings of the contrast cases. A determined Neo-Humean might insist that Odysseus and the Oxfam refuser are both rational, biting the bullet on the cases. Huemer's reply will then be that biting that bullet abandons most of what the Neo-Humean wanted out of the constraints in the first place. This is persuasive but not knock-down.

The weakest objections, in pure philosophical force, are the cultural-diagnostic chapter and the "anti-realism corrodes morality" argument. Huemer himself flags that neither is evidence for intuitionism. The cultural diagnosis is plausible but unfalsifiable in detail; the corrosion argument has rhetorical power but proves only that we should care whether anti-realism is true, not that it is false. They function as second-order observations rather than first-order arguments, and a careful reader should not let their rhetorical force substitute for the harder philosophical work in chapters 7.3 to 7.6.

The cumulative case nonetheless presses hard. Huemer is not picking off Hume on a single point. He is showing that the Humean Conception fails to fit the introspective phenomenology of motivation, that Hume's own argument for it is invalid, that the most sophisticated modern defence of it (Smith's) equivocates, that every Neo-Humean rescue collapses, that the resulting view cannot accommodate the authority of morality or the phenomenon of weakness of will, and that the cultural ascendancy of Humean anti-realism owes more to mood than to argument. If even half of this case lands, the standard anti-realist deployment of Humean premises against moral realism is in serious trouble.
