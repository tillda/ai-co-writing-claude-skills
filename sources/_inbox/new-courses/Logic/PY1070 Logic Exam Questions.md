# Proposed modules

These are identified modules of the course, taken form syllabus. These modules are numbered (including 0, usually for "Introduction") the numbering should be preserved in further referencing. These might help in grouping/organizing questions into major themes. Each module can have multiple topics of inquiry / of questions:

0. Introduction 
    What is logic, the difference between deductive validity and inductive strength, and the importance of logic in rationality and information extraction.

1. What is Logic? 
    The varieties of logic, formal vs. philosophical logic, the language of classical logic (formulae, connectives, and quantifiers), and the distinction between models and proofs.

2. Truth 
    The paradox of the Liar, Tarski’s schema, deflationary theories (disquotationalism and minimalism), the correspondence theory, and the principles of bivalence and the excluded middle.

3. Modality 
    The logic of necessity and possibility, the use of "boxes and diamonds" (□ and ◊), de re versus de dicto necessity, and David Lewis’s counterpart theory.

4. Conditionals 
    The relationship between the English "if" and the material conditional (→), the "bombshell" of probabilities in conditionals, and the logic of subjunctive or counterfactual conditionals.

5. Reference – Names and Descriptions 
    Russell’s theory of definite descriptions, the debate between Millian and descriptive theories of proper names, existence, and the de re/de dicto distinction in belief ascriptions.

6. Non-extensional Language 
    The limits of extensionality in classical logic, truth functionality, intensional transitive verbs (like "fears" or "thinks about"), and the problem of empty terms.

7. Vagueness 
    The Sorites paradox (the "heap"), borderline cases, many-valued logics, and the supervaluational approach to vague predicates.

8. Essentialism 
    Metaphysical necessity versus a priori knowledge, Kripke’s counterexamples to the traditional coincidence of these categories, and Quine’s arguments against the notion of essential properties.

9.  The Justification of Deduction 
    The epistemological challenge of justifying logical laws, Lewis Carroll’s dialogue between Achilles and the Tortoise, and the concept of reflective equilibrium in logical reasoning.
