Sarah Stroud argues that being a good friend requires being epistemically biased: we form different beliefs about friends than we would about strangers, by different methods, and we end up believing better of them than the impartial evidence warrants. She also argues that this bias cannot be saved by saying we just have more evidence about friends. So there is a clash between friendship and the standards of mainstream epistemology. In my view her case for the clash is sound, and the right response is to side with friendship — but not by simply biting the bullet that we should be irrational. The better lesson is that the purist conception of epistemic rationality is too narrow; friendship is one of the data points that any acceptable epistemology must accommodate.

Stroud's argument has two stages. The first is phenomenological. She asks us to consider how a good friend reacts when she hears a damning story about her friend Sam — say, that he callously broke someone's heart and then refused to return the calls. Notice that the good friend does not respond as a detached observer would. She scrutinises the source of the story more carefully, asking whether the speaker is malicious or has a stake in the report. She works harder to construct alternative interpretations — perhaps Sam is honest rather than cruel, fickle rather than predatory. And the result is not just a difference in cognitive procedure but a difference in conclusion: she ends up believing a more favourable picture of Sam than the evidence, taken impartially, would support. Stroud insists that this is not merely an empirical accident about how people happen to behave; it is constitutive of being a good friend. A friend who outwardly defended Sam while privately believing the worst of him would not be a real friend.

The second stage is the argument that this bias cannot be vindicated as ordinary evidence-weighing. Here Stroud takes on the natural objection that the friend is just a better-informed observer: she has years of background knowledge about Sam's character, and is rationally giving that knowledge appropriate weight. The objection has some bite. Sometimes the friend really does know something that places the report in a different light — that Sam was distracted by upsetting news, say. But Stroud's reply is decisive. The friend's practices do not just track evidence; they often run counter to it. She seizes on discrediting hypotheses without independent reason to think them true, and withholds belief in propositions that an impartial observer would naturally infer. And there is a deeper problem: the very background "knowledge" the friend would invoke is itself the product of years of partial interpretation. The evidence base she appeals to is already slanted. So the evidentialist defence either fails to fit the practice or saves it only by relying on a contaminated body of belief.

Putting these two stages together, Stroud reaches an uncomfortable conclusion. Friendship requires epistemic partiality, and epistemic partiality is condemned as irrational by mainstream accounts of justification. So if we want to be good friends, we must accept being, in the standard sense, less than perfectly rational believers.

Should we then choose friendship over epistemic virtue? Stroud sketches three options. The first bites the bullet: friendship is more important than epistemic rationality, so we ought to be epistemically irrational where friends are concerned. The second treats the demands as incommensurable: there is just no answer to what we ought to believe overall. The third is more interesting. It says that if standard epistemological theories condemn as irrational something we have overwhelming reason to do — namely, be friends — then perhaps the trouble is with the theories. Maybe what looks like irrational partiality is in fact within the bounds of a properly drawn account of epistemic rationality, one that does not insist on a purist ideal of objectivity divorced from a good life.

In my view the third response is right, and the first response is not so much wrong as badly framed. We should side with friendship. But "side with friendship over rationality" suggests that friendship costs us something genuinely valuable, namely correct believing. I think that diagnosis overstates the price. Consider what the so-called partial friend is actually doing. She is being slow to accept negative gossip from sources whose reliability she has not checked. She is generating and weighing alternative interpretations rather than accepting the first one offered. She is bringing her standing commitments to bear on how she classifies new information. Each of these activities, taken on its own, is something we admire in a careful believer. The trouble is only that she does these things more for friends than for strangers — and that asymmetry is what the purist condemns.

But the purist's symmetry requirement is itself doubtful. Consider an analogy. A defence lawyer is not a worse believer because she works to construct alternative explanations of the prosecution's evidence on her client's behalf. Her role gives her a reason to spend cognitive resources differently from a juror's. Stroud notices an analogous point: the friend who consistently advocates the more charitable hypothesis serves a social-epistemic function, slowing the spread of unchecked negative gossip and making the social distribution of opinion more accurate. There are recognisably epistemic values that the narrow evidentialist framing leaves out.

Now, this defence has limits. We can imagine partiality so extreme that it ceases to be epistemic at all — flat denials of obvious facts, refusals to update on incontrovertible evidence. Stroud is careful to exclude these. The partiality she is defending is interpretive: it operates in the genuinely indeterminate space where evidence underdetermines what we should think. Human actions and characters require interpretation; the same behaviour can be filed under "refreshingly forthright" or "obnoxious", and the friend's commitment shows up in which classification she reaches when the data permit either. In that space there is room for the friend's commitment to do legitimate work without licensing self-deception.

So the answer is yes, we should be partial to our friends, but not because friendship beats rationality. It is because the standard conception of rationality that makes friendship look irrational is itself defective. Stroud has shown us that friendship is one of the constraints any acceptable epistemology has to live with. A purism that cannot accommodate friendship is not a deep theory of belief; it is a parochial one.
