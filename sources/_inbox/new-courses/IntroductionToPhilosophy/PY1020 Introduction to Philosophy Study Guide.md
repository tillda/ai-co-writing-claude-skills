### Course description

In this course, students will be introduced to the methods and content of philosophy by considering, at an elementary level and in a carefully guided way, some of the central problems that arise within the subject. Included here will be:

- free will, determinism and responsibility;
- personal identity;
- the relation of the mind to the body;
- the nature of knowledge;
- the ideal of equality;
- issues raised by portrayals of tragedy;
- the reality of qualities;
- and our understanding of moral dilemmas.

Appealing to your reading of **Chapter 4** of the assigned textbook for this course, this seminar session will focus on philosophical approaches to DILEMMA, starting with a quick look at two classic illustrative examples of moral conflict from ancient Greek and 20th century philosophical literature, to then begin discussing E.J. **Lemmon's article on 'Moral Dilemmas'** which is contained in the textbook in *chapter 4*. After that, I will open a second forum extending the discussion to the work of **Philippa Foot** (Moral Dilemma revisited) and that of **Martha Nussbaum**.

#### Two classical examples beyond analytical philosophy: Plato to Sartre.

NB: the two examples below introduces us to different philosophical approaches to morality and the issue of moral dilemmas 1) Platonism and 2) Sartrean existentialism [or existential psychoanalysis].

##### Key concepts to consider here:

- **moral norms and normativity as a force** (e.g. moral force of norms and how it affects behavior and rationalisation)
- **moral obligation and duty** (as regulatory force/ side of normativity)

'In Book I of Plato's *Republic*, Cephalus defines 'justice' as speaking the truth and paying one's debts. Socrates quickly refutes this account by suggesting that it would be wrong to repay certain debts—for example, to return a borrowed weapon to a friend who is not in his right mind. Socrates' point is not that repaying debts is without moral import; rather, he wants to show that it is not always right to repay one's debts, at least not exactly when the one to whom the debt is owed demands repayment. What we have here is a conflict between two **moral norms**: repaying one's debts and protecting others from harm. And in this case, Socrates maintains that protecting others from harm is the **norm that takes priority**.'

'Nearly twenty-four centuries later, Jean-Paul Sartre described a moral conflict the resolution of which was, to many, less obvious than the resolution to the Platonic conflict. Sartre (1957) tells of a student whose brother had been killed in the German offensive of 1940. The student wanted to avenge his brother and to fight forces that he regarded as evil. But the student's mother was living with him, and he was her one consolation in life. The student believed that he had **conflicting obligations**. Sartre describes him as being torn between **two kinds of morality**: one of limited scope but certain efficacy, personal devotion to his mother; the other of much wider scope but uncertain efficacy, attempting to contribute to the defeat of an unjust aggressor.'

#### So what might these examples tell us about MORAL DILEMMAS:

What is common to the two well-known cases [above] is **conflict**. In each case, an **agent** regards herself as **having moral reasons** to do each of two actions, but doing both actions is not possible. Ethicists have called situations like these **moral dilemmas**. The crucial features of a moral dilemma are these: the agent is required to do each of two (or more) actions; the agent can do each of the actions; but the agent cannot do both (or all) of the actions. The agent thus seems **condemned to moral failure**; no matter what she does, she will do something wrong (or fail to do something that she ought to do).

The Platonic case strikes many as too easy to be characterized as a genuine moral dilemma. For the agent's solution in that case is clear; it is more important to protect people from harm than to return a borrowed weapon. And in any case, the borrowed item can be returned later, when the owner no longer poses a threat to others. Thus in this case we can say that the requirement to protect others from serious harm **overrides** the requirement to repay one's debts by returning a borrowed item when its owner so demands. When one of the conflicting requirements overrides the other, we have a conflict but not a genuine moral dilemma. So in addition to the features mentioned above, in order to **have a genuine moral dilemma it must also be true that neither of the conflicting requirements is overridden**.

[Friendship from Aristotle to contemporary analytical philosophy](#) ◎

![A classical relief depicting two figures, likely representing a philosophical discussion or relationship.]()![](_page_7_Picture_6.jpeg)

![A painting depicting a group of philosophers, including Plato, engaged in discussion.]()![](_page_7_Picture_7.jpeg)

"O my friends, there is no friend" -

The concepts of friendship or *philia*, of affection and its opposite, *phobia*, have occupied the minds of philosophers since ancient times. French philosopher Jacques Derrida saw in it interesting paradoxes, tensions yet unexplored; some which he sought to investigate in his book *The Politics of Friendship* (1994) in which he appeals to this paradoxical statement (cited above) once attributed to Aristotle by Montaigne. The concept is particularly puzzling because it refers to an event which cannot happen to the individual but necessarily involves another, a form of reciprocity which is hard to seize and yet that is all too often taken for granted, the richness of its *sense* reduced to superficial *meanings*. Derrida only but hints at the complexity of the very idea of friendship as an event in this passage from aforementioned volume:

"Perhaps, one day, here or there, who knows, something may happen between two people in love, who would love each other lovingly (is this still the right word?) in such a way that friendship, just once, perhaps, for the first time (another perhaps), will become the correct name, the right and just name for that which would then have taken place, the condition being that it take place between two, 'two people', as Nietzsche specifies. But how can you adjust a name to what could take place only once, perhaps, for the first and last time - how can you name an event? For this love that would take place only once would be the only possible event: as an impossible event. Even if the right name for this unique love were to be found, how would you convince everyone else of its appropriateness? And what about the task of convincing the partner, at the moment of the act in which this love would essentially consist, that of giving him or her the name?"

(Derrida, 1997: 66)

The specificity of this event make the ground from which it springs hard to grasp. It is an event that changes us, affects us, the way we see, speak consider others etc. One acts differently towards friends than others, but how reasonable is this? What implications might it have, politically, epistemologically, ethically? While Derrida will be particularly interested in the political implications of friendship, the two texts you will have to read in the assigned textbook for this introductory module will instead focus more on epistemology and ethics in relation to friendship. We start with a passage from Aristotle's 8th book from the *Nicomachean Ethics* and then move on to consider the contribution of contemporary analytical moral philosopher Sarah Stroud.

## SEMIMAR 6 \_ IDENTITY

[The problem of identity from Locke to Bernard Williams](#) ◎

![Illustration of John Locke, surrounded by four thought bubbles representing different aspects of identity: a church (Christ Church, Oxford), a doctor (Physician), a factory (The Factory of Commerce), and a boat (The River).]()![](_page_9_Picture_6.jpeg)

Illustration © Simon Ellinas 2023 Please visit caricatures.org.uk

In this seminar, we will be discussing the problem of identity and how it is approached in modern and contemporary philosophy by looking at the respective works of Locke and Williams. This will lead us to think of identity – the idea of being 'one and the same' – as a relation, and thus implying more than a mere technical distinction, or technical term. We will problematize the nature of this relation.

In ancient Greek philosophy, Heraclitus famously introduced the problem of identity and difference by exploring the idea that "**You cannot step into the same river twice**". This is an image of thought meant to help us think about identity conceptually and critically. As one steps a second time in the river, one may be tempted to say that one stepped into the same river. Yet, between the first and second time I step in it, the river flowed. Yes it is the same river, but is it identical – or has the flow of the water perturbed the relation? We are here dealing with the return of the same but not the identical (flow needs to be accounted for, the passage of time etc.). The same logic applies if one was to ask: **am I the same person I was yesterday?** time passed, I have experienced new things, I might have changed a little. It could be said I am the same person, but is today's me identical to yesterday's me?

**Why this matters:** the problem of identity is important when we come to consider (philosophically) how we engage with and in the world, the fact that we are constantly encountering and re-encountering things (including ourselves), often taking for granted their continuing identity.

We will start with John Locke on personal Identity, looking at chapter xxvii of the second book of his *Essay Concerning Human Understanding*. (**Chapter 7** of the textbook). We will then turn to Bernard Williams on 'the self and the future'.

### SEMIMAR 9 - QUALITIES

![Three portraits of philosophers: G. Berkeley, D. Hume, and D. Anscombe.]()![](_page_11_Picture_9.jpeg)

Size, figure, extension, duration, motion, position, color, taste, odor, sound, heat, coldness, and tactual qualities such as hardness, softness, roughness and smoothness: these are basic qualities we attach to objects in the world. But how we make sense of such qualities has been the subject of philosophical debates for a long time. The first six (to which solidity and number are sometimes added) have at times been called **"primary" qualities** (Locke); roughly speaking, they are said to be real objective properties of objects and to be distinctly known. The remaining, called **"secondary"**, are said to be in some way—metaphysically, epistemically, linguistically—derivative, less than fully real, or otherwise metaphysically feeble? misleading, subjective, ambiguous, or otherwise not perspicuous.

?From Locke to Berkeley, early modern philosophers became particularly interested in the modalities of interpretation and representation of qualities, working in tandem with the development of new sciences, which opened new questions about the nature of the world and the limits of our capacity to seize it as it is as human beings. One of the most basic questions here, would for instance be: Are things always as they seem? Someone like John Locke would argue that yes, when it comes to the primary qualities of objects in the world, but no, when it comes to their secondary qualities.

Key problems emerged in Early Modern European philosophical circles surrounding the question of qualities :

- Are things as they seem?
- Are all truths Scientific truths?
- objective and subjective interpretations of the truth.

This seminar invites you to engage with these issues, by drawing on selected passages from the work of Early Modern Philosophers like John Locke, Robert Boyle and Berkeley.
