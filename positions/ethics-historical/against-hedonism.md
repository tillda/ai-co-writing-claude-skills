# Against Hedonism (Cookie, Equality, Experience Machine)

Hedonism — pleasure (and the absence of pain) as the only intrinsic good — is the foundation Mill inherits from Bentham and tries to soften with the higher/lower distinction. The position concedes hedonism's appeal but rejects it on three counter-examples and one structural dilemma. Pleasure is intrinsically good; it is not the only intrinsic good.

The argument:

- **Cookie (desert).** A single cookie can go to a saintly person who has spent her life helping others, or to a serial killer in prison who likes cookies very slightly more. Total pleasure favours the killer. Almost everyone judges the cookie should go to the saint. The intuition is robust and easy to multiply: distributing goods by who deserves them is part of moral assessment, not reducible to maximising pleasure. Hedonism cannot accommodate this without smuggling desert into the calculation.

- **Equality (fairness).** Two equally deserving people, equally well off. You can give 100 units of benefit to one and nothing to the other, or 45 units to each. Total benefit favours the first option; almost everyone prefers the second. Fairness in distribution is part of what we are evaluating, not a function of total pleasure produced.

- **Experience Machine (reality matters).** A machine producing any desired experiences by direct brain stimulation, with the option to erase memory of life beforehand, would deliver more lifetime pleasure than nearly any actual life. Most people refuse to plug in. The refusal shows that we value living in contact with reality, having genuine relationships, and actually doing things — not just feeling as if we did. If hedonism were true, the machine would be the best possible life, which it manifestly is not.

- **Higher/lower as a structural dilemma.** The move to qualitatively higher pleasures answers the swine objection but at a cost. Either higher pleasures are higher because they yield more pleasure overall (then the distinction is empirically dubious and the criterion remains pleasure-amount, not quality) or higher pleasures are higher because they engage faculties of intrinsic worth (then a second criterion has been admitted and pleasure is no longer the sole good). The "competent judges" appeal relocates the question without resolving it: what makes the experienced judge's verdict authoritative on quality unless quality is something other than pleasure?

- **Why preferentism doesn't rescue.** The preference-satisfaction variant fares slightly better on the experience machine: the desire to live in contact with reality goes unsatisfied even when the agent is fooled. But preferentism still fails Cookie and Equality (the killer's preferences and the lop-sided distribution maximise satisfied preferences in the same way they maximise pleasure). The desert and fairness intuitions point past hedonism *and* preferentism to a pluralist account of the good.

- **Why I reject the rivals.** Strict hedonism in Bentham's form is the cleanest target and bites all three bullets. Mill's qualitative hedonism either collapses back into Bentham or quietly abandons hedonism. Preferentism is closer to defensible but still incomplete. Pluralism — pleasure, knowledge, achievement, fairness, desert as plural intrinsic goods — fits the cases without having to deny any of them.

**Usage:** Additive to a Mill essay on the conception of happiness or the higher/lower distinction. Engage Mill's own competent-judges argument first; the three counter-examples and the structural dilemma are the modern set-piece against the position Mill is trying to occupy. Do not flatten the position into "Mill is just wrong" — Mill is responding to the swine objection in a serious way; the point is that his response either fails or concedes more than he wanted.

**Applicable to:** Mill's conception of happiness, hedonism, qualitative hedonism, higher and lower pleasures, swine objection, competent judges, experience machine, intrinsic value of pleasure, does Mill remain a hedonist, preference utilitarianism, *ethics-historical / 5. Mill*
