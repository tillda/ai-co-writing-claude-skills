# Hume's Case Against Moral Reason Is Not Compelling

Hume mounts a broad case against the role of reason in ethics. Two strands of that case form one front against moral rationalism:

> **First strand — reason cannot evaluate actions.** The function of reason is to determine truth and falsity. Actions are not true or false. Therefore reason cannot evaluate them; actions cannot be rational or irrational.
>
> **Second strand — reason alone cannot move the will.** Beliefs about how the world is can never, by themselves, motivate. Only desires can. The modern reconstruction generalises this into the Argument from Motivation: if there were objective values, beliefs about them would have to motivate independent of desires; but no belief alone motivates; therefore there are no objective values.

Neither strand does the work it is asked to do. The verdict is light, not decisive: each premise has some pull, but Hume's broader anti-rationalism in ethics does not stand up.

This position takes a stand on these two strands only. It must not be conflated with two other Humean theses, each treated by a separate position:

- **Hume's Law / the is-ought gap.** A logical thesis about deductive derivability between descriptive and evaluative statements. That thesis is genuine and independently defensible; both strands here can fail while Hume's Law remains correct.
- **Hume's positive sentimentalism.** The first-order claim that moral judgments arise from sentiment. Distinct from the negative claim that reason cannot do the work, and treated separately.

The arguments:

- **The truth/falsity argument collapses by parallel reasoning.** Hume's premise is that reason judges only truth and falsity, and that actions are not truth-apt. But numbers are not truth-apt either, and reason evaluates them perfectly well — that is how we know seven is prime. What reason judges in the numerical case is not the number itself but propositions about it. The same applies to actions: actions themselves are not true or false, but propositions about them — including the proposition that an action is right or that it is wrong — can be, and reason can evaluate those. The argument equivocates between actions and propositions about actions; once that is disambiguated, it loses its force.

- **The motivation strand oversells what realism would require.** Many realists hold that the belief that *X* is wrong moves you only insofar as you also have some standing concern to do right. On that picture, objective values exist as facts about what is wrong, but they do not necessarily motivate the indifferent. (Compare the comedian who says he thinks meat-eating is terrible but doesn't care, and isn't moved to stop. He isn't contradicting himself; he's an immoral person.) The Humean reads into realism a stronger internalist thesis than the realist need accept.

- **The motivation strand is intuitive for descriptive beliefs and weakens for evaluative ones.** The standard motivating example — the news that the airport train is leaving in one minute does not move you to board it without an antecedent goal of going to the airport — works because the descriptive proposition leaves the evaluative question open. But evaluative propositions just are the kind that report what is good or what should be done. If "getting to the airport is good" is itself the content of a belief, the belief already supplies the goal a desire would have to supply on the descriptive side. Whether such evaluative beliefs exist is the very question at issue, so the Humean cannot help himself to the negative answer without begging it.

- **The Moorean closer.** Suppose both strands retain some pull. Set them against "you shouldn't torture babies for fun". The latter is more obvious than any premise either strand rests on, taken on its own. So whichever direction the inconsistency runs, the rational move is to reject a premise rather than the conclusion. Hume's anti-rationalist case cannot bootstrap its way past a more secure first-order moral judgment.

What survives: Hume's Law is untouched. Hume's positive sentimentalism — the claim that moral discrimination is a matter of sentiment, not reason — is a separable first-order question. Rejecting the anti-rationalist case here leaves both intact as separate matters.

**Usage:** Treat as an addition to the Hume essay. The exam essay's spine is the *Treatise* III text; this position adds the modern reply that Hume's anti-rationalist case in ethics does not stand. Engage Hume's own arguments first; bring this reply in only when the prompt invites assessment of Hume's case against moral reason — the truth/falsity argument, the inertness of reason, or the modern Argument from Motivation. Disambiguate on the way in: a prompt about the is-ought gap belongs to the separate is-ought-gap position; a prompt about sentiment as the positive foundation of morality belongs to the separate sentimentalism position. Lead with parallel-reasoning for the truth/falsity strand, with the train-belief disambiguation for the motivation strand, and use the Moorean closer as the single strongest finishing move.
