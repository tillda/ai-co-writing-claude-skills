# The Is-Ought Gap Is Genuine, but It Cuts Toward Intuitionism

Hume was right that no collection of non-evaluative facts entails a non-trivial evaluative conclusion. The standard anti-realist deployment of this — "therefore there are no objective values" — gets the dialectic backwards. The gap shows that moral knowledge cannot come by deduction from natural facts; it does not show there are no moral facts to know.

- **The modern attempts to refute Hume's Law fail or are concessive.** Searle's promising derivation trades on an ambiguity in "undertaking an obligation" between a convention-fixed weak sense (purporting to obligate oneself) and a strong sense (actually being obligated); only the strong sense reaches the moral conclusion, and reaching it imports an evaluative premise. Geach's argument uses an inference form invalid in general (counter-example: a judge who, denied a brief sentence, would impose life imprisonment — "ought not impose life" plus "if not death, then life" does not yield "ought to impose death"), and premise (1) hides evaluative content. Prior's disjunctive trick technically bridges the gap but yields conclusions that are either trivial or epistemically inaccessible.
- **Karmo's proof secures the epistemic version.** Stipulate: a statement is evaluative-in-w iff its truth-value, holding the non-evaluative facts of w fixed, depends on which value system is correct. No valid argument can have premises non-evaluative in the actual world and a non-trivial conclusion evaluative in the actual world: validity forces premise-truth to track conclusion-truth, the conclusion's truth varies with the value system, so at least one premise must too — making it evaluative after all.
- **The flip.** If moral knowledge cannot come by deduction from natural facts, and (separately) cannot come by analytic reduction, observation, or inference to the best explanation, then a faculty of intellectual seeming directed at evaluative content is what is left. Hume's Law is a lemma in the case for intuitionism, not a verdict against realism.
